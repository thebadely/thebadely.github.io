The Metropolitan Police repeatedly missed opportunities to stop a predatory detective inspector from sexually exploiting women whose cases he investigated, including one who married him and later died in unexplained circumstances, BBC Panorama can reveal.

Warren Arter used his position - running a unit investigating sexual assaults in south-east London - to target women it was his job to protect.

Two women, speaking out for the first time, have told Panorama how Arter groomed them. One says he raped her.

In the case of his wife, Rebekah, he filmed her being sexually abused and distributed the footage. She died in Barbados two years ago, while on holiday with him. On Sunday, the BBC revealed how he lied about what happened on the Caribbean island.

There were multiple investigations into Arter while he was employed by the Met. Now the former detective who led one of them has told the BBC that senior bosses tried to interfere with his inquiries, despite him having found potential victims.

While Arter was suspended as a result of that investigation, he remained on full pay for seven years - before being sacked in 2023 for drug offences.

The following year, the Met - having learned of his wife's death in Barbados - arrested him on his return to the UK for sexual offences alleged by another woman.

Arter took his own life days later in HMP Wandsworth. Despite the evidence against him, the Met has not reviewed all the cases he handled.

Arter subjected his wife, Rebekah, to years of abuse

In response to our investigation, the Metropolitan Police has for the first time publicly apologised to the women he abused. It said it was now working to examine Arter's electronic devices to identify if there are further victims and that it encouraged other women to come forward.

Panorama has uncovered several opportunities to stop Arter that were missed by the Met:

In 2010, medical staff found Arter alone with a woman in the toilets of a sexual assault referral centre hours after she had reported being raped. Despite the Met being told, this did not lead to an investigation

The Met received six corroborated intelligence reports into sexual misconduct by Arter between 2014 and 2018

The force failed to arrest Arter in 2024 after being told he had sent an illegal upskirting image, and only did so after Rebekah's death three months later

This last missed opportunity came after the Casey Review's conclusion that the Met was "institutionally misogynistic", as well as the convictions of rapist and murderer Wayne Couzens, and rapist David Carrick - both Met officers - and Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley's promise to "lift the stone" and "tackle everything underneath it".

Panorama can now reveal that 21 potential victims of Arter were identified in an investigation by the police watchdog, the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC). The Met has not contacted all the women whose cases he investigated during his 20 years as a serving officer.

The upskirting image that was sent in March 2024 to a woman we are calling Layla should have led to Arter's arrest.

Bail conditions or custody would have prevented him from taking his wife, Rebekah, to Barbados.

On 28 June, Arter rang Rebekah's relatives from the island to tell them she had died.

Rebekah had been escaping an abusive relationship in 2006 when the Met put then-Det Sgt Warren Arter in charge of investigating her former partner.

Arter pursued Rebekah, they began a relationship and he quickly moved in with her.

Her now-adult son, Elliot, says Arter initially made his mum feel "safer" but that his behaviour soon changed.

"He'd say to her: 'Get in the kitchen, make my dinner you slag. Make me a tea you [expletive] bitch,'" Elliot recalls.

Arter would openly watch pornography on his tablet while he was in the living room with her, Elliot says.

He also collected urine from Elliot, claiming he was testing it at the police lab to help the boy's promising football career - but Elliot now suspects it was an effort to hide Arter's drug use.

The inquest into Rebekah's death heard evidence that use of sedatives had become so routine in the home she shared with Arter, that a daily smart-speaker reminder announced "G o'clock" - a reference to the date-rape drug GHB.

In the years before she died, Arter had filmed and distributed videos of his wife being sexually abused. The BBC has seen some of them.

Many show her unconscious or otherwise incapacitated while Arter insults her.

"You should get raped," he says, in one video.

In another, Arter can be heard speaking to a man behind the camera, asking him to be quiet and saying "they go mad otherwise" - suggesting the video was being made for a wider audience.

His crimes were so brazen that he sent abuse videos of Rebekah unsolicited to at least four women he knew.

Two were women whose cases he had investigated - one of them was Layla.

She had met Arter in 2010 after she reported being raped. His exploitation of her continued for more than a decade, up until she reported the upskirting image - which she had deleted - to staff from the IOPC.

Both Layla, and her adult daughter who was with her when she spoke to the IOPC, say she identified the woman in the photo as Rebekah - whom she recognised from previous videos.

The IOPC says the staff member has no record or recollection of being told the identity of the woman in the photo - and it did not have the authority to investigate as Arter was no longer a police officer.

It did, however, tell the Metropolitan Police about the upskirting allegation.

Arter's abuse could have been stopped more than a decade earlier - soon after he had met Rebekah.

He claimed their relationship had begun after a chance meeting on a night out, an account she corroborated, when he was questioned by the IOPC during an investigation started in 2017.

However, Panorama has learned Rebekah was bombarded with more than nine hours of calls from Met phone numbers in the three months following the conclusion of her case in 2006. We also know that Arter searched her name on Met computer systems without any obvious justification.

Officers have faced misconduct proceedings, even prison, for relationships which began in similar circumstances, but at that time Arter was not disciplined.

Arter falsely claimed he had met Rebekah by chance on a night out

It is not clear when his bosses found out about the relationship, but some of his colleagues appear to have known early on.

The BBC understands that four officers told the IOPC investigation they believed Arter had met Rebekah on a case. One said they knew Rebekah had been a victim of domestic abuse, but they refused to sign a statement.

The Met told Panorama that the IOPC's subsequent report had found that Arter's colleagues knew of his relationship with Rebekah, but did not know he had met her in the course of his duties, therefore he was not subject to any misconduct proceedings.

None of the various investigations into Arter led to criminal charges before he was arrested in 2024. On one occasion, he even received a payout after being arrested for harassment.

The IOPC has now told the BBC that of 21 possible Arter victims it had identified by 2020, it assessed he had inappropriate contact or relationships with eight of them, including Layla and Rebekah.

Panorama has, however, learned of at least one more alleged victim who says she was groomed and sexually exploited over 12 years.

Former Victims' Commissioner for England and Wales, Dame Vera Baird, is calling for an investigation by an outside force into all the cases Arter handled throughout his career.

"If a doctor had been found to abuse one person, they would look at all his list from start to finish.

"It's quite outrageous - there will be women living now with trauma imposed by him on them."

When Det Insp Jaysen Veeren tried to investigate Arter a decade ago, he says senior Met bosses tried to interfere with his work.

Veeren was a fast-rising officer in the Met's Directorate of Professional Standards (DPS) - which investigates police misconduct - and says he was warned he would be a "fool" to join the team because of its reputation within the force.

"The DPS is referred to as 'the rats' - you eat your own," he says.

But when, in 2016, he intercepted a Crimestoppers tip complaining about Arter's conduct with victims, he recalls telling his boss: "This is the case that I joined for."

Initially, his investigation was well resourced and he travelled to Australia to interview a woman who said Arter had sex with her after she reported being sexually assaulted as a tourist in London.

Veeren then cold-called other cases.

"We had one victim and, all of a sudden, you've got a second, third," he recalls. "It was quite obvious you're dealing with a predator."

Arter had been able to visit many of the women alone. One woman told Veeren: "He may as well have walked into my house with his [penis] out".

Veeren eventually arrested Arter and suspended him in December 2016 but Arter's phone had been wiped.

He then designed a plan to contact all the women whose cases Arter had investigated, but says he was prevented from doing so and his team encountered "interference" from senior officers outside of the DPS.

Emails seen by the BBC reveal his investigation was likened to a "witch hunt" by a detective chief inspector, who questioned its justification with the Met "haemorrhaging talent". A chief superintendent told Veeren's boss that staff felt the case was a "fishing expedition".

The two senior officers - who have since left the force - told the BBC this was not interference and said they were acting on concerns of colleagues about how the investigation was being carried out.

Veeren says he felt unable to widen his investigation and examine all of Arter's cases.

He referred his case into Arter to the IOPC in the expectation it would do so, and later reported his concerns to the watchdog as part of a wider whistleblowing complaint - alleging corruption within the DPS.

He resigned from the force in 2019 when he discovered none of his allegations would be upheld. The Met says his complaints to the IOPC had either been disproven or not corroborated.

Layla says Arter's abuse has "ruined her life" and she still struggles to leave the house.

Fourteen years before she told the IOPC about Arter's upskirting image, Layla reported being raped in a hotel room by a member of staff.

Arter was put in charge of her case, as the rape unit lead. He immediately began to target her.

Arter sexually exploited Layla during what he described as "therapy time", she says

Within hours of their first meeting, Arter asked Layla to put her head in his lap and then he stroked her hair, while a PC drove them to a sexual assault referral centre.

There, Arter was found by staff alone in the toilets with her.

A doctor was so alarmed by his behaviour that she reported concerns to the Met about his intimacy with Layla.

Layla has told us that Arter remained present while she had an internal medical examination.

The Met failed to investigate or monitor the detective's ongoing contact with Layla, who at the time was a vulnerable alcoholic.

Arter visited Layla dozens of times over the next few months, she tells us, sexually exploiting her during what he described as "therapy time".

Panorama has learned that Arter told colleagues that Layla was becoming too attached to him.

He was commended for victim care in her case - just as he had been in the domestic abuse case of his future wife, Rebekah.

Even after Arter informed Layla that the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) had declined to authorise charges in July 2010, he continued to contact her - helping her with a successful compensation claim under a government-funded scheme.

After Veeren referred his investigation to the IOPC, the watchdog described Layla as having been "groomed" by Arter.

Layla says Arter, who by this time had been suspended, was telling her what to tell investigators. She did not provide them with a statement.

Despite Layla's known vulnerability, and the evidence against Arter, it took three years for a file of evidence about his sexual misconduct to be sent to the CPS, which then declined to pursue criminal charges in 2020.

It was a further three years before Arter appeared at a misconduct hearing - and only then for drug offences.

He was finally dismissed from the Met in 2023 - have received at least Â£300,000 in pay while suspended. He kept his pension.

However, despite having left the force, a decision to review Arter's alleged sexual misconduct was then made.

In March 2024, IOPC staff visited Layla at home. She revealed Arter had sent her the upskirting image. By this point, she says she had begun to "wake up" to Arter's exploitation of her.

The watchdog told the Met about the illegal image - but the force failed to arrest him.

The Met told Panorama this was because of human error when using a new crime recording system.

On 28 June 2024, Rebekah died in a hotel room in Barbados while on holiday with Arter.

Disturbed by the lies Arter was telling them - his account of what had happened kept changing - her son Elliot and daughter-in-law Lacey went to the couple's house in south-east London.

They say they found a variety of drugs and hidden CCTV cameras that Arter had rigged up.

They also discovered photographs of Rebekah naked and bruised, involved in sexual acts, seemingly taken without her consent.

On 2 July, Elliot and Lacey drove to Bexleyheath Police Station - the same station where Arter had worked.

Staff were initially uninterested in Rebekah's death thousands of miles away, say the couple - and it was only when they mentioned Arter's name that officers became interested. "It was like chaos," Lacey says.

The family say they were told the Met was considering sending officers out to Barbados to help the investigation.

However, the BBC has been told that a senior Met boss from its Gold Command group was briefed that evening about the incident, in light of Arter's alleged offending, and a decision was taken not to send officers to the Caribbean island.

The Met told the BBC it took this decision because no allegation of criminality had been made.

Later that week, the Met visited Layla and interviewed her about Arter, where she disclosed he had raped her on one occasion and sexually assaulted her on another.

Arter flew back to London from Barbados on 9 July and was arrested at Heathrow Airport, accused of rape, voyeurism and coercive control. He took his own life in prison three days later.

Neither the Met nor the IOPC has ever released their investigation reports into Arter's sexual misconduct - despite Freedom of Information requests by the BBC.

Arter was seen as the star detective in a unit lauded for its results investigating rape and sexual assault.

But the Met has never investigated that unit's "sanction detection rates" - the proportion of reported crimes for which action was taken against suspects.

In 2009, Conservative politician James Cleverly, then a Member of the London Assembly, said the unit had achieved rates of 96%.

This is despite the figure being three times the London average and at a time when some officers were known to be systemically manipulating the recording of offences. The Met says it does not recognise the 96% figure.

Dame Vera says there seems to be many missed opportunities to stop Arter, spanning from 2006 to 2024: "There must have been other people who knew exactly what was going on."

Rebekah's family say the Met should investigate whether other men helped Arter abuse her.

When she died the Met seized electronic devices from the house she had shared with Arter. Elliot says some of those devices have not been analysed in two years.

His solicitor says a public inquiry is needed to reveal the full extent of Arter's offending.

"It's a disgrace for the Metropolitan Police, and it's also a dirty secret - one they want to keep hidden," says Sophie Naftalin.

Rebekah died in Barbados in 2024 while on holiday with Arter

The Met told Panorama that Arter could have been arrested sooner and it was "deeply sorry" for the harm caused to victims by his repeated exploitation of his position for sexual purposes.

It acknowledged progress with Arter's devices had been slow but said it was now being prioritised, and it encouraged anyone else who believes they were targeted to come forward.

It added that the recording of rape cases was examined nationally in 2014 - and there have been significant changes to the scrutiny of crime figures.

The IOPC says it regretted that its investigation took three years but it was a complex case involving delays out of its control. It added that the allegations were treated "extremely seriously" and resulted in referrals to the CPS.

The CPS says it thoroughly reviewed this evidence but concluded there was no realistic prospect of conviction at that time.

It says it did charge Arter with misconduct in public office before his death in 2024.

In September 2025, the UK coroner in Rebekah's case issued what is known as a prevention of future deaths report.

It said there was a risk that further deaths could occur unless action was taken.

In response, the Met said it had already taken action to improve vetting and to tackle police officers abusing their powers.

The IOPC said information had not been made available to it at the time of a specific risk to Rebekah, and that it had since strengthened its safeguarding procedures.

Elliot says he has spent the past two years trying to get answers.

"I don't think I've grieved yet", he says.

"I don't know what will happen when that day will come."

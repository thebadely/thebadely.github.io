Zaher had lost both his legs in a drone strike. With little access to specialist treatment, he dreamed of the day he might walk and play football again.

More than a year later, as we walk into an apartment in the Egyptian capital, Zaher greets us with an enormous hug. His mother, Habibah, embraces us too.

Zaher is one of millions of children whose lives have been devastated by Sudan's civil war

Zaher lost his legs in 2024, when he and his mother had been trapped in Khartoum. Much of the city had become a battleground as it was torn apart by fighting between Sudan's army and the RSF.

Food and basic supplies were running short and the family did not have enough money to flee. To survive, they sold lentils on the streets.

Then one morning, as Zaher and his mother worked side by side, a drone struck.

"I looked at him and he was bleeding. There was blood everywhere," Habibah explained last year.

She was devastated. "He would wake up and ask: 'Why did you let them cut off my legs?' I couldn't answer."

Back then, Zaher was wheeling himself through the rubble of his neighbourhood in a wheelchair, past burnt-out cars, abandoned tanks and shattered homes.

Before the war, Zaher had loved football. After the drone strike, he could only play on his knees, a painful process that put him at risk of serious infection.

"I wish I could have prosthetic legs so I can play football with my friends like I used to," he said at the time.

There seemed little prospect of that happening, but his story resonated far beyond Sudan, prompting an outpouring of support from people around the world.

Among them was Dena Latif, a UK-based lawyer of Sudanese origin. After seeing the BBC report, she helped spearhead an online campaign raising Â£45,000 ($60,000) for his medical care.

Unable to get the treatment he needed in Sudan, Zaher had to leave the country, which was far from straightforward. He and his mother had no passports and their birth certificates had been destroyed during the war.

Zaher has learned to use his new legs with months of support from doctors and physiotherapists in Cairo

After months of planning, he and his mother were able to fly to Cairo where an apartment had been prepared for them, with food in the fridge and medical appointments arranged.

For the first time in years, their immediate concern was no longer simply how to survive.

Zaher underwent further surgery, followed by months of physiotherapy and training to learn to use his prosthetic legs. He also received psychological support to help him process the trauma he had been through.

"He was shy, a little bit depressed, sad," says Dr Bintelmuna Eltayeb , one of the doctors involved in his care. "To get a smile out of him was almost impossible, but now you can see he's laughing. He has great confidence in himself."

His transformation has been as much psychological as physical, say those close to him, and Dena says Zaher now approaches life with a determination that has surprised them.

As we spend time with Zaher, he laughs and jokes, making up for some of the childhood that war has taken from him.

He is eager to try new things, from horse riding to cycling, and wants to become an airline pilot. He hasn't had a game of football yet but is looking forward to playing with his friends back home.

For Habibah, watching her son regain his confidence has brought enormous relief. But she worries about his future, his education and how he will be received in Sudan, a country still locked in civil war.

Khartoum has suffered extensive damage, with many buildings damaged and their contents stolen

The state of emergency has been lifted in their home city, Khartoum, with the Sudanese army back in control there. But throughout the capital, buildings have been left destroyed - burned, blackened and looted.

Concerns over security remain and 41% of Sudan's population are experiencing high levels of food insecurity, according to a recent statement released by the US and 30 other Western states. The group also warned of "potential famine".

"I hope that Zaher's story doesn't end with him," says Dena. She hopes his recovery will attract more attention and funds for the thousands of other Sudanese children who have suffered life-altering injuries.

Zaher and his mother are now back in Khartoum. They hope that as the city is more stable at the moment, he will be able to get his prosthetic legs checked and adjusted there as he grows.

Despite the safety they found in Cairo, they missed their home, family and friends. And while Zaher has been given a chance to reclaim his childhood, millions of other Sudanese children are still waiting for theirs.

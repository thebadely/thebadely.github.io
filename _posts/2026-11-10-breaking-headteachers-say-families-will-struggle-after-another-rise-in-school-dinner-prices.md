A headteacher has said she believes "families are going to struggle" as the price of school dinners is to increase for the second year in a row.

The price of meals for pre-school, primary and special school pupils will go up by 30p, for post-primary pupils will they will rise by 15%. It follows an increase of just under 20% introduced by the Education Authority (EA) in January 2026.

There will also be a change to some menus in schools, replacing baked desserts with cheaper alternatives.

At Malone Integrated College about 55% of pupils have free school meal entitlement and there are also a lot of parents who are on lower incomes.

When the prices of school meals increased earlier this year, Leslie said she remembers "sitting with the head of the cafeteria and heartbreakingly decide how much to put up each item".

"I think the cumulative effect of this will be immense, particularly families who have multiple children, both at a primary school and a post-primary school, and trying to make ends meet, it's going to become incredibly difficult," she said.

"I firmly believe families are going to struggle at Malone.

"I do believe that there will be some families and some young people who will be worried about the stigma attached to this, and won't voice their concerns and won't ask for help. And as an integrated school, that's really sad for us."

She added that she thought it would be "quite cutthroat for families".

"Already, we have some of our children, particularly in junior school talking about Christmas and how they've had discussions with their families that things are going to be much more scaled down... just with the general cost of living crisis. 

"This is only going to amplify the situation even more for young people."

Leslie added that not having a healthy, balanced diet or going without food will be a "barrier to learning".

Education Minister Paul Givan said he was disappointed and concerned but added it was due to underfunding of the education sector as a whole. 

"I don't want this to be introduced but it is a result of the Education Authority not having sufficient funding," he told the BBC's Good Morning Ulster programme. 

He said education often bears "the greatest burden" during cost-saving exercises and argued his department should receive a greater share of Stormont's budget. 

Givan also said the "inadequate" funding that Stormont receives from Westminster was "bearing down on a huge number of public services in Northern Ireland".

Stormont ministers are yet to agree a budget, despite weeks of talks with Secretary of State Sir Chris Byrant, who has repeatedly said there is "no more money".

Northern Ireland's commissioner for children and young people said he was ''flabbergasted" at the change.

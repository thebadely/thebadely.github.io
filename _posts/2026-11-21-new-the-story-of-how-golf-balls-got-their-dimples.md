Here’s what you need to know: Golf balls and their renowned, dimpled, design

In the late 19th Century, golf balls were totally smooth, but players soon discovered that they did not fly as well as hoped.

Players found that once balls had picked up a few dents and chips, they flew straighter and further, with some hammering grooves into them.

However, this all changed when William Taylor - an engineer who founded a company in Leicester - took up the sport and designed the dimples we know and love today.

"Golf was supposed to be the relaxing hobby, but then of course he turned it into an engineering problem. I suppose it's just that kind of mind that he couldn't resist," Sophie Edwards - of More Than Just a Gift at Narborough Hall, where Taylor designed his dimples, said.

Golf balls have gone a long way - both in distance but also design 

Gavin Bottrell, who makes replicas of old and rare golf balls and owns Timewarp Golf in Warwickshire, said for several hundred years, golf balls were made of leather sewn together and stuffed with feathers - which were called featheries.

Then, from the mid-19th Century, Bottrell said golf balls began to be made from gutta-percha - a natural tree sap - but golfers found they did not fly very well.

"They knew empirically that they needed to mark the surface of balls, but they didn't know why," Bottrell said.

Taylor founded lens making company Taylor Hobson with his brother in 1886, with the firm producing large numbers of camera lenses of such "high quality", The Royal Society said, they were desired all over the world.

In a bid to temporarily escape from the demands of his career, Taylor started playing golf.

According to the firm he founded, it became common practice at the time to produce balls with "irregular" patterns. 

However, Sophie Edwards said the results "weren't consistent".

"He [Taylor] was a very skilled engineer and he'd taken golf as a hobby to relax, but obviously decided also that he would seize on this problem," she said.

William Taylor founded lens making company Taylor Hobson with his brother in Leicester in 1886

His firm said Taylor designed a golf ball driving machine, which comprised a club, swinging around an inclined axis under the influence of a weight. 

It added he used a large number of golf balls, each with a different dimple configuration.

His driving machine measured the distance of flight of each ball as it flew from the device, the company said.

Edwards told the BBC that Taylor also set up a smoke chamber in one of the barns at the Grade II listed Narborough Hall, so he could test the way air was moving across the golf ball. 

According to Taylor Hobson, this comprised a glass-fronted test chamber in which smoke was blown over differently patterned ball surfaces.

"It was really very high tech at the time and that's what he did at Narborough Hall," Edwards said.

Taylor settled on a dimpled surface design for a golf ball, and patented it alongside his mechanical driving machine for testing the balls, according to The Royal Society.

"He was the guy who really developed it," Gavin Bottrell said. "I mean, like a lot of things, there were other people probably working on it at the same time, because even then golf was a big business to be in. 

"The boom time for golf was actually between 1890 and 1900. That's when golf really exploded as a past-time. 

"And so it was a huge market potential for anybody who could develop a better golf ball."

The United States Patent Office received Taylor's application in 1906, and granted it in 1908.

"Be it known that I, William Taylor, a subject of the King of Great Britain, residing at 57 Sparkenhoe street, Leicester, in the county of Leicester, England, engineer, have invented certain new and useful improvements in golf-balls," an extract of the document reads.

It adds the main objective of the invention is to "obtain better results in the flight of the ball in the direction of a sustained hanging flight giving a flat trajectory with a slight rising tendency".

According to golf equipment firm Titleist, dimples reduce the drag force on the ball.

Bottrell added the design Taylor came up with consisted of quite "big dimples" compared to modern golf balls, with balls starting to look like modern ones from about the 1930s onwards. 

In 1898, Coburn Haskell developed the Haskell ball, which consisted of rubber thread, wound around a solid core - an innovation that Bottrell said was a "massive revolution" at the time.

"Then only a few years later, you've got William coming along and then developing a better surface cover. Those things all sort of happened around about the same time," Bottrell added.

He said the longest drivers of golf balls in the late 19th Century were hitting balls about 200 yards (182.88m), but with the dimple pattern and the new ball top, professionals were driving balls up to about 270 yards (246.89m) in 1910.

"There were so many other patterns that were tried, but the dimples that Taylor developed were the ones that actually stood the test of time - and became the standard that we wouldn't imagine any golf balls in any other way now," Sophie Edwards added.

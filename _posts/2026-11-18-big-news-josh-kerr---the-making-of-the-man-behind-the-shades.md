Experts revealed that Josh Kerr: The making of the man behind the shades

You know Josh Kerr, right? 

The guy with the shades. The fastest man to ever run a mile. Scottish, but with a curious transatlantic accent. Slicked-back hair. Beard. Usually got a medal of some kind slung around his neck. No stranger to telling you what he thinks.

That's Kerr the competitor, one of the foremost faces in world athletics, and a man who rampaged around London's Olympic Stadium in three minutes 42.66 seconds in July to set a new world record - then followed it up with a home Commonwealth gold and an imperious triumph at the Ultimate Championship.

Kerr recognises the description, smiling as it's articulated. But is there another version of him? Speaking to him during one of his regular returns to Scotland from his New Mexico base, it becomes clear there is.

There's scant chat about records, medals and his enjoyably niggly rivalry with Jakob Ingebrigtsen. 

Instead, he talks about disguising alcohol-free cider, entering races under a false name, cold-calling universities, being starstruck meeting Scotland's rugby players and his granny keeping him humble.

This is the Kerr who spent the thick end of an hour on a chilly August evening taking selfies, signing his name and sharing his wisdom with young fans at Scotstoun after his Commonwealth medal presentation. 

The Kerr who is still a regular visitor to first club Edinburgh AC's training sessions. This is Uncle Josh, who is heading for a party with his nephews after this chat.

The difference between the two versions of Kerr? The shades.

"They really help me switch my personality into work mode," he tells BBC Sport. "I think everyone has a work persona and it's a bit more intensified when it comes to my sport. 

"Not a lot of things matter when I'm focused on my job, but the rest of the 23 hours and 56-and-a-bit minutes of the day, it's very different."

Kerr is sitting in the bay window of his parents' dining room as the autumnal gloom gathers in this desirable part of Edinburgh. All grand facades, imposing trees and permit parking. 

His granny lives at the end of the road. His brother Jake and his family are 20 minutes outside the city. His school â the prestigious George Watson's â is a mile away.

The house has been home since Josh was five months old. Even now, this is where he returns on his five or six trips back to Scotland each year from his Albuquerque base, albeit the "chaos" of his teenage bedroom has long since been vanquished.

"It brings all different sorts of emotions," Kerr says of walking back through the terraced property's teal front door at the start of each visit. "As a kid, I would train from here every day. Everything started from that door. 

"It's nice to fall back into that routine when I'm here. I can see the splits I'm running in my loops versus when I was 15. Slightly quicker..."

Around him in the room, pictures of the two brothers fight for space with memorabilia from their careers and assorted artwork. Jake - like their father John - was a professional rugby player, earning a cap for Scotland in the 2019 Six Nations. 

He is 18 months older so served as not just an example to follow, but also a target to catch when Josh started racing at the age of nine, having caught the eye at a summer camp.

"If anyone ever wants to nerd out on the family stats, Jake has a pretty awesome under-15 cross-country record," Kerr says. 

"We raced a couple of times, once at the Scottish cross-country relays when I ran under someone else's name because they didn't turn up, which was very hush-hush. I just followed him around, holding on for dear life.

"He still likes to remind me that I've never had an unbeaten season and he has. He still thinks he can beat me. That's the solid Kerr delusion in him. But it got me this far, so I can't question it."

The 29-year-old hasn't always had that familial brashness, though. 

Dad John says "you wouldn't believe how quiet he was" as a child, speculating that Jake did his talking for him. And mum Jill, who has a background in tennis and is now her son's lead physio, recalls his "kindness and emotional intelligence".

Like all good parents, they are also a source of more esoteric anecdotes. Jill remembers providing both boys breakfasts in bed on competition days and teasing Josh for his "showboating", while John reveals that the teenager turned up to parties with non-alcoholic booze or arrived home sweating if he did have a beer, having run all the way back.

"Those are pretty niche stories," Kerr says, perhaps making a mental note to clamp his father later. "Everyone was starting to get into beers and things like that but it was just not really my thing. I found you could just rip the non-alcoholic bit off the label and get on with the socialising."

By that stage, any ambitions of following his dad and brother into rugby - as a full-back in his case - had been shelved, and his attention focused on athletics. 

Kerr "wasn't a phenom by any means" and recalls winning races only "now and again", but the quiet kid had developed a resolve that running would be his future.

"Someone's got to be the best in the world, so why not me? That's what I had in my head," he explains. "I wanted to be a pro athlete and I knew running was going to be my ticket to that. The next stage for me was to go to university and get that paid for."

The problem was his athletic record, while good, wasn't stellar. And neither were his academic grades. Kerr needed to hustle. 

He enlisted the help of his parents and fellow Edinburgh AC luminary Chris O'Hare to compile a list of US colleges and compose a CV and cover letter, then set about selling himself.

"I was 16 and just whipping emails out left, right and centre, saying: 'Hey, I'm coming over in 2015, do you have a spot for me?' And it was a pretty big no across the board," says Kerr, who re-read all the rejection emails recently. 

"Then New Mexico said 'we'll take you' and I was like, 'perfect, I'll be there'. I told my parents and their first question was 'are they going to pay your fees?' and I was like 'uhm, well, I didn't ask'. So they made me phone back and check!"

He did and they were, which set in motion the move that would change Kerr's life in more ways than one. 

It was on his first week in Albuquerque, while on a campus bus that his parents blagged their way on to posing as professors, that Kerr met Larimar Rodriguez, who became his wife last year.

"It was a big week," he says. "It's one of those ones where you don't really realise until you look back. 

"I met Larimar when I was 17 and I'm incredibly proud of her. She's done four years of undergrad, four years of med school, and now she's in her fourth year of residency. She'll be a full attending dermatologist by June or July."

Josh and Larimar live together in New Mexico with their mini schnauzer El Presidente, who often joins Kerr on his lighter runs. 

That's part of a precisely-honed routine that starts with alternate ice baths and hot tub dips at 5am, and some visualisation and journaling around breakfast before he "goes to work" at the track or in the gym from 10am-2pm. 

The next three hours are spent in his bedroom, which has been adapted to serve as an altitude chamber, before he retires for the day before 9pm.

The attention to detail is startling. Childhood friend and fellow former world 1500m champion Jake Wightman recounts sharing a room with bronze medallist Kerr at the Tokyo Olympics and looking on in bemusement as he unpacked a scented diffuser, mattress topper and waffle maker to replicate being at home. 

Five years on, Kerr has a team of 16 in his Brooks Beast running family under head coach Danny Mackey, including personal chef Jameel, a mental performance consultant and a chiropractor.

It's a long way from life behind that teal door in Edinburgh, but how different is Kerr now from the one who left his homeland 11 years ago? "Pretty similar," he says, while acknowledging that he is now more outwardly confident of his abilities.

'Olympic gold is last little piece of my puzzle'

That "abrasive, calling-your-shot side" as he calls it, is not a traditional trait for a Scot but has been shaped by more than a decade in the United States and by his desire to shine a light on athletics. 

"Everyone says, 'oh, we're not getting attention' but go out and earn it," Kerr says. "Make people want to care about what you're doing.

"I'm an individual business and I'm trying as hard as I can to bring eyeballs because I think athletics is the purest form of sport. It didn't really come naturally to me before - being in the US has kind of shaped me a little bit - but I hope people realise that the reason I'm in this position is the blood that runs through my veins.

"I'm able to represent the UK a lot more than I'm able to represent Scotland, but it's such a huge part of who I am. And I hope they take great pride in the performances I'm able to produce. 

"I've been inspired by some incredible athletes and I guess this is my turn to step up and try and bridge the gap between grassroots and where I'm at."

Kerr has done that on the track, with two Olympic medals to add to his world indoor and outdoor golds, but he is approaching a stage where his celebrity might breach the confines of athletics.

Take last week. On a four-day visit home, his duties as Uncle Josh were surrounded by a glut of sponsorship and media engagements - including an appearance on the BBC's flagship current affairs programme Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg - as well as a coaching session with young athletes and an invitation to carry out the match ball at Murrayfield before Scotland faced New Zealand.

Of course, the omnipresent spectre of BBC Sports Personality of the Year is looming into view, too. Kerr, it should be noted, is not shy in coveting that prize.

That he will likely be one of the main contenders speaks to how life has changed for him in the past couple of years. 

Kerr can still enjoy being back in Edinburgh in relative peace - "I got recognised at the end of the street recentlyâ¦ it was my granny" - but concedes life in America can be a bit more challenging because of requests for "400 selfies and a video for their nan".

That attention will only intensify over the coming months should he complete the one or two outstanding items on his athletics tick list and frank his ambition of being "remembered as the best middle-distance athlete of all time".

While he is waiting to see how his winter training goes before 'calling his shot' again, Kerr has other world records in his sights and, of course, Los Angeles in 2028 and a chance to complete the full set of Olympic medals by winning gold. 

Silver was not what he wanted in Paris, albeit the passing of time has allowed some satisfaction with the British record he ran, so righting that wrong in the States is top of his agenda.

Kerr has already made a low-key visit to the Colosseum, the epic venue two hours from his New Mexico home where the athletics events will be staged, to help visualise the moment - just as he did with the Stade de France. 

He will be back again before the shades go on and he gets down to the business of proving himself to be the best ever.

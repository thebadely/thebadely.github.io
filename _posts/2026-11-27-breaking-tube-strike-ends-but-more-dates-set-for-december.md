The first in a series of 24-hour strikes on the London Underground has ended, with more industrial action planned for December, January and February.

Tube drivers from the Rail, Maritime and Transport (RMT) union walked out at noon on Thursday, with workers ending their action at noon on Friday. 

The strike action was the result of a dispute between the union and Transport for London (TfL) over the changing terms and conditions attached to the option of a four or five day week.

On Friday, six Underground lines were suspended or part-suspended, and five others experienced delays. Disruption was forecast to last until 18:00 GMT "as services start to recover".

Simon Mitchell, chief executive at hospitality organisation Kerb, said the Tube strike could not have come "at a worse time" for businesses.

Kerb operates Seven Dials Market in Covent Garden in London's West End.

Mitchell told BBC London there was a "big drop" in footfall on Thursday and he anticipated the same on Friday.

It came at a "really hard time for all hospitality businesses", he said, adding that although the strike was for 24 hours, it had affected two trading days.

He said the upcoming strikes in December were "planned for the best week of trading in the whole of Christmas and what would be our best week of the whole year".

He urged the RMT to call off the strike, adding "we're on our knees".

The strike action was instigated by one of the two unions that represents Tube drivers

TfL said the number of Tube drivers who booked on for their shift during the strike was higher than during previous industrial action, and more than 55% of the Tube network ran on Thursday.

Although there was disruption across the network as anticipated on Friday morning, the Jubilee and Victoria lines were able to start at the normal time and all lines were operating services before 06:30 GMT, earlier than expected, TfL said.

Despite a limited Piccadilly service and no service on the Waterloo & City line lowering the average, the network was said to be operating just over 40% of services on Friday morning up to 09:00.

On Thursday, usage across the transport network was at 90% of normal levels, with Tube ridership at around two thirds of normal levels.

Forest, which operates e-bikes in London, said it experienced a 17% increase in rides on Thursday evening compared to the same time last week.

Uber said it also saw an increase in demand as a result of the strike action. 

Some services were delayed and others were suspended during the strike

Two trade unions represent London Underground train drivers: Aslef and the RMT.

During the strike on Thursday and Friday, only RMT members walked out, as Aslef has agreed to TfL's voluntary compressed four-day week and previously said it was "surprised that the RMT has decided to take this action". 

Aslef primarily represents Tube train drivers, while the RMT represents drivers as well as general operational, engineering, maintenance and station workers.

The dispute follows the introduction by TfL of a new framework for drivers, who have been given the option of a four or five-day week.

The RMT said it supported the framework but did not agree with changing workers' existing terms and conditions.

The union also expressed concern that staying on the old deal would lead to working practice changes including moving depots.

General secretary Eddie Dempsey has said: "RMT is not opposed to a genuine four-day working week that benefits our members, but we will not accept it being used as a vehicle for job cuts, intensified working and the erosion of terms and conditions."

TfL said changes to working patterns were voluntary. A spokesperson said: "Any suggestion otherwise is misleading the public in order to justify strike action over proposals that would give Tube drivers more time off and create a more modern, efficient Tube service."

Trader Ollie said the frequency of Tube strikes was "excessive"

On Friday morning, some commuters faced being late for work and missed appointments.

At King's Cross, the reduced service TfL ran through the station coped with demand and no platforms were packed with passengers waiting for delayed Tube trains.

Ollie, a stall trader outside King's Cross, said the walkout had left trade "really slow" as fewer people travelled in and spent money at stalls.

With Christmas approaching, he said it was a vital time for traders to earn money as more visitors came to London's Christmas markets.

He said: "I think holding Tube strikes like so often is a bit excessive and I don't think there's really any need for it."

This was echoed by Amelia, who works at The Flower Station bakery stall outside King's Cross station.

"I think it affects a lot more people than they realise," she said of the strike. "I appreciate that they obviously have their reasons for striking, but it impacts a lot more people than they think - not just people getting to and from work, but businesses like us."

Buses were busier than usual on Friday morning

In Finsbury Park, people could be seen running for buses and trying to board the few Tube services that were running.

One young woman waiting for the bus said that she was concerned she would miss her A-level mock exam.

"I'm just stressed out, I need to get to school immediately. I need to get the bus but they aren't working, so I'm just standing here," Aya told BBC London.

"I'm going to be late so I'll have to explain it to the teachers, but hopefully they'll understand."

Marie, who was also at Finsbury Park, said she had been left "waiting for buses that don't show up".

She was trying to get home with her guinea pigs after collecting them from a sitter after returning from a trip to Morocco.

She said she used a Lime bike to travel to the sitter, but wanted to use a bus to travel home with her pets.

The strike "makes life really complicated and stressful", she said.

Asked for her reaction to the seven remaining strikes planned over the coming months, Marie said it "just complicates our lives".

It went beyond getting into the office for work, she said, adding that it was "almost impossible" for her to travel across London to see friends when strikes were on.

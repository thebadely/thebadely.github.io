Experts revealed that In recent months, Paloma Buddemeier has watched a stream of people walk into her nail salon with the goal of looking as if they'd never been there at all.

"It's crazy," the owner of Wumman Spa in London says.

These customers still get their nails filed, buffed, and trimmed. "You still do the cuticle, you do the soaking and hydrate everything," says Buddemeier, "but you just put no polish on."

Driving this, she says, is a viral craze for bare-looking nails.

In recent months, artsy shots of glossy naked nails have been flooding social media and taking over fashion runways and red carpets, which some are viewing as the latest symbol of "quiet luxury".

But critics argue the bare nails trend actually says something about class, the hamster wheel of beauty standards, and who gets to define what is considered tasteful.

Paloma Buddemeier has seen a shift in what customers are looking for at her London nail salon

Nail art's historical and creative roots are linked back to black, Latino, and Asian communities and the suggestion that bare nails are now a sign of high status could stigmatise bold manicures, they say.

"That trend really promotes blankness and this kind of sameness and erasure of culture and identity that is so important to black and brown communities," beauty journalist Kristina Rodulfo tells the BBC.

"They are often called elegant or tasteful, and these are often usually euphemisms that perpetuate white supremacist standards of beauty."

For some, though, the choice is less about a dislike of bold nails and more about simplifying their routine.

Some converts say they're ditching polish as a way of rebelling against an entrenched female beauty standard or to save time and money. Others just love the look. Then, there are some women who've long left their nails alone, amused to find their routine â or lack thereof â suddenly in vogue.

For years, Irish TV presenter Angela Scanlon marched herself to a nail salon every fortnight, a routine that started when she was living in New York in the 2000s. There, she says, manicures were so universal they felt like a uniform. But it soon became a chore.

Scanlon would have everything from thin, shiny shellac to sturdier "Builder In A Bottle" gel manicures layered onto her nails every other week without fail.

"It wasn't like I was in my head going: 'I must go because Dave from Hull is going to be disgusted if I'm on This Morning next week without a full set'," the Radio 2 presenter says. "I just added it to the list of things that we must do... in the way you would wash your hair."

Eventually, in a moment of clarity, Scanlon decided to stop going, in a bid to free up time and mental bandwidth.

"I heard myself saying to people: 'Ugh, my god, I have to go and get my nails done.'

"And I thought: 'I probably don't need to do this anymore'."

Scanlon's posts about her decision to ditch the continuous manicure blew up on Substack and Instagram earlier this year. She seemed to have tapped into a vein.

But there are those who think the trend - or the way it's being discussed by some - belies something more sinister.

Manicures have been whispering messages about the wearer's position in society for as long as they have existed, according to fashion journalist Suzanne E. Shapiro.

"Women's bodies - and sense of self-presentation - have always held huge political and cultural significance," Shapiro, who wrote Nails: The Story of The Modern Manicure, tells the BBC.

Even back in 13th century Imperial China, the lengthy talons grown by nobility and the delicately carved brass nail shields which usually guarded them were a symbol of a life unmarred by manual labour.

A single layer of the rose-tinted waxy salves which were popular in the 1800s was a sign of vigour and health, but two layers - in an era when cosmetics and artificial beauty were tied to virtue - was the mark of a vain or "loose" woman.

And the red at-home nail lacquer, released by Revlon in the early 1930s, was a badge of honour for modern women rebelling against that kind of oppressive judgement.

But it was women of colour who really made manicures an accessible and fashionable form of self-expression, historians say.

Entertainer Josephine Baker, disco icons Diana Ross and Donna Summer, and Olympic gold-medal-winning sprinter Florence Griffith-Joyner - whose dramatic acrylic sets often distracted headline writers from her record-setting performances - have all pioneered nail art.

And when waves of Vietnamese refugees gave rise to the nail bar in the 1970s, professional manicures were no longer the preserve of the rich.

"The same way that logo bags are considered gauche or quiet luxury is more aspirational, bare nails are considered the trend now.

"The examples are almost always white women or celebrities... [and] they'll often conflate class and taste with that bareness."

Others point out bare nails often demand their own time-consuming and costly routine.

"The irony in all this is these natural nails are not natural either," Shapiro says. "They also require a lot of grooming and sometimes pretty premiere services."

Few would argue there aren't valid feminist questions for the beauty industry to answer, around unrealistic standards for example, but the largely female-run nail sector also feels empowering for many women.

"I feel like nail care has very much had a 'for women, by women' sort of ethos around it," Shapiro says. "The first salon opened in New York City was owned by a woman, and she was a remarkably independent one... a lot of the first entrepreneurs offering product lines, opening salons were women."

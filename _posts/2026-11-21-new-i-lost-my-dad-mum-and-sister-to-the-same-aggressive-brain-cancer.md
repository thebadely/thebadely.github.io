Let's dive into the details: Iola Williams wants to see improvements in the way brain tumours are diagnosed and treated 

After firstly losing her dad to an aggressive brain tumour, Iola Williams then had to watch as her mother and sister both died of similar diseases.

It was in 2004 that Billy died from glioblastoma, external - the most common and fastest growing type of brain cancer - with mum Eleanor dying a decade later, followed by Helen in 2024.

Charity Brain Tumour Research said about 3,200 people are diagnosed with glioblastoma in the UK each year, with only a third surviving past a year.

Iola said she has seen little change in treatment options since her dad was diagnosed 20 years ago and wants changes.

The Welsh government said it was developing a National Cancer Strategy for Wales, external to try and improve things.

"My dad was diagnosed in 2004, my mum in 2014 and Helen in 2024," said Iola, 68, from Llanfrothen, Gwynedd.

"Nothing has really changed.

"They get the diagnosis, they get radiotherapy or chemotherapy, but they still can't tell us where these tumours come from.

"If nothing happens then other people are going to have to go through what we have as a family."

Billy, Helen, Eleanor all died from a similar brain tumour 

Brain Tumour Research described her family's story as "highly unusual".

Opening up on her family's experience, Iola said the effects of the disease has remained with the family long after their deaths and was calling for increased funding and improved access to clinical trials.

In 2004, Billy died within weeks of being diagnosed with glioblastoma.

Iola, a retired psychiatric nurse, described her father's initial diagnosis after he collapsed while gardening for a friend in the village.

Family members initially feared he had suffered a stroke, but hospital scans revealed multiple tumours on his brain.

Despite undergoing radiotherapy, his condition deteriorated quickly.

"He loved his job," said Iola.

"He worked as a groundsman at Morfa Bychan Golf Club [near Porthmadog] and adored his grandchildren.

"We managed to look after him at home, but he passed away very rapidly."

Billy and Eleanor died a decade apart from a similar condition

Exactly a decade later, the family faced another cancer battle when Iola's mother, Eleanor, developed a brain tumour.

She had already been treated for breast cancer and lung cancer and, according to Iola, was "a fighter", dying from complications related to her cancer treatment.

Then in January 2024, Iola's younger sister, Helen, was told she had the same aggressive form of brain cancer as her father.

The diagnosis was confirmed on Helen's 56th birthday.

Deciding against chemotherapy and radiotherapy treatment, she chose instead to focus on quality of life in the time she had left.

"All she wanted to do was go home," said Iola.

She died 12 weeks after her diagnosis.

Having watched three close relatives deteriorate quickly, Iola says it was only afterwards that the full emotional impact began to sink in.

"At the time you just cope and get through it for everyone else," said Iola, 68.

"You never forget it. It's still there all the time.

"With the support of my husband and family we've come through it, but it's always there."

Hugh Adams described the family story as "highly unusual"

Hugh Adams from Brain Tumour Research said it remains one of the most underfunded areas of cancer research.

There are around 150 different types of brain tumour ranging from slow-growing to aggressive cancers, each of which can respond differently to treatment.

The charity said only a third with glioblastoma survive beyond a year, and just 4% for five years or more.

"Brain tumours, sadly, are the biggest cancer killer of children and adults under the age of 40, and we need to invest in research to get closer to cures," he said.

"Now, historically, that research from central government, from the devolved nations and governments, has been poor. It's been less than 1%.

"It is starting to increase, but it needs to be greater."

He described Iola's family story as "highly unusual".

"I've worked in this area for a long time and I've not heard of three members of one family, so tragically, being affected by the same tumour type," he added.

"We're not aware of any links based on hereditary makeup.

"So, this is something that's very, very unusual, and perhaps we just say it's just terrible, terrible luck for Iola and her family."

Iola urged the Welsh government to put more money into support and research on this "horrible, horrible illness".

A Welsh government spokesperson said: "We are sorry to hear of Iola's loss, and we recognise how much this matters to everyone whose life has been affected by cancer.

"We are developing a National Cancer Strategy for Wales to transform the way the health service prevents, diagnoses and treats this set of diseases - including brain cancer.

"The 10-year strategy will set out an ambitious future for cancer care in Wales and give the NHS the clarity it needs to focus on this set of conditions and to deliver better services for the people of Wales."

They said that since 2015, more than 200 patients in Wales have been recruited in nearly 20 brain tumour research studies.

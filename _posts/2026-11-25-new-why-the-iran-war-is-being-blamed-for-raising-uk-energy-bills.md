Experts revealed that The latest increase in energy bills is hitting British households hard in the pocket.

Announcing a 26% rise in the energy price cap from January, energy regulator Ofgem said the hike is largely down to the conflict in the Middle East.

Natural gas is used for heating by around 85% of homes, according to trade body Energy UK, and by power companies to generate electricity.

This means that when the wholesale price of gas rises, its feeds directly into the costs British households pay when they use either gas or electricity.

While the price of gas puts pressure on energy bills, other costs including upgrading the electricity grid to cope with more sources of renewable generation, feed into what households pay.

However, Ofgem says that these additional costs made no contribution to the rise in bills in the latest period.

Around a third of Great Britain's natural gas comes from the UK's own North Sea fields.

But the rest is imported - some by pipeline and some by ship as Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) - which means the price paid is determined by international markets.

Since the US-Israel strikes on Iran began on 28 February the UK wholesale price of gas has more than doubled.

Analysts say this is a direct consequence of the Middle East conflict.

Around a fifth of the world's supply of LNG normally comes from the Middle East but key producers like Qatar have seen supplies badly disrupted by Iranian attacks.

After the country's giant Ras Laffan LNG plant was hit by an Iranian strike on 18 March it was estimated that repairs could take up to five years.

The same site was rocked by an explosion in June which killed 13 people. The Qatari energy minister said the blast would not affect the country's exports and it was "an accident and not sabotage or hostile in nature".

This image from June, checked by BBC Verify, shows a fire burning at the Ras Laffan complex in Qatar. Officials said there had been an "operational incident"

And while Middle East producers have been able to significantly restore flows of oil through the Strait of Hormuz despite Iranian threats to shipping - largely by using covert ship-to-ship transfers of oil - the same cannot be done with LNG.

The number of LNG tankers exiting the strait is well down on pre-war numbers according to data from maritime intelligence firm Kpler.

The removal of this significant supply of natural gas from global markets has pushed up international wholesale prices.

Analysts say there is no realistic prospect in the near term of new regional natural-gas pipeline infrastructure being built to bypass the Strait of Hormuz and compensate for lost LNG shipments.

This absence of alternative options means the wholesale price of natural gas is likely to be heavily influenced by the course of the US war with Iran - and whether it escalates or eases.

That means it's impossible to predict when wholesale prices might start to come down again - and when UK households might get some financial relief on their energy bills.

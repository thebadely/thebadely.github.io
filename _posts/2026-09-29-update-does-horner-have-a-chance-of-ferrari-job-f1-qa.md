Mercedes' George Russell took his first victory since June by holding off Max Verstappen to win the Azerbaijan Grand Prix.

Verstappen's Red Bull team-mate Isack Hadjar completed the podium on his return after missing three races with a broken wrist.

This weekend, Formula 1 heads to Malaysia, the host of the Bahrain Grand Prix - an event originally due to take place in April but which was postponed because of the war in the Middle East.

Before the race in Sepang, BBC F1 correspondent Andrew Benson answers your latest questions.

Does Christian Horner really want the team principal job at Ferrari, or even have a shot at it? - Ben

This topic has become a subject for conversation in recent days following an interview Horner did with the Times, external as he promotes his new book. 

Horner said: "At this stage in my life, there's only one team for which I would accept non-participation in ownership."

Asked whether he would take a job there, he replied: "Would I consider Ferrari? I mean, Ferrari is the biggest brand in Formula 1. It's the dream. And arguably it's the sleeping giant of Formula 1."

Ferrari team principal Frederic Vasseur made his views about Horner's intervention pretty plain after the Azerbaijan Grand Prix when he said: "Mr Horner is looking for a job - that, everybody knows - everywhere."

Vasseur said the speculation surrounding the team meant Ferrari were "not working in the perfect serenity".

Horner's comments seem to make it clear that he would consider a job offer from Ferrari. But the key questions are under what conditions he might accept it, and whether he would be offered it in the first place.

Horner has made it very clear over the years that he believes an F1 team needs one person in charge and in overall control.

It's highly unlikely he would get that at Ferrari, where chairman John Elkann and chief executive officer Benedetto Vigna will always have more authority than any team principal.

Even at Red Bull, where Horner had theoretical leadership at the F1 team, he was ultimately answerable to headquarters in Austria and that is what led to his removal in the end.

It's hard to imagine how Elkann and Vigna could or would countenance any situation where they were removed from any input into the F1 team.

And it's even harder to imagine a situation where that was not the de facto position even if a team boss had been given assurances they would be left alone.

Then there is the question of whether Ferrari would want Horner.

Horner comes with baggage. All of the above for a start, and on top of that there is the cloud under which he left Red Bull.

There is no doubt that he was a remarkably successful team principal there, the team winning eight drivers' titles, six constructors' championships and 124 grands prix in his two-decade tenure.

However, the team unravelled in his final 18 months in charge, in the wake of the accusations of sexual harassment and coercive, controlling behaviour that were made against him by a female employee.

Red Bull dismissed those allegations after two internal investigations led by external lawyers.

But they were a key factor in Adrian Newey leaving his position as chief technical officer - along with internal politics in which Newey felt his role had been diminished by Horner.

Newey was followed out of the door by long-time sporting director Jonathan Wheatley and head of strategy Will Courtenay.

Horner was finally dismissed following a slump in form which Red Bull bosses concluded the team did not appear to be capable of arresting under his leadership.

In the Times interview, Horner says that the only justification he was given by Red Bull for his sacking was "it was more Christian Horner Racing than Red Bull Racing".

He adds at other points in the interview: "I am a bit of a Marmite character. I know I'm not everybody's cup of tea. It's impossible to be." And: "I'm the cure and the problem."

Clearly, any decision as to whether Horner is the person you want to lead your F1 team would need careful consideration.

It's also worth mentioning that Ferrari driver Lewis Hamilton has made it pretty clear over the past few years that he has little time for Horner.

Why hasn't the FIA made a decision on the final two races of the season? They've cancelled the final rounds of the World Rally Championship and World Endurance Championship because of the Middle East conflict. Surely they should be confirming the changes in the calendar now? - Nigel

To start with a point of order, it is Formula 1 that decides the calendar - as in, the commercial rights holder - not the FIA, the governing body. The FIA's role is effectively only to rubber-stamp F1's decision.

As things stand, F1 is planning to end the season as the calendar was originally scheduled - with Las Vegas followed by Qatar and then Abu Dhabi, external over the last two weekends in November and the first in December.

Although the war continues between the US/Israel and Iran, the advice F1 has been getting is that the situation in those two countries is stable, and there is not a high risk of attacks on civilian infrastructure.

The UK Foreign Office, for example, does not advise against travel to Qatar and Abu Dhabi., external It simply says the situation there "remains unpredictable" and that "there is a risk of more attacks and unforeseen escalation in the region".

And the two countries consider themselves open for business.

However, F1 has a contingency plan.

Imola in Italy is preparing to host a race on the weekend of Abu Dhabi. 

If the two Middle Eastern races cannot happen as a result of an escalation of the situation in the Gulf, Imola would step in and hold the season finale on 4-6 December.

But unless and until that happens, there is no confirmation of changes to make, as there are currently no changes.

Why wasn't a virtual safety car called when Valtteri Bottas crashed right at the end in Azerbaijan? This was obviously a dangerous situation as the car was buried in the barrier. And it almost cost George Russell a well-deserved victory - Steve

Valtteri Bottas crashed at Turn 15 on the penultimate lap of the race, meaning his Cadillac was in the barriers there when leaders George Russell and Max Verstappen came around their last lap less than a second apart.

Normally, a car in the barriers on a street circuit means at least a virtual safety car.

However, in this instance a spokesperson for governing body the FIA says that race control "determined that it was safe to let the leaders race to the line - they were able to cut the apex at T15, as it's a relatively slow corner".

In the event, the Bottas incident, the FIA says, was "successfully managed by localised double yellows".

Whether this was the correct course of action, and whether the decision was influenced by wanting to prevent the grand prix finishing under caution, will remain a subject for debate.

On the one hand, it allowed the race to continue and be exciting to the end.

On the other, slowing for the incident caused a technical issue for Russell's Mercedes.

"I lifted off the power and when I went back, I had no turbo, so then I had no power," Russell said. "Max almost got me at the line."

Russell was able to mitigate this loss of power from the internal combustion engine because, it being the final lap, he could use up all his battery on the run to the flag, rather than have to keep it balanced for the following lap.

So he managed to hang on - but by less than 0.2 seconds.

Had he not, the decision by race control would have come under a great deal more scrutiny than it did.

Williams' Carlos Sainz qualified ninth in Azerbaijan and although he was given a five-place grid penalty that dropped him to 14th, he finished 10th in the race to score a point 

I love your explainers. Please can you analyse the data explaining the jump Williams have made with their upgrades. It seems more significant than Aston Martin's much written-about 'leap'? What are the percentages, averages etc. - John

First of all, thanks very much for the compliment.

This is a complex topic. There is no doubt that the lighter chassis and aerodynamic upgrades introduced by Williams in Baku marked a step forward.

It was their best qualifying performance of the year in terms of positions, even if in percentage terms they were actually slightly closer to the front in Miami back in May.

But it's too early to judge the Williams upgrades - and Aston Martin provides a clue as to why.

This year, there is quite a swing in competitiveness for certain teams depending on circuit type.

Regardless of the upgrades, Williams always expected Baku to be one of their stronger circuits - short-duration corners and long straights suit their car.

But they struggle in long-duration corners, so it will be interesting to see how they go this weekend in Malaysia, because Sepang is full of them.

Likewise, Aston Martin have specific weaknesses.

Their aerodynamic upgrade in Hungary improved their chassis performance by in the region of two seconds a lap.

Hence their significant improvement there and in Zandvoort, where Fernando Alonso drove superbly to take two points for ninth place.

But, despite its upgrade for Zandvoort, the Honda engine remains the worst on the grid, in terms of overall power, driveability and energy management.

So, it's no surprise that on two hugely engine-dependent circuits such as Monza and Baku, they would struggle.

On that basis, Aston Martin can be expected to be relatively more competitive again in Singapore, with its low power-sensitivity, and where Alonso is usually outstanding.

Let's dive into the details: The park was evacuated after the attack on Saturday afternoon

Yorkshire Wildlife Park remains closed for a third day as investigations continue into the death of a man who was attacked by a tiger when he entered its enclosure.

The park near Doncaster was closed over the weekend after reports of a man climbing down into the enclosure from a walkway on Saturday afternoon.

An eyewitness described seeing the man wearing headphones and "pacing around" near the enclosure before he went in and was attacked.

A park spokesperson said: "Due to the events over the weekend, the park will remain closed today. Thank you for your understanding."

South Yorkshire Police said the man died at the scene and his family were being supported by officers. "Our thoughts are with them at this distressing time," the force said.

Yorkshire Wildlife Park said the rare Amur tiger, called Altai, remained in the enclosure and would not be euthanised.

Cars could be seen coming and going from the park earlier, but there were no visitors.

In a message to ticket holders on Monday morning, Yorkshire Wildlife Park said anyone with a booking would be contacted within the next five days to reschedule.

Police were called to the park at about 13:35 on Saturday

Angela Seivewright, 47, from Bawtry, was laying flowers outside the park gates in tribute to the deceased man. She said she had an annual pass and had visited many times with her two children.

"My daughter was unfortunately sent the video and she's a teenager," she said.

"I found it very distressing, but I think what I've found more distressing is the reaction of the public that I received when I said I hope the human is OK.

"I was surprised at the amount of anger and hatred the people seemed to have towards the gentleman."

On Saturday evening, police said they were aware of videos circulating online and encouraged people not to share the footage.

Angela Seivewright, who has an annual pass to the park, laid flowers on Monday morning

Dr Campbell Murn, a previous member of the government's zoo expert committee and lecturer at the University of Reading, said while it was a tragic incident, determined people could get inside zoo enclosures.

"I view it in the same way a determined thief will get into somebody's house or somebody's car and despite the best security systems and enclosures sometimes these things happen," he said.

"One of the fundamental approaches of a modern zoo is to give people a wildlife experience and keep them safe so they can learn and they can enjoy being near the animals, but keeping a safety distance that is relevant but not making it too heavy handed."

Speaking on BBC Radio 4's Today programme, Murn said public safety was an "enormous consideration" for zoo standards in the UK.

A spokesperson for the Born Free Foundation, which campaigns to phase out zoos, called for an "urgent review" into "how the man managed to enter the enclosure and what happened next".

Born Free's co-founder Will Travers said: "Putting dangerous wild animals on public display in zoos and safari parks creates a false relationship with humans. 

"Visitors are encouraged to get up close and personal - that is a deeply unnatural situation and not without risk."

An eyewitness took this photograph of the tiger minutes before the incident

Jo Judge, chief executive of the British and Irish Association of Zoos and Aquariums, said zoos were subject to a "huge number" of risk assessments, along with full inspections every six years and periodic inspections every three.  

"There are so many processes and procedures in place to ensure the safety of everybody who is visiting zoos and aquariums and these are incredibly rare incidents," she said.

"Zoos are actually one of the safest places you can visit."

She added the body had received many messages of support for the Yorkshire Wildlife Park.

"There's no concern from the rest of the community, because we know how well regulated we are, how well licensed we are, all of the procedures and policies that have to be put in place to make sure zoos and aquariums are safe for staff, for the animals and for the public."

The park in South Yorkshire remains closed to visitors

The tiger has been at Yorkshire Wildlife Park since it was moved from Dyreparken in Norway in February 2025 as part of the international EAZA European Endangered Species Programme for Amur tigers.

Dyreparken biologist Helene Axelsen said Altai was born at the site in 2021. 

She said: "We were deeply saddened to hear about the tragic incident at Yorkshire Wildlife Park over the weekend. 

"Our thoughts are first and foremost with the victim's family, loved ones, and the entire team at Yorkshire Wildlife Park during this extremely difficult time."

Retired animal behaviourist Dr David Sands said anyone entering an enclosure would be seen as a threat.

"The animal is just being the animal that one million years of evolution has produced," he said. 

"It's the top apex predator of its group of animals and I don't think any blame can be put on to the animal itself.

"Could you make the enclosure higher, bigger, more inner protections - well, where do you stop?"

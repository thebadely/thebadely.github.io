The increasing price of school dinners in Northern Ireland has left some pupils "struggling just to get a hot meal," one teenage student has said.

Robert-Frederic, 17, is among pupils at Belfast's Malone Integrated College who spoke out after it was announced prices are to rise for the second year in a row.

Post-primary pupils will pay an extra 15% on their canteen choices from January, while set meals at pre-school, primary and special schools will rise by 30p.

The Education Authority (EA) said the amount parents would have to pay was still well below the average cost of producing a school meal, estimated at over Â£5.20.

Families recently had to set aside extra money for term-time meals after primary and special school dinners increased by 50p in January 2026.

At that time, post-primary pupils were informed they would pay almost 20% more for the food they bought in school canteens. 

Robert-Frederic told BBC News NI that the previous increase had already made paying for lunches "more difficult for all students".

"Back before the price rise in 2026, you could get water, a cookie and your hot meal and now with the price rise students are struggling just to get a hot meal," he said.

"Instead of just going off numbers on a page, you should actually see how it affects people."

Malone Integrated College pupil Naomi said lack of proper food can affect learning

More than half (55%) of pupils at Malone Integrated College are entitled to free school meals but many others are from low incomes households.

"It's horrible seeing people around me not being able to afford school meals," said 17-year-old Naomi.

"It also puts pressure on the parents," she added.

"Without proper lunches it can affect people's learning and their mental health."

For 16-year-old Paddy, the last price hike meant he had to change his canteen choices at Malone.

"Some of my favourite foods such as like the school chicken - that went up," he said.

Education Minister Paul Givan said he was disappointed and concerned but added it was due to underfunding of the education sector as a whole. 

"I don't want this to be introduced, but it is a result of the Education Authority not having sufficient funding," he told the BBC's Good Morning Ulster programme. 

He said education often bears "the greatest burden" during cost-saving exercises and argued his department should receive a greater share of Stormont's budget. 

Givan also said the "inadequate" funding that Stormont receives from Westminster was "bearing down on a huge number of public services in Northern Ireland".

Stormont ministers are yet to agree a budget, despite weeks of talks with Secretary of State Sir Chris Bryant, who has repeatedly said there is "no more money".

Before we get too deep, it's worth knowing that a current Prem player moving to a different Prem club is not the same as a player coming in from outside the league. 

Basically, don't think that Pollock can be compared to Scotland's Finn Russell at Bath, as there are different rules.

Time to grab a pen and paper. In the Prem salary cap regulations section 3.26 on excluded players, the first problem Pollock has is that to become the excluded player he must have "been a player of that club for at least two full salary cap years prior to the salary cap year in which they are nominated to be an excluded player".

This means Pollock can't just become Northampton's excluded player next season, they have to declare him to be that player in the future. But why, I hear you cry.

It's to stop clubs poaching players off each other and back-loading player contracts. 

We learnt about this during the Covid pandemic, when clubs agreed bigger pay-days with their players in advance of the new lower salary cap regs being introduced, so that their salary didn't count in the reduced cap.

It also prevents sides from manipulating the cap. For example, you can't pay player X Â£5m in year one of an excluded deal, then just Â£100 in year's two and three, rinse and repeat with a new big name every year. Not allowed.

The way the cap stops clubs doing this is a process of "averaging".

These rules mean it's actually quite hard for players to move around the league and command the excluded player spots.

To conclude the excluded player regs, it's also worth knowing that we're not allowed to know the identity of any excluded players.

You can guess, assume, even ask them, but technically "a club is not entitled to know the identity of the excluded players of other clubs".

This means that if Northampton already have a player nominated for the excluded player spot they would probably have to unpick a huge amount of spreadsheet calculations to move it over to Pollock.

To those outside the sport much of this will seem like madness and an unfair market. However, the salary cap is basically designed to stop clubs making poor financial decisions or over-stretch themselves. The demise of Wasps, Worcester and London Irish being the forefront of this.

Prem Rugby told BBC Sport: "The salary cap is designed to prevent clubs from over-spending. It helps control inflationary pressures from club costs and ensures the viability and competitiveness of clubs.

"This year the Prem is forecast to post record revenues of more than Â£200m for the first time, with that number forecast to grow again year-on-year."

With six different Prem winners in seven seasons, you can't argue that the league isn't competitive.

Clubs like Saracens have long championed salary cap increases and now that the league's stability has been assured following the removal of promotion and relegation, this is likely the next chapter in the salary cap debate.

Prem Rugby said: "Any change to the wider cap structure would always go through a full club consultation and be up for discussion amongst the men's professional Rugby Board before any decision is made.

"The removal of promotion and relegation is a separate strategic decision aimed at giving clubs long-term investment certainty, as is evident with the new ownership of Red Bull at Newcastle and Black Knight Rugby at Exeter Chiefs - as well as the new investment from Sir James Dyson at Bath."

Some players can command big commercial deals outside rugby, and these deals will eclipse their club earnings multiple times over.

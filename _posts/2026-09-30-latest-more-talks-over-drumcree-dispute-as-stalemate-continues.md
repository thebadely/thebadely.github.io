Northern Ireland Secretary Sir Chris Bryant is holding further talks with community leaders on Wednesday over the controversial Drumcree march.

It comes after lawyers for the Garvaghy Road Residents Coalition issued a fresh legal bid to try to prevent the march from going ahead.

Members of the Orange Order are still waiting to begin their permitted parade, which was due to take place on Sunday, and walk down the mainly-nationalist Garvaghy Road in Portadown.

The residents' appeal will be the latest challenge to the march, which was given the go ahead after three separate legal hearings over the course of 13 hours late on Saturday and into the early hours of Sunday morning.

Wednesday's talks at Hillsborough Castle are taking place in an upstairs room which, under normal circumstances, is used as a sitting room.

The Garvaghy Road Residents' Coalition and the Portadown Orange Order will wait in separate rooms before going upstairs for talks with the secretary of state.

Senior members from the Orange Order, including Portadown District Master Nigel Dawson and Deputy District Master Alastair Power, met the secretary of state on Wednesday morning. 

They were accompanied by loyalist activist Jamie Bryson, who has acted as an adviser for the lodge.

Speaking after their meeting, Dawson said the contents of the meetings remained "confidential".

But he added that the secretary of state had been left "in no doubt" that the Orange Order's position continued to be that he and the chief constable must apply the law to allow the march to take place.

Bryson was asked about what had happened and said while he did not want to speak for the Orange Order, "if the law is not applied how can the executive possibly survive?"

It is understood Bryson has not been part of the discussions with Sir Chris and has been waiting in a separate room.

The discussions are a form of proximity talks, with the two sides not meeting face-to-face but both being under the same roof.

Wednesday's discussions are the third day in a row that Hillsborough talks have taken place.

At this stage there is no sign of progress but the fact that both sides are still engaged in discussions is being seen by government officials as a positive.

Keeping the talks going, however, remains a challenge with neither side showing a willingness to compromise.

The Drumcree parade, which in the 1990s sparked some of the most serious disorder in Northern Ireland's history, was due to go ahead on Sunday for the first time in almost 30 years after a series of legal challenges.

Since 1998, the Orange Order had been banned from completing its Drumcree march - and until this year had been repeatedly refused permission to finish the route by the Parades Commission, which was set up to adjudicate disputes.

The Orange Order is a Protestant pro-British organisation, while the area where it wants to walk - specifically along the Garvaghy Road - is largely Catholic and predominantly inhabited by pro-Irish nationalists.

The Orange Order, the most prominent of the loyal orders associated with Protestants in Northern Ireland, holds thousands of parades every year and the vast majority pass off without incident.

However, many Catholics see the marches as sectarian and object to them passing through, or close to, Catholic or nationalist areas.

The Garvaghy Road had been part of the Drumcree route, but by the mid 1990s nationalist residents protested against the parade. The row sparked some of the most serious public disorder in Northern Ireland's history and was linked to several murders.

The Drumcree dispute was one of Northern Ireland's most serious parading disputes during the 1990s, when stand-offs over attempts by the Orange Order to march along the Garvaghy Road were accompanied by riots, roadblocks and killings.

When the Orange Order was prevented by police from walking down the route in July 1995, there was a tense stand-off. It was the same for the next three years.

Violence related to the dispute resulted in riots, roadblocks and murders across Northern Ireland.

In 1998, the newly-established Parades Commission ruled that the Orange Order should not march down the road - a decision upheld for the last 28 years.

The row reemerged last month after a court case in which a ruling by the Parades Commission was overturned, and a fresh application was lodged.

On Friday, the commission decided the march could go ahead with restrictions in place. However that decision was challenged in several Belfast courts on Saturday, before finally being given the go ahead just hours before it was due to take place.

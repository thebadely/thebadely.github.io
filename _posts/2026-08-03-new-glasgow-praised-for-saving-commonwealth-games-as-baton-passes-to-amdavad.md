The man responsible for delivering Glasgow 2026 said the city had "saved and reinvented" the Commonwealth Games, three years after its future was plunged into doubt.

Ahead of Sunday's closing ceremony - which featured a dazzling fireworks display over the River Clyde - Phil Batty recalled how Glasgow stepped up when the original hosts pulled out.

And he believes the legacy of the sustainable, scaled-back Games will shape the future of the multi-sports event.

The Commonwealth flag was last night ceremonially handed over to the Indian city of Amdavad, which will stage the centenary event in 2030.  

But Batty said Glasgow's decision to host the event for a second time in 12 years would never be forgotten.

He told BBC Scotland News: "We have created a Games here that will inspire more nations to host but, probably more importantly, that every nation feels it could host at some point in the next 100 years."

Simple Minds, led by frontman Jim Kerr, closed the ceremony with two of their biggest hits

Simple Minds brought the curtain down on an 11-day sporting festival during the closing ceremony at the Hydro on Sunday.

As athletes from 74 nations and territories looked on they opened with Alive and Kicking.

It was appropriate song choice for an event that was, not too long ago, on life support.

In July 2023 the Australian state of Victoria withdrew from staging the Games due to soaring costs.

But in September 2024 Glasgow, with support from the Scottish government,   agreed to a put on a scaled-back event.

Phil Batty, chief executive of Glasgow 2026, said the city has helped shape the future of the Commonwealth Games

Batty said the city had, over the last 11 days, answered any questions around the relevance and, indeed the existence, of the Games.

He told BBC Scotland News: "I don't think we can underestimate that the future of the Commonwealth Games was in jeopardy.

"Glasgow saved the Commonwealth Games but it also reinvented the Commonwealth Games."

He said this process included re-formatting sports for the future.

"It isn't about saving something to preserve it. It is about reinventing something to give it the purpose and vision it needs to last," he added.

Australian singer Delta Goodrem welcomed the athletes into the Hydro for the closing ceremony

Batty said the event was for far too long "hosted by the few and not the many".

But he added: "This is a really important moment for the movement to become a truly Commonwealth-wide global sporting competition.

"Glasgow and Scotland should be incredibly proud, of not what only has happened here but what that means for the whole Commonwealth in the years and decades to come." 

Princess Charlotte high fives official Games mascot Finnie outside the Hydro

The Glasgow 2026 boss described how the Games may develop.

He explained: "It may be a twin nation host in Asia, it may be a multi-island host in the Pacific Islands, It might be an absolutely compact multi-venue model in Africa.

"This will look different in every edition but, importantly, it will now be designed to the place that is hosting."

Batty said the privately-funded event had been delivered over 18 months on "triple speed" compared to a typical build-up of six years.

Batty said it was "fuelled by the legacy" of the last Games in the city and more environmentally stable.

This follows as it used existing venues and infrastructure within an eight-mile radius. 

English powerlifter Mark Swan won the first gold medal of the 215 that were in offer at Glasgow 2026

The more compact 23rd edition featured 215 medal events compared to 261 when the city last staged the Games in 2014.

It also saw a record 47 para-sports events, which included the symbolic first gold medal.

It was won by English lightweight powerlifter Mark Swan.

The following day organisers were dealt a major blow when reigning 10,000m champion Eilish McColgan pulled out with a foot fracture. 

Abgharad Evans of Team Scotland is seen on the podium with her 200m breaststroke gold

But new heroes stepped up for the host nation, most notably swimmer Angharad Evans who won two golds, a silver and a bronze.

Further inspiration was found in the pool by in the shape of Archie Goodburn, who is fighting brain cancer.

Scotland's Melanie Woods holds the David Dixon Award for the 'outstanding athlete of the Games and fair play' during the closing ceremony

Scotland's Melanie Woods won gold in the 1500m T54 for the second time in under 40 hours after the final was restaged.

Her victory, in a new record time, was recognised at the closing ceremony when she was named the outstanding athlete of the Games.

And para cycling great Neil Fachie bowed out with the seventh gold of his career.

Kurtis Marschall of Team Australia reacts after winning the gold medal in the Men's Pole Vault Final for the third Games in a row

Australia dominated the medal table by amassing 171 followed by England with 110.

The host nation finished fifth, with 39 medals, behind Canada and India, a performance described by First Minister John Swinney as "truly historic".

Emma Finucane of Team Wales celebrates her victory in the Women's Sprint Finals

Records also tumbled in the velodrome, on synthetic carpet and in the pool, as fans were treated to some world-class sporting action.

Welsh star Emma Finucane created history by becoming the first sprint cyclist to win four track gold medals at the same Games.

Louise Hoskins became the oldest Commonwealth Games gold medallist, winning the para-bowls women's pairs title for Australia at the age of 76 years and 173 days.

And legendary South African swimmer Chad le Clos became the most decorated athlete in the 96-year history of the event with 21 medals. 

Thousands of fans watched Team Jamaica play Team New Zealand in the final of the netball at the Hydro 

Empty seats were visible at many events but organisers said they exceeded their targets by selling more than 430,000 tickets.

The action unfolded across four venues - each powered by renewable energy - with the most noticeable buzz around the SEC Campus.

Netball at the Hydro proved to be box office by selling 100,000 tickets.

Batty said the Games had been able to grow the fan base and make it the single largest attended sport in the programme. 

On Saturday Prince William and Princess Kate were among those who took in the action with their children.

Mile world record holder Josh Kerr celebrates his Commonwealth title

Elsewhere, Tollcross International Swimming Centre was a cauldron of noise during the medal events.

And Scotstoun Stadium was the scene for everything from a biblical downpour on Tuesday to a race for the ages on Saturday night.

Indeed those who witnessed world record holder Josh Kerr's thrilling triumph in the mile - which last featured at the Games in 1966 - will never forget it.

The star repaid fans for their support by taking 45 minutes to pose for selfies and autographs after his emotional medal ceremony.

The King and Queen Camilla arrived at the opening ceremony in a Tardis with Olympic cycling legend Sir Chris Hoy  

Glasgow's famous sense of humour was a common thread throughout the Games.

The sight of the King and Queen emerging from a Tardis at the start of the opening ceremony in the Hydro stunned the audience.

And Callum Beattie's performance of the Scotland football anthem Yes Sir, I Can Boogie - featuring silver unicorns and dancing Barrowland-style stars - put a smile on everyone's face.

At the end a giant Tunnock's Teacake rocket took off on stage, in tribute to the dancing versions from the 2014 opening ceremony at Celtic Park.

It later appeared to have landed at the Games Live site in Kelvingrove Park, where it provided a popular photo opportunity.

More than 200,000 visited the west end park and live sites in the city during the Games.

Fun was also injected some fun into proceedings at the Sir Chris Hoy velodrome in the east end. 

Before every race the cycling legend appeared on the big screen and bellowed: "Hoi you lot! Shhhh."

Swimmer Duncan Scott, who won his fourth Commonwealth gold, then added: "Haud your wheesht" - a traditional Scots phrase meaning "be quiet."

Team Scotland athletes arrived at the Hydro on Sunday wearing traffic cone hats

On Sunday, Team Scotland stood out at the closing ceremony with traffic cone hats, in a nod to the iconic Duke of Wellingston statue. 

Away from the action the Games mascot Finnie proved a merchandising hit.

Small cuddly toys of the unicorn sold out in store and online before the weekend.

Meanwhile, athletes and Games staff continued the long standing tradition of collecting team pin badges, resulting in some impressive bling lanyards.

And throughout all the action an army of 4,000 volunteers - aged 16 to 96 - kept everything running smoothly. 

During the closing ceremony George Black, chair of Glasgow 2026, described them as "the heartbeat of the Games".

Batty said both developments meant there was now a "pathway and a pipeline to the future".

Asked how Glasgow 2026 would be remembered, he said: "The Commonwealth is never going to forget what Glasgow did on behalf of 74 nations.

"It did it with bravery and courage.

"I think Glasgow will be remembered as the friend that was there when 73 other nations needed a helping hand.

"And I think that kindness and generosity will live long in the memory of the whole Commonwealth."

Aleesha Adams joined a video call with Kashim Chowdhury in May 2023.

He'd been on sick leave from his job at the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) for nearly three months and Adams, his line manager, wanted to put a plan in action to ease him back into work.

At the start of the call, Chowdhury was coughing and waving tissues up to the camera in an attempt to show how ill he was. But what really caught Adams' attention was how pale he looked.

She noticed white powder had started separating from his forehead. "He was sweating, it was like rubbing off, so it was darker underneath and darker on the neck," she told his trial.

She claimed he was wearing white make-up to look sick. Something he denied in court.

Little did she know UKHSA was not Chowdhury's only employer.

That month, he was also being paid to work full-time for the Department for the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra), the Department for Business and Trade, the Food Standards Agency and the Home Office. But his fraudulent behaviour remained undetected. Chowdhury was able to keep working multiple government jobs until September 2023 when he left the Civil Service.

He was finally caught in 2024 by the government's National Fraud Initiative, which conducts searches of Civil Service databases, such as payroll, every two years to look for anomalies.

Chowdhury, 56, arrived each day at court alone. He is married, yet there never seemed to be any friends or family there to support him.

He sometimes looked bewildered in the witness box. 

Chowdhury, who had a diagnosis of PTSD in 2015, said that he had taken on so many roles because he feared losing his livelihood after experiencing insecure employment. He said he'd been forced out of a job at the Department for Education in 2013.

He said he hadn't been thinking clearly when applying for jobs ''because of my stress, distress and trauma and due to my panic at the time".

But the prosecution argued his behaviour was "deliberate" and "intentional dishonesty" and that he had exploited the system.

However, the case raises important questions for the Civil Service. How was he able to get away with it for so long?

Part of the explanation is that the Civil Service's human resources system is not centralised. Chowdhury did not declare on government application forms that he was already a civil servant, so the system did not spot the duplication.

Hannah Keenan, who works at the Institute for Government, says the system clearly failed. "Government departments are taking hiring decisions independently of each other and without systems that join up," she said. 

"That the model and the culture surrounding it aren't working shouldn't be a surprise though - we've known for a long time about poor performers flying under the radar by shuffling between jobs."

There are more than 550,000 people employed across the Civil Service, many in offices in Whitehall, in central London. They are responsible for carrying out government policy and the daily administration of the country.

Another reason why Chowdhury's fraud went undetected is that he was working entirely from the east London house he shared with his mother. In 2023, she had dementia and terminal cancer, and he told his employers that he needed to be at home to look after her.

But rather than reducing his workload to concentrate on her care, he took on more jobs. He covered up his impossible daily schedule by getting sick notes from his GP.

In April 2023, he was off with what he called "mixed anxiety and depressive disorder" from Defra, the UKHSA, the Department for Business and Trade and the Food Standards Agency.

While the sick notes were shown to the jury, they were not given evidence about what caused the depressive disorder or of a psychiatric assessment having been done during this period.

Unsurprisingly, a succession of his line managers told the jury he had not been very good at his jobs.

Especially damning was the evidence given by Dan McCartney, who managed Chowdhury at Defra. He said he had to "effectively tell him what to do the whole time" and that Chowdhury kept his camera off during video calls for four months, claiming technical problems.

But because Chowdhury was not subject to any formal performance measures at Defra, he was given a permanent job. He remained there for two years.

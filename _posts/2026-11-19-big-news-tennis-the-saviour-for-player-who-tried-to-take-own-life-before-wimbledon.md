Warning: This article contains references to suicide

The clues were there. Leandro Riedi struggled to even force a smile after winning a match which moved him closer to the Wimbledon main draw.

These are the moments usually cherished and celebrated by a player ranked outside of the world's top 100.

The Swiss player's solemn reaction was jarring, but then nobody outside of his immediate circle knew what had happened a few days earlier.

Shortly before Wimbledon qualifying began in June 2024, Riedi had tried to take his own life.

Panic attacks had been plaguing the world number 137 for almost 18 months. Anxiety and self-doubt had been around for much longer.

The toll they were taking on his mental health meant he felt there was no other way out.

"I always had anxiety. I was always a shy person, especially when I was younger. I always had this mindset that I wasn't good enough," Riedi told BBC Sport.

"I always thought I needed to do something special. I have to be perfect. This was my mental battle all the time.

"February 2023 to November 2024 was a horrible period of time. I was always scared of having a panic attack. I had no control over it whatsoever."

Very few people knew the extent of Riedi's mental health issues until he revealed last week that he had attempted suicide.

The 24-year-old wants to make two things clear.

Firstly, he is not looking for sympathy. He has only decided to talk about his experience to "send a strong message" for others struggling with their mental health.

Secondly, he is now in a "very good" place, thanks to professional treatment he has received over the past two years.

"I hope nobody ever has to go through what I did - it's horrifying and scary," Riedi said.

"But I want to be an example it can turn out well."

I don't know how I played at Wimbledon - Riedi

In his darkest moments Riedi found salvation in the sport which he started playing as a three-year-old growing up in Zurich.

"I felt really home at a tennis court - I was able to perform and I was able to play," he said.

"So tennis was my saviour in that time. It helped me to go out of my thoughts for one second and focus on doing what I love."

It goes some way to explaining why Riedi decided to still compete at Wimbledon so soon after attempting to end his life.

Looking back, he is unsure how he summoned the strength to play.

"My coach told me to go home. He was the only one who knew. But then what? Be sad at home?" Riedi said.

"I didn't want to go home. I wanted to stay and I wanted to play. It was a horrible, horrible week.

"After the first round I remember I just left and went to the hotel. Usually you're excited and happy about a win, but I couldn't speak."

In his hotel room Riedi continued to have troubled thoughts.

He credits his psychologist, who stayed awake with Riedi until 3am to make sure he was not alone, for saving his life.

Riedi went on to win his second qualifying match before losing in the third and final round. At first he felt the pressure was "lifted", but not competing created a void.

Riedi describes how he was "scared" to leave his hotel room for the next two weeks, only going out to collect food deliveries from the lobby.

Tennis continued to be the only force which Riedi thought provided purpose. He managed to leave London for a tournament in Sweden, banking on it "bringing happiness".

But, after winning a match in Gstaad, he started to realise that going on to court was merely a placebo effect.

The catalyst of change came a few weeks later when a knee injury - the first "big injury" of his career - required surgery following the US Open.

"It was devastating for me because I knew my one happiness - tennis - was gone," Riedi said.

"In tennis I always had to fight [my mental health] because I was still playing. I didn't want to show any weakness.

"Then I realised I didn't want to fight any more. I wanted to help it. I wanted to work with it."

Riedi (right) reached the Surbiton Challenger final just before Wimbledon qualifying in 2024, finishing runner-up to South Africa's Lloyd Harris (left)

Focusing on tennis and receiving the support of his "closest people" helped Riedi through his most troubled times.

Reopening his psychological "wounds", and discussing his inner trauma with experts, has been the key to his recovery.

He no longer has suicidal thoughts. The panic attacks have stopped. He still has anxiety but possesses the coping mechanisms to prevent it being debilitating.

Riedi had been planning to open up publicly for several months. He still felt nervous when he hit publish on his social media post, but he has been heartened by an overwhelmingly warm response.

"I have got messages from all over the world. Someone texted me from India saying they saw the post and are going through something similar right now," Riedi said.

"They said 'Your message really helped me. It gave me some power to believe it's going to work.' That's exactly what I was aiming for.

"People think tennis pros have all they need. I wanted to showcase that it's OK not to be OK even though others think you have everything."

Riedi says his mental health struggles were caused by "private issues" in his life and were not a consequence of his career.

But, with players increasingly speaking out about mental burnout, he wants his words to drive further change.

The ATP already provides an in-house psychology and wellbeing service, which offers confidential support at tournaments and remotely.

If a mental health disorder prevents a player from competing for at least six months, they can apply for a protected ranking. It allows them to enter tournaments without penalty when they are ready to return.

Riedi says the governing bodies can "never do too much", but he thinks there is still an issue on the men's tour with reaching out for support.

"For me, mental health is like a muscle. If your knee is injured, you do surgery and take care of it," he said.

"If you're injured in your mind, it's like you can't speak about it. That's not good.

"I hope my message shows tennis players, or anyone, that speaking out isn't a weakness. It's actually the opposite. It's strong and brave."

Since working on his mental health, Riedi has produced some of the best results of his career.

He came through qualifying to reach the last 16 of the 2025 US Open - his standout performance at a Grand Slam tournament - and climbed to a career-high world ranking of 116 this year.

The most poignant moment came in June 2025 when he returned to Wimbledon.

A year on from his suicide attempt, Riedi decided to stay at the same Chelsea hotel because he wanted "make peace" with what had happened.

"It was very emotional, but in a good way," he said.

"I was like, 'wow, when you really work on something, if you stay consistent about something, one year can do so much'."

This year Riedi's progress on the court has been hampered by a torn tendon in a wrist.

Being sidelined for six months led to some friends wondering why he does not stop playing professionally. He says his "love and passion" for tennis will not allow him.

Riedi says the "sky is still the limit" and plans to make his comeback at the start of 2027, using a protected injury ranking to enter Australian Open qualifying.

"I really had to go through big obstacles, but I'm very proud I did this at such a young age. I'm actually this guy who just likes life," he said.

"I love to do things. I want to be happy. I want to be excited for my future and not be scared."

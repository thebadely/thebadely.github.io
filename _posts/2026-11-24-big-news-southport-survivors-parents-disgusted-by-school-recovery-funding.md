Parents of children injured in the Southport knife attack have criticised "huge discrepancies" in support money given to schools in the area after the stabbing.

Schools attended by 10 of the survivors received more than a million pounds in recovery funding between them, while schools attended by 13 other survivors received no direct funding, the families' lawyers have said.

The mother of a girl the BBC has been calling Daisy, who was seven when she was stabbed 33 times in the attack, said she was "horrified" by the differences in funding.

"We have had to fight for every penny to support our daughter, and none of it has come from this fund," she said.

"To know that we have had such a private, anonymous, invisible battle to get our daughter the most basic support and care she needs at school, and that it has been provided by other local authorities, while Sefton Council was handing out over half a million pounds to a single school just makes me feel incredibly angry and sad."

She said that the council should have approached every school attended by children who were present at the attack and ensured that they had fair access to support.

The mother of two other surviving girls said that she was "absolutely disgusted".

"The council never spoke to us or asked us what we needed, meanwhile our daughters have been passed from pillar to post, moving schools to try and get the help they needed in class after everything they had been through," she said.

The elder daughter, whom we are calling Sarah, spoke to BBC Panorama last year. Aged 14 at the time of the attack, she was seriously wounded but managed to lead several other children, including her young sister, to safety. 

The girls' father added: "Nobody is suggesting schools shouldn't have received funding. Nor should anyone question the importance of remembering the children whose lives were lost.

"But what about those who survived; the children who were physically injured; those who witnessed the unimaginable; those who are still struggling with their education, their mental health and everyday life."

Nicola Ryan-Donnelly, of Fletchers Solicitors, which represents 22 of the 23 surviving children, said the disparity in funding is "shameful and outrageous".

"Eye-watering sums have been handed out to single schools, while six schools attended by 13 surviving girls we represent received absolutely nothing from this pot," she said.

She accused Sefton Council of having "completely failed in its due diligence" to establish where money and support were most needed.

A spokesperson for Sefton Council said: "The children, families and school communities affected by the events of July 2024 remain at the heart of Southport's recovery and support of course continues."

The council said that it had initially made support available across Southport schools, with additional funding for those facing specific pressures arising from the attack.

It added that "schools who came to the council with additional needs were asked to provide specific information about what they required" and that "allocations varied based on the type of help required by those schools."

The council also said that the individual school grants were only one part of a wider response, which included increasing capacity in children and family services across the local authority.

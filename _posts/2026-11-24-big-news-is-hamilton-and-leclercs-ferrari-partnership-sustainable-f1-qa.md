After Las Vegas, have Ferrari now reached a crossroads? With Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc's on-track antics, is their partnership sustainable? Should Ferrari consider a change of direction, potentially bringing Oliver Bearman into the equation? - Michael

The battle between Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc at Ferrari is one that keeps on giving - but not in a way Ferrari would want.

The collision in Las Vegas for which Leclerc was penalised was just the latest flashpoint for the team in a season that has been full of them.

The drivers argued over race strategy in relation to each other in Zandvoort and Singapore.

Hamilton made an aggressive dive down the inside at the first chicane on the first lap at Monza, only for Leclerc to shove him on to the gravel.

Hamilton ignored calls to pit in both Singapore (because he didn't want to just circulate behind Leclerc) and Brazil (because, he said, he had not been given enough information about the weather).

There was the first-lap incident at Turn Four in Mexico in which their fighting led to both going off track and letting in Max Verstappen to win a race Ferrari should have finished one-two.

And now Las Vegas, where first Hamilton earned a 10-second penalty for gaining a position on Lando Norris on track in an incident that also involved Leclerc, then Leclerc was given a 10-second penalty for tipping Hamilton into a spin later in the race.

Leclerc was on the receiving end of team principal Frederic Vasseur's ire in Las Vegas - the Frenchman calling the situation "unacceptable". But inside Ferrari they felt both drivers had a degree of culpability for the Mexico situation.

Somehow Vasseur needs to get a handle on this.

Ferrari want to allow them to race. And it's hard to imagine two drivers of such standing would accept anything else.

The problem is that doing so, amid their battle for supremacy within the team, is harming the team's results.

The concern for Ferrari is this is happening when there is relatively little at stake. It's easy to imagine how combustible it could become if Ferrari have a car capable of challenging for the title next year.

Both want to be the driver to deliver that next championship, for very different reasons.

Hamilton would get his eighth, which he feels he was unfairly denied in Abu Dhabi in 2021. And Leclerc would win his first, and complete the journey of being the first Ferrari academy driver to win the world championship for the team.

At the moment, this is not an existential question, but it's easy to see how it could become one, and Ferrari left with a decision of it being one or the other if they can't find a way to race without compromising the team.

But that's not just on Vasseur.

Both drivers have buy into that and, as Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri have largely done at McLaren, accept there will be days when they have to sacrifice themselves for the greater good of Ferrari while ensuring they are ahead of the other more often than not on speed and fair racing.

It's then up to the team to decide when there comes a point to impose team orders if either or both are in a title fight with another driver.

It remains to be seen whether either of them can fully buy into that in circumstances when they individually end up losing out.

Why does George Russell often feel the need to try mind games and make comments, such as those about Max Verstappen? It often backfires on him and are usually made when he's got nothing to race for. Shouldn't he focus on himself? - Nick

This, as Mercedes team principal Toto Wolff pointed out after the race in Las Vegas, is a rivalry that goes back years.

"They've known each other so long," was Wolff's verdict. "Since karting. I think it's probably been a bit of cat and mouse. Provocations on either side. Another good factor of entertainment."

Verstappen also had a bit of a set-to with Racing Bulls' Liam Lawson in Mexico practice, which was not dissimilar in some ways.

There, stewards ruled that both had driven in an "unacceptable manner" and both drivers admitted their moves had been unnecessary and clumsy.

There's no question Russell believed what he was saying about Verstappen's driving in final practice at Las Vegas. According to the stewards, he was in the right, as Verstappen was given a warning for his changing direction in the braking zone.

Are these mind games? Or just Russell saying what he thinks? He feels he has a valid point to make.

Making it in public does not seem to affect Verstappen in any way. Does it change anything? Verstappen is a remorseless, ruthless competitor on track whether he is provoked or not.

Yes, he admitted satisfaction when he saw Russell make his late-race error in Las Vegas. But did he race any differently because of the Briton's comments? That's unlikely. Verstappen was just doing what he felt was right to win the race.

Ultimately, it's Russell's career, and it's up to him how he conducts himself, what he chooses to say in public and what he does not.

'Russell is living rent free in Verstappen's head!'

Track design, cold temperatures, energy deployment strategies or just the unwillingness of one team-mate to hold station behind another. What was the dominant factor in Las Vegas that led to such a hectic race? - Tim

The extraordinary Las Vegas race, with long trains of cars unable to break free of each other, was a combination of technical factors. Nothing to do with the willingness or otherwise of team-mates to hold station.

It was a combination of the track characteristics, particularly the long straights, the energy deployment demands of the engines, which are energy starved, and the cold temperatures and track surface and the effect they had on tyre behaviour.

As Max Verstappen said: "It was just the tow (in) combination with the deployment. Of course, when you are within a second, you have the overtake mode as well, and around here that is powerful. So, you could not pull away."

The tow provided a significant lap time gain of well over 0.5secs a lap. That's why a car in front could not pull away from a car behind even if it was slightly quicker.

The overtake mode provides an extra 0.5MJ of electrical energy per lap for a car following another by under a second. So that's another factor preventing someone pulling away.

Then the batteries were running out before the end of the straights, so a car running in front in free air was suffering from that, too.

On top of that, Las Vegas is a smooth track which is light on tyres. So degradation was almost non-existent. That kept tyre temperatures under control and allowed people to push hard throughout.

If the tyres had shown their usual behaviour of surface overheating leading to sliding, there would have been less action as drivers would have found it much harder to follow each other closely. As is usually the case in F1.

Drivers have been pushing for years for tyres to behave in this way, but so far Pirelli has been unable or unwilling to provide them. It only happens occasionally in specific circumstances.

With a move to engines with a smaller electrical component in 2030, are F1 cars becoming less relevant to modern road cars? The argument was always that F1 is the test bed for future road car technology. - Paul

The current F1 engines, with a near 50-50 split between internal combustion and electrical power, were decided upon five or so years ago when the road-car market was slightly different to now.

The push for electrification is still there, and will continue. But consumer take-up of electric cars, especially in the performance market, has not been what manufacturers expected. Hence a slight shift in focus.

That said, it's not as if the current F1 engines are especially road relevant. With a few exceptions, road cars tend to be either full electric, plug-in hybrid, where the electrical element is much less than 50-50, or full internal combustion.

So there is an argument the new engines, with sustainable fuel and a hybrid element that is about 12-13% of total output normally or 25% when on overtake mode, are more relevant to road cars, not less, at least in their fundamental architecture.

The sustainable fuel is key here.

If this can get to a point where it is available to the mass market - and F1 is simplifying the demands on its make-up for the next ruleset in an attempt to help make the fuel more cost-effective for exactly that reason - it could be a game-changer for emissions in the road-car market.

That's because there will inevitably be a lot of legacy internal combustion vehicles on the road globally for many years, even after the new-car market becomes fully electric.

So if emissions from these can be reduced by 80%, as is the ballpark figure for the carbon-impact reduction of sustainable fuels versus fossil fuels, that's a major step.

Don't forget, too, the 2030 rules were the product of talks between the manufacturers and the FIA.

It was the manufacturers who pushed back against FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem's initial desire for a naturally aspirated engine with no hybrid element.

And they did that exactly because it made them more road relevant.

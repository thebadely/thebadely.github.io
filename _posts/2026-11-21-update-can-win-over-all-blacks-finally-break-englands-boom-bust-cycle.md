England beat New Zealand by record margin

We have been here before of course.

Twelve months ago, as today, England took a famous win over New Zealand.

After a run of victories against lesser opponents, it was the litmus test of their progress. They passed with flying colours and a comfortable scoreline.

It was a statement victory, a sign of things to come. England were building into world-beaters.

The similarities to today's 37-12 success are striking. Even the attendance figure - 81,953 - matches.

For England fans, burned by the dismal Six Nations that followed the 2025 result, there will be a shot of caution with the celebratory drinks tonight.

Steve Borthwick's four-year reign has been marked by booms and busts.

Five defeats in six Tests preceded the 2023 Rugby World Cup, when they came within a point of making the final.

They lost five straight matches in the back half of 2024, before embarking on 12-Test winning streak at the start of 2025.

The sunny uplands have too often turned out to be false dawns.

But, there are grounds to think this time, just maybe, is different.

For a start, it is a different England team, playing a different way.

When the team was named on Thursday, it was striking that England's backline contained none of the same starters as 12 months ago.

Steward, Roebuck, Lawrence, Dingwall, Feyi-Waboso, Ford and Mitchell read the teamsheet for the 2025 win over New Zealand.

Furbank, Murley, Slade, Atkinson, Joseph, Smith, and Van Poortvliet was its 2026 equivalent.

But for injury, Immanuel Feyi-Waboso and Alex Mitchell may have featured in both. Tommy Freeman likewise.

But the balance has shifted.

The class of 2026, with Fin Smith pulling strings, Henry Slade distributing to pace out wide, and George Furbank out the back and at first receiver, is a more fluid unit.

Seb Atkinson, up against Jordie Barrett, was critical to England's variety, able to combine full-throated midfield carries with deft hands.

In part, it is horses for courses.

Twelve months ago, kicking and chasing was in vogue. And England were setting the trend.

In their 2025 win over the All Blacks, England put boot to ball 35 times and passed it 104 times. A ratio of one to three.

This time around, the stat showed 29 kicks to 176 passes. The boot is being used half as much in relative terms.

The change has been forced to a degree. After a law tweak initially made the aerial route profitable last year, defences have found ways to counter, with retreating catchers and pockets of players posted to pick up scraps.

England had to find new dimensions. And they have.

Jack van Poortvliet and Ollie Chessum's tries were created by clever movement and midfield breaks by Cadan Murley and Will Joseph respectively.

From similar positions 12 months ago, England would have been tempted to take to the skies.

The current way of playing is less predictable, and more sustainable as a long-term tactic to threaten the game's superpowers at the backend of next year's Rugby World Cup.

The balance of power up front has tilted further in England's favour as well.

New Zealand were without talismanic captain Ardie Savea and fellow forwards Luke Jacobsen and Shannon Frizell. But still, England's heavy traffic ram-raided through the heart of the visiting pack a startling number of times.

Ben Earl particularly had a field day. The number eight has been a star performer throughout his team's fluctuating form and stamped his authority on this contest with a whopping 33 carries and 138 metres made.

Ellis Genge, Greg Fisilau and George Martin were abrasive off the bench, cementing England's advantage.

Defensively, England were tight.

Twelve months ago, New Zealand leapt out into a 12-0 lead with two tries in the first 17 minutes. They never looked like piling on points in a similar quickfire way this time around.

The visitors had only three line breaks compared to nine in 2025.

The dominance was near total. The All Blacks were all out of answers.

And England would have been out of sight sooner with a fewer errors in the first half.

"It was a very good performance and what's exciting is we left some points out there - I think we can be even better," Borthwick told BBC Radio 5 Live afterwards.

"No journey of a team is ever linear. There are always ups and downs that you work through. We continue to strive to improve.

"This is a team that has got enormous growth in it and I'm excited about the journey.

"If players keep the attitude they have to want to get better - and you can see that with the defence, because it was a brilliant defensive performance - when you have that level of commitment, what the players can achieve, I'm excited by the possibilities."

England's fans have certainly bought in.

Lewis Moody's appearance alongside the team for the haka, after presenting them their shirts the previous evening, was an invite to emotionally invest that was impossible to turn down.

The manner in which England's players, in the seconds before kick-off, sought Moody out to embrace him spoke of a bond.

The crowd responded to something special and real.

The All Blacks, famed aura faded, were reduced to minor roles.

Beaten last weekend by Wales, their long season is in severe danger of fizzling out.

If so, it is understandable. They have been set the most brutal of schedules.

In the aftermath of July's Nations Championship they have played nine Tests in less than three months. Four of those were away to South Africa, with the final meeting in the Greatest Rivalry series staged in Baltimore to add a few more air miles to their dizzying itinerary.

Battle-hardened can turn to over-cooked quickly. And with Ruben Love, Quinn Tupaea, Caleb Clarke and Leroy Carter out, their backline is as patched up as their pack.

After a superb start to his All Blacks reign, Dave Rennie would be forgiven for wanted to recuperate and regroup.

As it is, his side will be back at Allianz Stadium for a rematch against England in the Nations Championship third-place play-off next Saturday.

There are big Tests down the road for Borthwick's side. Ireland away in the opening round of the Six Nations in February looms large.

But they are measuring up well, compared to their own high watermarks, as well as the rest of the Test game.

Under the plans, which will not become law until at least next year, most new leasehold flats will eventually be banned and replaced with commonhold, where residents collectively own and manage their buildings.

Housing Minister Matthew Pennycook said the current leasehold system was "blighting lives" and was an "anachronism in the 21st century".

But the Residential Freehold Association says the plan is an "unprecedented abuse" of their rights, which will wipe billions from the value of pension funds, which have invested heavily in property.

The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, to be published on Thursday, is likely to contain further details on when the measures would come into force.

When she bought her flat, Malik assumed the system would work in the same way it does in Pakistan, where she grew up, and in most other countries, where buying a home also means owning a share of the land it sits on.

However, in England, most flats are sold on a leasehold basis. That gives the buyer the right to occupy and use a property for a set period of time, but the freeholder retains ultimate ownership of the building and the land.

Systems based on shared ownership and collective management - such as commonhold - are already standard in many parts of the world, including Australia, New Zealand, the US, Canada and much of continental Europe. They give residents ownership of their home and a stake in the building's common areas.

Scotland does not have leasehold tenures, while Northern Ireland has just a handful of leasehold properties.

The government says its reforms are intended to bring England and Wales more closely in line with other other countries around the world, which do not have leaseholds.

It comes as more than 1,000 leaseholders have contacted the BBC over the past two years with complaints about the current system.

Campaigners have welcomed many of the reforms, but questioned what happens to the millions of people who already own leasehold homes.

While the legislation is expected to reshape ownership for new developments, converting existing leasehold buildings into commonhold is far more complicated. And it has led to concerns that current leaseholders will be left behind.

Giles Grover, from the campaign group End Our Cladding Scandal, says there is a risk the proposed changes will create a two-tier system of those buying new homes and existing leaseholders.

He began campaigning on behalf of flat owners following the Grenfell Tower tragedy. He says: "It's so complicated, will the measures that are being introduced actually benefit everyone?"

The government says the bill will make it easier for existing leaseholders to convert to commonhold, abolish forfeiture - so people cannot lose their homes over relatively small debts - improve transparency around service charges and crack down on estate charges.

Pennycook says it will transform "the experience of homeownership for existing leaseholders by giving them greater rights and protections and providing them with easy and effective escape routes".

However, the issue about how existing leaseholders can move into commonhold and how freeholders should be compensated remains unresolved.

A murderer who became the first person tried after a change to the 800-year-old double jeopardy law has been recommended for release from prison.

William Dunlop, now 63, strangled Julie Hogg, 22, in Billingham, County Durham, in 1989 and hid her mutilated body behind a bath panel which was discovered by her mother more than two months later.

The Parole Board has recommended he be released after his fourth application to review his case. A panel had recommended Dunlop's transfer to open prison multiple times but three justice secretaries had intervened.

Justice Secretary Alex Norris has asked officials "to examine whether there are grounds to reconsider" the Parole Board's decision.

The 22-year-old's murder was "accompanied by the brutal sexual degradation of [her] body", the Old Bailey judge said at Dunlop's sentencing, which came in 2006 after Julie Hogg's family campaigned for the legal change so he could be charged with the same crime twice.

In response to the result, Hogg's son Kevin called the Parole Board "not fit for purpose".

He said: "You know them victim personal statements? They mean absolutely nothing â nothing whatsoever and that's been proven time and time again."

In its decision, the Parole Board concluded there was "no more than a minimal risk" Dunlop would go on to commit a further offence which would cause serious harm.

"After the time he has spent in custody and process of rehabilitation he has steadfastly pursued would not, in the judgment of this experienced panel, undermine the confidence of the public in the parole system," the decision read.

The panel said it was "mindful of the feelings" of Julie Hogg's mother Ann Ming, who believed Dunlop should never be released.

"However, if a prisoner presents as a person who, on the totality of the evidence available to the Panel, meets the statutory test for release then the Panel is obliged by law to conclude that his release must be directed."

The Parole Board acknowledged that the Secretary of State may still seek to review the decision, adding: "However, the Panel has had the advantage of seeing and hearing the witnesses, and, in particular, WD [William Dunlop]."

Kevin Hogg said: "When will they actually listen and take note of what the ministers said? Take note of what victims and the victims' families are saying?"

In his victim impact statement, Kevin Hogg recounted his childhood trauma of "hearing screaming and shouting in the home with the pungent smell of my mother's decomposing body" as his grandmother found it.

"I can say categorically that time isn't the great healer it's purported to be," he said.

"As the years pass, the pain of losing my mother to this murder remains acute."

Julie Hogg's family campaigned for 15 years to get the double jeopardy law changed

Dunlop, known as Billy, was tried twice for the murder but both juries failed to reach a verdict.

Three years later, while in jail for another crime, he confessed and admitted lying in court, boasting there was nothing anyone could do about it because of the double jeopardy rule in place at the time.

Hogg's family, including her mother, campaigned for 15 years to get the law changed and he became the first person to be tried under the new rules.

He admitted murder and was jailed for life with a minimum term of 17 years.

At the public parole hearing last month, Dunlop, who psychiatrists previously found had "psychopathic traits" and "severe personality problems", told the panel that he now empathises with the family after his son died last year.

"I get it now. I totally get it now," he said.

Per Dunlop's request to not be seen, he spoke with his back to the camera that was live-streamed from an unnamed prison to a room in London with Hogg's family inside.

The following day, Ming, 80, said she "wanted to scream" when hearing of his newfound empathy, adding: "I don't know how we kept it together."

At the hearing, Dunlop's prison offender manager said his ability to cope had improved.

She said she observed this last year as he navigated his son's death, then-justice secretary Shabana Mahmood's intervention to keep him in the prison, and the documenting of his case in ITV drama I Fought the Law.

A Ministry of Justice spokesman said: "Julie Hogg's murder was a horrific crime and our thoughts remain with her friends and family.

"The Lord Chancellor's first priority is keeping the public safe, which is why he has instructed officials to examine whether there are grounds to reconsider this decision."

The spokesman added that offenders released from life sentences were subject to "strict supervision and licence conditions for the rest of their lives" and would be recalled to custody immediately if they broke the rules.

Dunlop has served five years more than his minimum term for murder.

He was already imprisoned at the time of that sentence and has now been detained for about 28 years.

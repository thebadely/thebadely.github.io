'They made a mistake, not me' - Sir Billy Boston tribute

When Sir Billy Boston was knighted in 2025, it was a unique moment for rugby league.

For the first time, this rebel sport from the north of England had been recognised in such a manner; one of its heroes had been acknowledged for their contribution to history through the game.

Sir Billy was a fitting recipient of the honour and a world-class athlete, whose story deserved to be told.

You can tell the importance of someone by how many statues have been erected in tribute, and Sir Billy Boston wasn't lacking.

His name was chosen as one of just five to grace Wembley's monument to rugby league's Challenge Cup, while years after his departure north, his home city of Cardiff also ensured his legacy remained cast-iron strong as he formed part of a three-man tribute along with Clive Sullivan and Gus Risman.

Unsurprisingly, Boston is also captured in bronze in his adopted home of Wigan; the place where his legend really came to life.

Born and brought up in Cardiff's ethnically-eclectic Tiger Bay district, he learned his rugby skills on the streets and with the talent factory-esque Cardiff International Athletic Club. 

Like all Welsh lads he harboured dreams of playing for his local club Cardiff RFC and hopefully the national team.

Like others before and after for a time, Boston found those routes blocked, having to travel to Neath and Pontypridd to trial before his military service took him north.

It was here where the league scouts came calling, and Wigan persuaded Boston to turn professional in 1953, forever ending his union dreams in the time of 'sine die' penalties for liaising with league personnel.

The money was life changing. 

Â£3,000 could easily buy a house at that stage, while it proved money well spent as he took to the new code like a duck to water, scoring with his first touch in a Wigan A team game.

This prime specimen of Welsh breezeblock had everything; he was quick, he could step and as former player and BBC commentator Ray French put it - his fend was like "being hit in the face with shovel".

The sepia footage and slow shutter speeds of the cameras that captured his magic during his playing days simply cannot dull his brilliance. 

Whether it was bustling over for another try in cherry and white hoops at Wembley or crushing the Aussies at Sydney Cricket Ground, Boston was a blur of speed, footwork and power.

Having swiftly progressed to the first team at Central Park, he would go on to score 478 tries in 488 games for Wigan, and tales of his feats would be passed down through generations. There are very few down at the Brick Community Stadium these days who don't know of Sir Billy, young or old, his name carried a cache right until his last.

Great Britain gave him the international recognition denied to him by Wales, and he rewarded the selectors by racking up the tries, particularly against the Australians who would bear the brunt of his scoring ability.

He scored 36 tries alone on his maiden Great Britain tour, dazzling both Australia and New Zealand in 1954 under captain and fellow Welshman Dickie Williams.

Boston was a phenomenal rugby league player, but there is also a powerful message in how in post-war Britain a black athlete could become a hugely popular figure by communities adjusting to a new, more diverse world.

Others like Roy Francis beforehand, Johnny Freeman, and Colin Dixon, also became established names, but never quite sprinkled the same stardust as Sir Billy.

It paved the way for players such as Ellery Hanley and Martin Offiah to charm the public with their talents, while modern day talent such as Junior Nsemba and Kai Pearce-Paul continues to stand out in a sport which has broken down barriers for years.

Sir Billy had a unique unifying effect on a normally partisan and tribal rugby league community. 

He was revered naturally in Wigan, his adopted home, but you won't hear a boo or jeer at mention of Boston anywhere - not even at sworn rivals St Helens where they appreciate a good footballer when they see one.

Still a regular visitor to Wigan games right up to his passing, it was always a beautiful moment when the cameras found him, the crowd spotting him on the screen bursting into cheer, and the realisation creeping over his face in the shape of a smile.

There was a photo of him and his wife Lady Joan, who had died just a few weeks previously, on her seat with flowers at Wigan's game with Wakefield last week. There is a poignancy that Sir Billy departed soon after her.

His seat will always be reserved at Wigan, and he will be dearly missed but certainly not forgotten.

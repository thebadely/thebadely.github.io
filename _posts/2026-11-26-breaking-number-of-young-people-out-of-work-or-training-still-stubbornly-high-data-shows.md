It has been recently reported that There were just under one million young people aged 16 to 24 not in employment, education or training (Neet) in the UK in the three months to September, according to new data.

Thursday's figures from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) recorded 959,000 people who were Neet in that time, a very slight increase on the same period a year ago, though the number of women who were Neet was at its highest in a decade.

The data comes a week after a major review by former government minister Alan Milburn set out a series of recommendations to bring the numbers down.

Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden said the government was "fundamentally reforming how we support young people".

Thursday's figures from the ONS , externalsuggest there were 17,000 more young people who were not earning or learning this summer compared to the same period last year.

The overall number of 959,000 young people who were Neet this summer represents 12.7% - about one in eight - of the whole UK population aged 16 to 24. 

The rate has dropped slightly, by 0.3 percentage points, compared to earlier in the year, with 22,000 fewer young people recorded as Neet between July and September than in April to June. 

The Neets data tends to fluctuate across the year with seasonal hiring patterns and academic summer holidays.

The changes in the latest figures are also within the margin of error of the Labour Force Survey which the stats are based on.

In his long-awaited report into youth employment, published earlier this month, Milburn set out proposals aimed at bringing the Neet rate down to 5% over the next decade.

If successful, that would put the UK in line with some of the lowest rates in Europe. Currently, only Romania has a higher Neet rate than the UK.

Milburn called for a "whole system reset" across education, health and welfare to achieve that. It would include an overhaul of the benefits system for under-25s, new legal rights to work and training opportunities and plans to expand college places and double the number of young people taking up apprenticeships.

The government has broadly accepted Milburn's proposals, but is yet to respond to all the plans in detail.

In the data, Neets can be categorised as either unemployed - which means they are actively seeking work - or economically inactive - meaning they are not seeking work, which could be because of illness or caring responsibilities. 

Most young people who are Neet fall into the economically inactive category, with  596,000 young people economically inactive in the latest stats, compared to 363,000 who were unemployed.

Overall, there are still more young men who are Neet (483,000) but the number of young women who are Neet is at its highest level in a decade, at 476,000.   

Ben Harrison, from the Work Foundation at Lancaster University, says the higher number of women out of work could be partly down to the loss of a "significant" number of jobs in retail, a sector in which "historically, many young women have taken their first steps into work". 

Responding to the latest Neets figures, McFadden said "every one" of the young people out of education, employment or training "deserves better". 

"That's why we're fundamentally reforming how we support young people to grow up in this country," he said.

He said the government was "building a new settlement that invests in young people's success instead of paying for failure" in the wake of the Milburn review.

"That includes a Jobs Guarantee offering eligible young people on universal credit a fully funded six-month job, as well as thousands more work experience placements, and youth hubs bringing employment, health, and skills support under one roof."

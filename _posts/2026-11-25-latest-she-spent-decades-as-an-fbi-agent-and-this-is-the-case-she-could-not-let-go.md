Here’s what you need to know: Jane Collins spent 30 years with the FBI - but no case stuck with her quite like the case of Nexus, Michael Donovan and Richard Moore

Jane Collins had spent 30 years as an FBI agent, but there was one case that haunted her long after retirement.

Michael Donovan was a fraudster, former minister and multimillionaire she had pursued for years, and even in her retirement she could not escape him.

He was regularly featured in the news and on the radio, appearing as a spokesperson for a number of causes, from migrant rights to anti-racism and justice reform.

He and his husband, Richard Moore, lived about an hour's drive from her, in Augusta County, Virginia, on the edge of the Appalachian Mountains.

Together they owned a business, Libre by Nexus, which had made more than $200m (Â£152m) from migrants seeking release from US immigrant detention centres - but it was a scam, government regulators said.

To Collins, they were the ones who got away.

She had investigated Nexus from 2016 until her retirement from the FBI in 2021. But the only federal criminal charges either faced - after almost six years of her investigation - was a tax charge against Moore.

So when Collins left the bureau, there were still a few strands left of her investigation that she could not let go - like the case of Zach Cruz, the brother of the man who killed 17 people in Parkland, Florida.

They had fronted his $500,000 bail bond, personally picked him up from jail, given him somewhere to stay, and made media appearances on his behalf.

Investigators later found they also took $400,000 from him, proceeds from his late mother's life insurance. Last month, the pair were found guilty of stealing it - with Collins playing a key role as a witness during the trial.

Collins told the BBC in her only interview about the case that she saw it as her final chance to face them in court.

 "It means something when people are held to account," she said.

Collins says she was still a young girl when she first thought about joining the FBI.

"I've always been very interested in justice, making things right, and I think all of those things just fell into place," the 64-year-old recalls from her home in Charlottesville, Virginia, where a picture of her late golden retriever named Yogi hangs on the wall and a vintage car is parked in the driveway.

She was accepted to Quantico, the FBI training academy, soon after finishing university, and began to climb the ladder at the bureau, conducting surveillance on suspected Russian spies and crack dealers. In the late 1990s, she began working more on white-collar crimes, which really excited her - "much to my surprise," she adds. What she liked about these cases was the paper trail, and the way they presented themselves as puzzles to solve.

"There's a lot more in those cases that are concrete. As we say, documents don't lie," she says. "It was more challenging."

It also changed her perspective on crime, and victims. Although there was no trail of blood, there was an undeniable ripple from the fraud to the people and families hurt by it.

"People getting defrauded of money, some people their life savings," she explains. "There's a lot of psychological issues that go with that for a victim. They have been duped, so they're very hard on themselves."

Weeks after his older brother gunned down 14 students and three staff members at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Parkland,  Zachary Cruz went to the campus to "reflect on the school shooting and soak it all in".

But when police found him there skateboarding, they arrested the then 18-year-old, charged him with trespassing and put him in jail. On the heels of his brother's massacre, a judge set his bail at $500,000.

His friend called Nexus, looking for help to get him out. But Donovan and Moore did more than that - not only did they pay his bail, but they picked Cruz up from jail and took him to a hotel room.

Cruz later told the court that when he met them for the first time, it felt "like a movie".

During dinner that first night, the couple asked Cruz if he wanted to move to Virginia and work for Nexus.

The Florida judge overseeing his probation called it a "wonderful opportunity".

Soon he was living with them, and sharing in their media spotlight. Donovan and Moore accompanied him on interviews where the young man spoke about trying to understand his brother's crimes.

The couple even helped produce a short-lived online reality show called "Being Zach Cruz", which chronicled what it's like to be the brother of a famous killer. 

"It was not ordinary," he later said.

According to Moore and Donovan, in an interview with the BBC in 2022, Cruz had become part of their family. They called him their "son" and said they had spent lavishly on his pursuit of a skateboarding career.

But they were deeply in debt, fighting off creditors - and the IRS. And through it all, Collins was doggedly on their tail.

Zach Cruz, left, was taken in by Michael Donovan, centre, and Richard Moore, right, after his brother killed 17 people in Parkland, Florida

Donovan and Moore had spent their wealth extravagantly - a fleet of luxury vehicles, a $500,000 wedding and a million-dollar self-published book titled Not Free America: What Your Government Doesn't Want You to Know.

But they owed millions to several state insurance boards, which had fined them for operating an unlicensed bond company. They owed a former business partner, RLI Insurance, millions for their failure to pay back bail that was lost when clients skipped court. And Moore owed more than $3m to the IRS.

Prosecutors alleged it was this tax debt that spurred Moore and Donovan to dupe Cruz out of his inheritance. Donovan declined an interview, through his attorney.

Collins initially focused her investigation on Libre by Nexus, and whether the company had broken any laws.

But her case took a turn when she received a tip that Donovan and Moore may be personally exploiting Cruz.

Bank records showed that $428,000 - the insurance money Cruz was entitled to after his mother died - was transferred into a joint account held by Cruz and Moore, and then into Moore's personal account, in 2019, according to the county's search warrant.

Collins tried to interview Cruz, who was still living with Moore and Donovan.

Driving up to their house one evening, Collins says she saw an armed security guard stationed outside, while barricades blocked the entrance to the front door and driveway.

"People can't easily come and go," she thought to herself. Cruz never came to the door.

Since 2016, Collins had been working on the case in conjunction with the Internal Revenue Service and Homeland Security Investigations, which oversees immigration law. The agencies were investigating whether Nexus defrauded people and brought migrants illegally to work for their company, as well as the allegations involving Cruz's insurance money and tax fraud.

But by the time she was due to retire, the federal prosecutor in charge of the case had narrowed the focus to just tax fraud, which he passed onto another prosecutor.

Collins was disappointed.

Ultimately, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau - a federal agency with the power to bring civil, but not criminal, cases to court - took legal action against Nexus, leading to an $811m judgement against the company.

The agency provides a much-needed service, helping to pursue corporations when other avenues have failed, said Ted Mermin, executive director of the Berkeley Center for Consumer Law & Economic Justice.

To bring a criminal case, "you have to show that beyond a shadow of a doubt they violated the law", he said.

A civil case, on the other hand, only needs to show that it is more likely than not that they violated the law.

'My brother, the Parkland gunman'

Meanwhile, Collins was running up against the clock: the FBI has a mandatory retirement age of 57, and she had already been extended. She retired in the summer of 2021 at the age of 59.

A few months later, Cruz, Donovan and Moore sued her, alleging she had "conspired to create a fake 'joint investigation'" with a local agency, and that her career was "cut short after she violated department policies and/or laws in connection with her rogue investigation".

The bureau's attorney repudiated those claims, and a federal judge dismissed the case. The lawsuit did little to dissuade Collins, who brushed it off as "frivolous".

"I was not intimidated by them," she told the BBC. Through his attorney, Donovan declined the BBC's request for comment. Moore, who is serving a seven-year prison sentence for filing a fraudulent Nexus tax return, did not respond to the BBC's request for comment.

Before she left the bureau, Collins sat down with her bosses and the agent assigned to take the case over from her, she said. She still had hope that it would be prosecuted.

But more than a year went by, and there were no charges.

It seemed, at least for a while, that her case was finished. Then, in October 2022, local police and Virginia State Police stormed Moore and Donovan's home in Fishersville, and their offices in Verona.

It was not the feds, as Collins had hoped. But this was still a chance at holding the men to account.

They seized laptops, mobile phones, body cameras and thumb drives. Donovan and Moore were arrested and charged, and then released on bail.

Evidence of the raid was still clear in the shattered glass of the front door, when the BBC went to Nexus offices to interview the couple several days after police stormed the property.

In the interview, Donovan and Moore said they had not taken advantage of Cruz and had done nothing wrong.

Zach, who was still living with them at the time, told the BBC he had spent all his inheritance willingly. But he is no longer living with the couple, and appeared as a witness for the prosecution during the trial.

After Collins retired from the bureau in 2021, she began working for a state agency. She plans to retire for good in a few years, when she hopes to spend more time with her husband and three children and travel around the world.

Looking back, she thinks of this chapter at the end of her career in federal law enforcement as discouraging. She had wanted to declare "case closed" years ago.

"I enjoyed my career immensely. I wouldn't trade it. I felt like I made a big difference in a lot of ways," she says. "But this case, it caused me a lot of frustration."

Still, she says, she feels grateful that someone did take this case on.

When she heard the jury had convicted them on all counts, she thought: "Finally, justice".

"It had been a long time coming," she said. "But we needed to wait until the house of cards fell down."

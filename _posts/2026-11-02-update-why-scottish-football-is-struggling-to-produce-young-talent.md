Calum Ferguson achieved what thousands of young footballers dream of when he played for his boyhood club, Inverness Caledonian Thistle, in the Scottish Premiership.

Yet the 31-year-old looks back on his career as proof of a difficult truth about youth development in Scotland.

Ferguson graduated from Caley Thistle's academy at 18 but played just two games for the first team before moving to lower league clubs and then to Canada and New Zealand to try to get top-flight football.

Now a development officer with Alba Football Alliance, he believes Scottish football continues to sell young players and their families the promise of a pathway from the academies to the first team, which for most will never be a reality.

"Parents believe their boy, or their girl, will get to the first team - that they will be a star in football," he told BBC Scotland's Eorpa programme.

"That is not the truth at all.

"You just fall away from the game and your life changes overnight."

Calum now works as a development officer trying to bring through new talent

His comments come as Scottish football wrestles with a growing concern over the number of homegrown players who are making it to the top of the game.

In September, all 42 SPFL clubs gathered in St Andrews for a summit to discuss why so few young Scottish players are making the grade at elite level.

Data presented to the clubs showed that Scottish players under the age of 21 made up just 4.1% of minutes played in the Scottish Premiership.

By comparison, in the Danish Superliga, a league of the same size, the figure was 15.1%.

In the first round of fixtures in the Scottish Premiership this season, 28% of the starters were born in Scotland.

A stark contrast to similar sized Norway, where homegrown players made up 72% of the starting 11s on their opening weekend.

Nicklas Fuglestad regularly plays for Viking FK in Norway

This is because Norway has strict quotas.

Its league rules state that a club's full squad must include 16 homegrown players and a matchday squad can have a maximum of nine foreign players.

Nicklas Fuglestad is 20, and regularly plays for Viking FK, based in the Norwegian city of Stavanger. He believes quotas are good for the game.

"Everyone I know is very happy with how the Norwegian football league operates," he says.

"It makes it a bit easier for a young player to move up from the academy and make the step into A-league football."

Viking turned their focus to developing academy talent nearly a decade ago, after they were relegated to the second division.

After finishing champions last year, they have made it to the league stage of the Champions League, where they recently started eight Norwegians in their line-up, four of whom had been in their academy.

Another three from their academy came off the bench.

That talent system is Landslagskollen - or the National Team School (NTS) - that was established in 2013, following the country's failure to qualify for Euro 2012.

It's neither an academy nor a centralised school, but a national development structure connecting grassroots clubs, districts, top clubs and the federation.

Eleven of the 15 players who played in Norway's 2-1 win over Brazil in the round of 16 at this year's World Cup were part of the NTS pathway from under-15 or under-16s.

At the meeting in St Andrews in September, the Scottish FA proposed quotas, as well as financial incentives, in an attempt to increase game time for young Scottish players.

The governing body put forward three suggestions:

Two under-21 Scotland-eligible players to start in all Scottish Cup matches

Two under-21 Scotland-eligible players to start in all Premier Sports Cup matches

Clubs should field Scotland-eligible Under-21 players for an average of 60 minutes in each league game across all four SPFL divisions (6% of available minutes).

Two votes were to take place on the proposals. 

The first, on the issue of Scottish Cup rule adoption, should have happened in October at the SFA's AGM, but did not take place.

The SFA now says it is expected to happen in mid-December.

 The second vote is due to take place at the SPFL's annual meeting this month.

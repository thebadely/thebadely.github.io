Let's dive into the details: Andoni Iraola signed a two-year contract with Liverpool

Winning is important - but for many fans, how a team plays in the hunt for wins is almost as significant.

When results don't come, a style that fans enjoy watching often buys a manager more time than a pragmatist might be afforded.

Andoni Iraola replaced Arne Slot as Liverpool head coach a season after the latter won the Premier League with the Reds.

Many believe the Spanish coach's style is more aligned with the 'heavy metal' style of football Anfield had come to love under Jurgen Klopp.

So how have Liverpool fared in their pre-season games so far under Iraola?

Liverpool have beaten Sunderland and Wrexham in pre-season, but they threw away two-goal leads in defeats by both Leeds and Monaco.

They averaged 60.6% possession over this small sample size - which is a 12.9% jump on Bournemouth's average possession under Iraola in the league last season.

With teams more likely to sit deep against Liverpool, having lots of possession is a newer challenge Iraola has to tackle.

And he has looked to do this while sticking to the intense and chaotic style that typified his previous sides.

Since joining Liverpool, many of Iraola's tactics in possession look as they did last season.

Liverpool have looked to build up short at times with their goalkeeper, four defenders and one or two central midfielders deep.

Dominik Szoboszlai and Trey Nyoni, Liverpool's preferred deeper midfield duo in pre-season, drop to show for the ball, bouncing it towards wide players in space.

Iraola's sides have become known for fast and vertical counter-attacks but, with Liverpool looking to hold the ball more deliberately, these short passes in build-up are designed to entice opposition pressure before accelerating play.

Once a team have committed players to pressing Liverpool, Iraola's men look for forward passes readily - almost manufacturing their own counter-attacks.

If a team press immediately, Liverpool have been willing to skip short build-up play and go long from keepers Alisson or Giorgi Mamardashvili early.

By playing directly, even if Liverpool lose the initial duel, they are well placed to pounce upon second balls. They then look to attack the spaces that open up centrally against disorganised defences.

Here we can see Liverpool's wide back four with Ryan Gravenberch showing for the ball in front of them in build-up. With Leeds applying pressure from the start, Mamardashvili plays the ball long into the space

Many of Iraola's attacking principles resemble those of one of his long-time managerial inspirations, the former Leeds United boss Marcelo Bielsa.

The Argentine coached Iraola at Athletic Club between 2011 and 2013 and, speaking to Sky Sports in 2023, the Liverpool boss acknowledged this influence.

"I use a lot of exercises from Marcelo that I learned from him, especially with the ball," Iraola said.

"Offensively, his teams are very dynamic, willing to make all the runs to the space, he is ready to accept this kind of disorder offensively."

Like Bielsa's sides, Iraola already has the Reds playing in a busy manner. When attacking, it is rare to see Liverpool's players filling specific areas in a rigid style.

Another tactic Bielsa leans on is the use of wide pairings or trios to break teams down. Iraola experienced this at first hand when playing as a right-back for him.

The full-back and winger would regularly combine on the outside, but the relationship was never as simple as one holding the width and the other supporting underneath statically.

Bielsa, like Iraola now, encouraged players to change their positions in relation to each other.

The winger could come inside, opening the outside lane for the full-back, while a midfielder would move towards the pair to create a third passing option.

On other occasions, the full-back could move inside himself, allowing the winger to remain high and wide.

Liverpool are already showing similar patterns under Iraola, especially on the left, where full-backs have pushed into the final third alongside Rio Ngumoha or Cody Gakpo on the left wing.

Florian Wirtz, playing primarily as Liverpool's number 10 under Iraola, has drifted to that flank to provide a third option capable of playing centrally, deep or out wide.

The rotations of these wide triangles make it hard for opponents to know who they should be picking up.

By simplifying the attacking game into two-v-two or three-v-three situations, Liverpool are hoping to have a repeatable and dynamic solution, resulting in crosses from dangerous positions close to goal.

Rio Ngumoha finds himself alone against four Leeds defenders.

Ngumoha recycles the ball to left-back Milos Kerkez. At this point, Florian Wirtz moves over to provide support on the left flank, creating a wide triangle. This turns the match into a three-against-three mini-game. 

Kerkez passes the ball to Ngumoha before making a run in-behind which drags defenders away from the ball. Wirtz's movement deep into the area the left-back had vacated similarly draws players away from the ball, giving Ngumoha a one-against-one in space.

As exciting as this style of play can appear, it does leave Liverpool very top-heavy.

In the opening games, we have seen both full-backs join the attack alongside two of the midfield three and the front three.

This can result in situations that leave only the two centre-backs and one holding midfielder positioned behind the ball, leaving Liverpool vulnerable to counter-attacks.

Iraola has spoken about the need for more control while retaining the chaos that makes his team's attacks so effective, so he may decide to leave additional players behind the ball as the season progresses.

However, based off his previous principles and his managerial references, this feels less likely - with the Basque coach often encouraging six or seven players to push forward, leaving three or four players back when in attack.

The hope is to stop counter-attacks being conceded at source through intense counter-presses, something that is helped by attacking down the sides and with extra bodies nearby to apply pressure.

This is easier said than done at this early stage.

Indeed, Iraola reflected on the loss to Monaco, saying his side "can't sustain the level we want, we have work to do", referring to the dropped leads in second halves, caused by a lack of depth, a drop-off in physicality, and a press that hasn't yet been perfected.

When Liverpool lost the ball against Sunderland in midfield, they only had three players behind the ball with the remaining seven outfielders attacking high up the pitch. This left space for Sunderland to exploit in the lead up to their first goal

Throughout pre-season, we have also seen Iraola's trademark high press. What stands out is the roles Liverpool's players are taking up and what that tells us about how he wants his side to defend.

Szoboszlai played across the front line, midfield and even at right-back last season, but Iraola has mainly used him as the second central midfielder - a crucial role defensively.

Liverpool have often pressed in a 4-4-2 diamond shape, initially encouraging opponents to play short before launching more aggressive pressing sequences, often funnelling the ball centrally before trying to win it back.

One principle carried over from Iraola's Bournemouth side is looking to keep a spare defender against the opposition attack. If an opponent leaves three forwards high, Liverpool will commonly opt to keep four defenders back.

That leaves one fewer player higher up the pitch, so Liverpool have to compensate through intensity and intelligent covering.

When one player leaves his opponent to press somebody else, team-mates behind him have to recognise the movement and cover the player left free.

This makes Szoboszlai's midfield role particularly important. When he jumps forward to press, the holding midfielder and defensive line have to react behind him or gaps can quickly appear.

"He is very good," the Hungary captain has said of his new manager. "The intensity we need is here now, what we have been missing is back."

Szoboszlai's physicality allows him to cover the ground needed to make Liverpool's press feel numerically equal, while his judgement of when to step forward has helped the Reds start to implement Iraola's defensive ideas quickly.

Four pre-season games is a very small sample, but Iraola's stamp on Liverpool is already clear to see, featuring many of the same instructions that defined his Bournemouth side.

Those ideas have been proven to work - but how he balances the chaos and speed we've come to know his sides for, with a squad lacking depth and who are expected to dominate the ball for longer periods, is likely to shape his first season.

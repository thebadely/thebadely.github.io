It has been recently reported that Northern Ireland has become the first part of the UK to introduce new rules for new drivers in a bid to reduce fatal road crashes involving young people.

Graduated driver licensing (GDL), for learner and newly qualified drivers, applies to all new drivers but are particularly aimed at 17 to 23-year-olds.

Among the new rules is that learners must spend at least six months getting driving experience before they can take a practical test. 

Newly qualified drivers under 24 will also face restrictions on carrying young passengers at night. 

The rule changes were announced in January last year and will see the current speed limit of 45mph for restricted drivers removed.

Drivers who do not follow the restrictions could receive three penalty points or a fine of up to Â£1,000.

Versions of GDL exist in countries including Australia, the US and Republic of Ireland. Campaigners have called for them to be introduced across the UK.

Police say it will "save lives" but there has been a mixed reaction from young drivers, with some in favour while others, from rural backgrounds, feel frustrated.

However a driving instructor described it as a "terrific" step.

According to official figures, 17 to 23-year-old drivers were responsible for 24% of all collisions, external in Northern Ireland in 2024, despite only accounting for 8% of license holders. 

In the same year there were 251 people killed or seriously injured in collisions involving a young driver, and in 65% of those, the young driver was deemed to be at fault. 

In January, the government announced that learner drivers in England and Wales could face a minimum period of up to six months between sitting their theory and practical tests.

However, it stopped short of introducing GDLs, which motoring organisation the AA described as "a missed opportunity".

 Earlier this year Scottish ministers called on the government to grant them powers to put their own restrictions on new young drivers.

The UK government said it wanted to keep young people safe but had decided not to introduce "blanket restrictions" on new drivers.

For 16-year-old Tommy from Omagh, driving is a "big factor" in his life. 

He has been learning the basics, driving with his dad in fields and private yards for three years already. 

However he thinks the new rules will not suit young people who live in rural areas, who don't have access to public transport and rely heavily on lifts. 

"There's hardly any transport links out here so you sort of have to rely on people giving me lifts," he said. 

"We're about five miles out from the nearest transport hub and it's just hard to organise lifts even to get to work or college."

Tommy has his own car ready to go and considers himself a confident driver who would pass his test first time, but said the time he has to wait to drive it has now been "extended". 

"It is a bit gutting, because I was so excited until they announced the new rules coming in, it's hard to believe how big of a change it is going to be," he added. 

Adam says safety is important but, coming from a rural area, also feels frustrated 

While he recognises that something needs to be done to address the number of deaths on the roads there is a level of frustration for Adam, who is also from Omagh, about the minimum six-month delay to him getting behind the wheel.

"I'm really eager to get out on the road, especially since I've been hassling my parents. I go to the gym quite frequently, maybe four days a week, and it's straight after tech. 

"If I can get up off my own two feet, get into my own car, and go to my gym by myself, it's nobody's problem but mine.

"All I used to talk about in high school was 'oh, what car are you getting', 'where are we going on a road trip', and now there's just more complications, it's just bummed everybody out," he added. 

Moss thinks it's "not that big of a deal" to have to wait six months 

Moss is already learning to drive, so the new rules will not affect him, but he thinks it is a positive move because "you'll get a lot more confidence and a lot more experiences in different kinds of situations which will be really useful".

"Starting to drive by yourself can be a scary thing", so "if it's to help with safety I think its worth it," he added. 

He recognises that some people are annoyed about the new rules, particularly the six-month wait, but thinks "in the long run, does it really matter if you get your test six months later? It's not that big of a deal".

The Police Service of Northern Ireland's (PSNI's) road safety lead Ch Supt Sam Donaldson said that in Northern Ireland "more people per head of population are involved in collisions, basic collisions, serious injury collisions, and sadly, fatal collisions, than any other part of the UK".

He has "no doubt" that it will save lives. 

"Sadly the reality is that young and inexperienced drivers are disproportionately involved in more collisions.

"Some are minor, some are serious, and some sadly are result in fatalities as well." 

Veronica Weir has 26 years experience as a driving instructor

Veronica Weir, who has 26 years' experience as a driving instructor thinks the law change is "terrific" and that young drivers can be irresponsible. 

"It's been a long time coming, about 15 years in the pipeline.

"Kids today just think it's a video game, and if they crash, there's no consequences, there's no ripple effect, they'll just brush themselves off and drive off again," she said. 

She said that dangerous driving can affect "their family, their insurance and other parts of the community".

The first new practical tests will take place on 1 April 2027.

Let's dive into the details: Ulrich Siegmund, the AfD's leader in Saxony-Anhalt, says the result is a "clear mandate to govern"

The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) is "very, very close" to taking power, its deputy parliamentary leader has told the BBC, after a historic victory in elections for the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt.  

Sunday's preliminary results show it fell three seats short of an outright majority, with 43.8% of the vote. It is now seeking support from other parties or individuals in order to govern.

While most German political parties refuse to work with the AfD, Beatrix von Storch called for its opponents to enter talks and said: "You cannot say I do not accept the vote of the people". 

No far-right party has controlled a German state since World War Two, and governing at state level would mean powers over policing and local education.

Von Storch said if parties refused to work with the AfD, "then we might end up in severe chaos and maybe we have a new election".

Her comments were echoed by AfD's leader in Saxony-Anhalt, Ulrich Siegmund, who told a news conference on Monday that the party had won a "clear mandate to govern".

Saxony-Anhalt has a small population of 2.1 million and the AfD in the state is assessed by domestic intelligence as "right-wing extremist", a classification rejected by Siegmund, 35.

Siegmund said discussions with other parties were initiated on Sunday night and could potentially take days or weeks to conclude. 

"We reach out to everybody first, we will have consultations and then we'll see whether [there are] individual parliamentary groups or MPs who would be willing to help us," he added.

The AfD is currently heading for 39 seats in the 83-seat state parliament instead of the 42 needed for an outright majority, although the result has not yet been finalised.

Von Storch told the BBC the AfD was talking to individuals within the conservative CDU group - the largest party in both the state's outgoing coalition and Germany's government - because they were "very, very unhappy with their own party".

In Saxony-Anhalt, the CDU won 17.2% of the vote, according to preliminary results.

One idea being floated is whether there could be informal deal whereby a small party - the BSW - could give their tacit support to allow the AfD to form a minority government.

The left-wing populist BSW, which is set to win five seats, is also tough on migration and backs importing Russian gas.

The AfD has signalled an openness to talk with other parties but all - apart from the BSW - refuse to co-operate with the far-right.

The AfD leads the opinion polls nationally, ahead of the ruling conservatives of Chancellor Friedrich Merz and the Social Democrats, but their heartland lies in the eastern states. 

They also won the 2024 state election in neighbouring Thuringia, but failed to secure enough seats to win power.

Among the AfD's policies is a highly controversial plan for "remigration", which is widely understood to mean mass deportations of migrants, although the party says it refers to deporting criminals and those in the country illegally. 

Commentators and political opponents called AfD's victory a watershed and break with the past. 

According to German newspaper Bild, Chancellor Friedrich Merz told a private CDU meeting on Monday that the Saxony-Anhalt result marked a "profound turning point for the party" and had left it shaken "to its core".

"This isn't just about Saxony-Anhalt," Siegmund told supporters overnight. "It's a signal for the whole of Germany - a self-confident signal."

On Sunday, US President Donald Trump posted a screenshot of the state's projected results on his Truth Social platform. Russian presidential envoy Kirill Dmitriev then took a screengrab of Trump's post and praised a "historic win for the pragmatic AfD".

Dmitriev had earlier praised the Russia-friendly AfD as "the party of hope and reason, co-operation truth and hope for Germany". Ahead of Germany's federal election last year, US Vice-President JD Vance endorsed the party, in a move criticised in Germany as political interference.

Polish centre-right Prime Minister Donald Tusk saw the result very differently, saying "in Poland only idiots and traitors can rejoice at the triumph of the AfD".

Asked about those who had expressed fears over the far-right party gaining power in the state, deputy parliamentary leader Beatrix von Storch told the BBC that its supporters were afraid of "losing our economy, they are afraid of losing our inner security".

She said conservative voters were turning to alternative political parties because they were not being listened to".

She added that the party's detractors should: "Wake up and realise what happened in several other European states... The conservative parties will disappear from the landscape as it happened in Italy, as it's about to happen in the UK, as it might also happen in France".

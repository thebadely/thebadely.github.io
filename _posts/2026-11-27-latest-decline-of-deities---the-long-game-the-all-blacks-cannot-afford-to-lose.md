For decades, the All Blacks have defied gravity.

Despite a population, player pool and bank balance smaller than most of their rivals, New Zealand's rugby team have become sporting shorthand for success.

Deservedly so. No other team comes close to them.

England beat the All Blacks this year and last. They play them again on Saturday aiming for a hat-trick.

But, England would need that winning streak to continue until around 2050 just to level up a lop-sided historical head-to-head.

New Zealand have won 36 times to England's 10 in their 121-year rivalry.

For every other nation in Test rugby, the story is the same. New Zealand have the superior record against them all.

A section on the All Blacks' website, titled Rivalries,, external opens by listing South Africa's 40% winning record in their meetings. And the numbers only drop from there.

But New Zealand's grip on the global game appears looser now than ever.

If they lose to England on Saturday, it may not much sway the historical balance between the two teams.

But it would be a sixth defeat in a calendar year, a total matched only once before in the All Blacks past, back in 1949 when a split squad played two Test series in Australia and apartheid South Africa at the same time.

New Zealand's win percentage in 2026 is 58.3%, their third lowest for any year since the start of professionalism.

This campaign, marked by an exhausting schedule and four Tests away to the world champion Springboks, is in keeping with a recent trend.

Since the end of the 2010s - a period in which an all-time great team captured back-to-back world titles - New Zealand have come off the boil.

The 2020s are on course to be their least successful decade since the 1970s.

This current crop of All Blacks are suffering not just by comparison to the previous generation, but when set against every other modern predecessor.

One reason may be a source of strength turning against them.

The All Blacks' culture of promoting within and establishing coaching dynasties was seen as key when they were winning.

A thread ran through the Graham Henry, Steve Hansen and Ian Foster succession in the 2010s glory days. The latter two served under their predecessor and the first two earned their Test stripes with Wales, before taking the top job. 

All were schooled as coaches in New Zealand and steeped in the country's rugby traditions.

The in-house production line of leaders ensured continuity and protected a team ethos that has been admired and written about everywhere.

But, in a modern world in which opposition tactics are quickly picked apart and best practice spreads fast, has adherence to tradition held back the team?

New Zealand's rivals have all hired coaching talent from outside.

Rassie Erasmus, South African to the core, may front the Springbok coaching operation, but behind him Irishmen Jerry Flannery and Felix Jones and Tony Brown, a former All Black himself, bring specialist knowledge to different areas.

France, famously patriotic in their hiring, benefited from Englishman Shaun Edwards and South African Vlok Cilliers' work behind the scenes in recent years.

England, Ireland, Scotland and Wales all have or have had head coaches from other countries.

Dave Rennie, who has coached Australia, Glasgow Warriors and Japanese side Kobe Steelers previously, became the first person of Pasifika heritage to lead the All Blacks when he took on the job in March.

He has brought a Scottish contingent to mix up his backroom, with attack coach Mike Blair the most high-profile.

But his regime is still only eight months old. Other issues predate it.

The All Black brand is valuable. It has been licensed to sell watches, healthcare and information technology.

The team are still a powerful global draw.

They are among the keenest Test nations to play matches overseas, where they can fill stadiums bigger than Auckland's 50,000-capacity Eden Park and make more money.

A globe-trotting 2026 itinerary has filled coffers, but emptied players.

Still though costs are rising faster than their income.

In turn, that is compromising the All Blacks' ability to keep their centrally contracted stars at home.

As in England, playing for a domestic team is a requirement to play internationally.

Thirty-five-year-old playmaker Beauden Barrett's proposed move to Bristol Bears and out of eligibility at the end of the 2027 Rugby World Cup attracted headlines, but New Zealand are more concerned by other departures.

Back-row star Wallace Sititi, 24, is rumoured to have agreed a move to Stade Francais, while Luke Jacobson, 29, has already announced his own switch to fellow French side Bayonne.

There is another angle to the issue that cuts against the All Blacks.

Those who do stay and remain eligible are competing in a weakened Super Rugby tournament.

South African sides left in 2020, throwing their lot in with the United Rugby Championship and the European club scene.

With only Australian sides and the Fijian Drua to compete against, Kiwi provincial sides' fixture lists are a significant step down from international rugby.

It is far from ideal preparation for the Test arena.

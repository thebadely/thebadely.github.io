The prime minister has said he wants to "move quickly" on implementing recommendations in Alan Milburn's report on work and young people. 

Speaking to BBC Breakfast, Andy Burnham said: "This isn't about waiting forever and a long-off-promised reform, I want to get stuck into it right now."

Milburn's report, external urged more job support for under-25s, a shake-up of the benefits system, early intervention in schools and tax cuts for businesses hiring young people.

The government said it backed the review's principles and is expected to set out which proposals it will take on shortly.

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said there was no need for "costly government schemes to fix Labour's own mistakes".

"We need to stop making it so expensive and risky to hire young people," she added.

Last year, the government asked Milburn, a former Labour minister, to do a review into the rising number of young people not in education, employment or training (Neet), which has now reached almost one million. 

Burnham praised the report as a "landmark moment" adding: "This is not just a benefits reform, it's a whole society change he is calling for." 

He said he wanted to get the benefit bill down adding: "No one can be comfortable with the idea of hundreds of thousands of young people on benefits - nobody, not me, not anyone."

However, he added that it required "more than the crude cuts the other parties are offering".

"All of my experience says a 'names not numbers' approach that looks at the whole person, their housing, their debt, their mental health issues and creates a rounded response, that is proven to be more successful at getting people into work."

Asked what the number of young people not in education, employment or training should be by the next election, Burnham said he wanted the level to come down "as soon as possible". 

He said he wanted 45-day work placements offered to every 16-18-year-old "who wants one" and was working on funding the places.

During Prime Minister's Questions, Badenoch challenged Burnham over how long the plans would take to produce savings.

"[The prime minister] isn't planning any savings for 10 years," the Conservative leader said.

"We can't wait 10 years. Those young people need those jobs now."

Burnham insisted he would work "as quickly as possible to get the welfare bill down".

The prime minister added that he had asked the cabinet to "reprioritise spending plans" for next year to start funding new work placements for young people.

Accusing the Conservatives of a "cuts-only approach", he said: "The Milburn report says to reduce the welfare bill, you have to improve support."

Meanwhile, the government has also announced it will fully fund college places for everyone aged 16 to 18 in England for 2027/28.

The Association of Colleges said the money would allow its members to enrol thousands of extra students each year "confident in the knowledge that they will receive funding for them".

College funding in England is lagged, so colleges receive money every September for the students they enrolled in the previous September.

Colleges say more teenagers in the population and greater demand for vocational courses means they currently either take on more students than they receive funding for, or they have to turn students away.

However, the DfE does not collect data on how often this happens or how many of those students end up among the 150,000 16 to 18-year-olds who are not in education, employment or training (Neet).

In his report, Milburn warned that employer National Insurance, wage costs, business rates and employment protections could be factors in deterring employers from hiring unexperienced young people. 

"Fair pay, greater security and predictable hours matter," Milburn said, but added that the cost and complexity of hiring young people had increased "sharply".

On workers' rights, Burnham argued a balance had to be struck adding: "The more you strengthen employment security that creates more durable jobs that young people are more likely to stay in."

Asked if former Chancellor Rachel Reeves had made the situation worse by increasing the tax employers pay, the prime minister said the government had increased support for those hiring long-term unemployed people. 

He also defended his reset with the EU, which could make it easier for young Europeans to work in the UK.

He argued that a closer relationship with the EU would help grow the economy, which would provide more opportunities for young people. 

Attempts to change the welfare system under Sir Keir Starmer last year failed when Labour backbenchers refused to support the Â£5bn cuts to benefits, raising concerns about the impact on disabled people. 

So far, there are few signs that Burnham's plans will trigger a similar backlash, although some Labour backbenchers and the Unison trade union have said the proposals on benefits could push young people into poverty. 

Steve Darling, the Liberal Democrat work and pensions spokesman, said the government should "scrap the damaging job tax" and work with European countries to grow the economy.

Green Party MP Sian Berry urged the minister to "reject cuts" for young disabled people. 

"One million young people need a leg up - one that does not come with the chair being kicked out from under them," she said. 

Reform UK said immigration was the "elephant in the room" and called on the government to cut employer national insurance for British workers "so that young Brits are at the front of the queue for new jobs".

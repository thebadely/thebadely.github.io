Here’s what you need to know: Broadcaster Poppy Jay explores the tradwife world for a new BBC documentary

Dressed in an unfamiliar long skirt, Poppy Jay is standing in a chicken coop faced with an angry cockerel as she checks for eggs.

As a director and writer, Jay is normally behind the camera, but today she's in front of it, trying out life as a tradwife.

The Tradwife - short for traditional wife - movement sees women embracing traditional gender roles in the home, such as cooking, cleaning and child rearing, while submitting to their bread-winning husbands.

While tradwives say they are bringing back old-fashioned family values, the movement has faced controversy. Its critics say tradwives fuel inequality and diminish women's empowerment by reinforcing archetypal gender roles.

For three days, Evangelical Christians Molly and Aaron Edwards welcomed Jay, a proud feminist, into their Yorkshire home to show her their lifestyle for the BBC One documentary Young, British and Tradwife. 

On two of the days, the couple - who have seven children - allowed Jay to take on the homely duties that are usually Molly's responsibility, so Jay could try out the tradwife life.

"I was exhausted," Jay exclaimed when she spoke to BBC News.

During her stay, she helped Molly cook, home school the children, tend to the garden and brave the chicken coop.

She was given a book called The Gift of Womanhood to study the night before and asked to wear a dress.

"What struck me was how hard Molly works - like any mum of seven," Jay said. "She is a devoted mother, she cooks and cleans, she's doing endless loads of washing. She's barely got a moment to herself and I felt like I was getting in the way."

Presenter Poppy Jay (left) said while most of the time she disagreed with the tradwife lifestyle, she understood the appeal for Molly

The tradwife phenomenon has been popularised on social media which has been a major driving force in its expansion.

Some US creators have millions of followers - Hannah Neeleman, widely seen as one of the world's leading tradwife influencers, has 10.3 million users following her Instagram account, Ballerina Farm. 

However, another popular American influencer, Nara Smith, has rejected the label. Smith married young, has four children and her content centres on cooking food (or even making toothpaste) from scratch, but insists she's no tradwife.

"They kind of used me as the poster child of the tradwife movement, which was very bizarre to me," Smith said on the Call Her Daddy podcast. "Sure, do I love cooking... but that's a passion of mine, it's a hobby - it's not something that I need to be doing."

The tradwife trend has also made its way into popular culture, with author Caro Claire Burke's satirical novel Yesteryear selling more than 1.4 million copies and Hollywood actress Anne Hathaway has signed up to produce and star in a film adaptation.

Film maker Poppy Jay wants to know why the tradwife world of domestic chores and submission appeals to so many. Reflecting on her own experience of forced marriage, she pins on an apron and meets British tradwives to try to find out.

Much of the tradwife lifestyle is played out on social media with glossy images of baking and planting vegetables, but Molly Edwards has been a tradwife for more than 15 years, long before TikToks existed.

She sees the role as more than "just floating around in a dress, baking sourdough".

"It's very connected to our faith," she explains in the documentary, "and what we believe God has designed women to do and to be in their homes, just under the guidance and leadership of our husbands." 

Jay tells BBC News she saw how much work Molly did which had "parallels" with her upbringing in a conservative Muslim family, which included her entering an arranged marriage when she was 19. She and her husband divorced about five years later.

"I've helped raise my siblings, I know my way around a kitchen, I can cook, I tutored my siblings," Jay said. "Other than the chicken coop, it's not unfamiliar territory. But I think doing it for two days - I know this sounds ridiculous... it's just the relentless pace of her life."

While Molly manages the house, her husband Aaron is a full-time theologian who shares his views on religion and anti-feminism on social media.

When speaking to Jay, Aaron explained the reason the household work was down to his wife: "Molly's obviously omni-competent and brilliant but submission is still really hard," he says, "and when I say something that Molly disagrees with, that's when the submission really matters."

Jay admits that during her interviews with Aaron, there was some "sparring", with the pair often not seeing eye-to-eye.

"He doesn't like feminism," Jay said, "he thinks feminism's ruined society."

"He definitely romanticises a bygone era, which I think [means] you're going down a dangerous path. [He had] the idea that women were better off in the Victorian times - according to who? What evidence do you have of that?"

While Molly says her social media does not make money, some tradwives do look to turn a profit through their posts.

West Sussex couple Megan and Joseph Landreth-Smith have three children and have an Instagram account with nearly 6,000 followers.

Megan often gets brand deals to share products on her Instagram, which she says helps with some extra income and "feels like a really natural fit" around what they are doing already - producing content while being with their children.

Just like many influencers, trolls can be a problem, but they also help to bring in some money.

With her husband Zion, Yorkshire-based life coach Laura Zachary promotes a course called the Academy of Biblical Wifery, based on their born-again Christian beliefs, to more than 32,000 Instagram followers.

"The more disagreement on a comments section, the more people that come to it and the more you get paid".

Megan Landreth-Smith says while she spends time with her kids, promoting products like children's clothing helps with some extra income

In a busy and complicated world, Jay said she appreciates why some women see the attraction in living in what seems to be a more simple way as a tradwife.

But after a few days of experiencing a tradwife's role, she was exhausted and frustrated, saying the belief that men were in charge of everything was "where I draw the line".

"I came away having a lot of respect for [Molly] and to a degree understanding the appeal," Jay said, "but I think for me the submission was the hardest part to grapple with and seeing submission in real life play out in front of me - that was an interesting experience."

The "appeal" which Jay identifies goes much broader - a report published by King's College London, external found 79% of women surveyed were drawn to the relaxed, calm lifestyle it portrays.

But its co-author, Prof Heejung Chung, who specialises in researching the tradwife phenomenon, said that it can be the tip of a political change.

The content might begin with "nice dresses", said Chung, but can "draw them towards more extremist, far-right, or nationalist ideologies, which ultimately seek to curtail women's autonomy and reinforce patriarchal authority".

However, her report concludes most young women and men surveyed showed "largely progressive views on gender roles".

For those who liked the content, it was down to its "lifestyle imagery", rather than "the traditional gender division of labour or power".

But this does not mean the tradwife movement poses no risk to gender equality, the report says, and its "growing popularity... should not be dismissed or trivialised".

According to new developments, Oliver Bearman has not scored points in the past five grands prix, though he did finish sixth in the Singapore sprint race

The 2026 Formula 1 season closes with three races in consecutive weekends, the first of them a night race in Las Vegas. 

With the drivers' and constructors' titles won by Kimi Antonelli and Mercedes respectively, there remains a close contest over who finishes second in the drivers' championship.

Before the race along the Vegas' Strip, BBC F1 correspondent Andrew Benson answers your latest questions.

Is Ollie Bearman's career in danger of stalling by being at Haas and stuck in the Ferrari bottleneck? He obviously has the talent to be a world championship contender but being stuck at the back of the grid can't be doing his career any good. - Dave

Oliver Bearman has still not been confirmed as a Haas driver for 2027, even though both parties say that's what they want.

From Bearman's point of view, it's not hard to see why he might be asking a few questions about his future.

He is a member of the Ferrari driver academy and they show no sign of wanting him not to be, but at the same time they are not showing much sign of wanting to promote him to the senior team.

In that sense, Bearman's future hangs to a degree on Lewis Hamilton's.

Hamilton is talking about staying in F1 at least until 2030, which would mean a new contract at the end of next year, presumably with Ferrari.

But will Ferrari keep him? That is unknown, amid what has undoubtedly been some tension between him, team-mate Charles Leclerc and the team management this season.

If Hamilton stays, Bearman would be within his rights to ask how they see his future, and to ask for the chance to look for opportunities elsewhere.

How secure is James Vowles' position at Williams? He seems like a great guy, always very open and honest. But the fact remains that the team targeted this year for a big performance jump and they've actually gone backwards. Is Vowles running out of time? - James

Williams have had a bad season in 2026. There is no question about that.

For three years since Vowles became team principal in 2023, he had been talking about 2026 as the team's opportunity to take a significant step towards their ambitions of returning to the front.

Having finished fifth in 2025 - a result that even surprised the team - another step forward was expected this year under the new rules.

But that's not what happened. Instead, Williams have gone significantly backwards.

The car was late, too heavy and lacked downforce. Little relative progress has been made through the year and they lie ninth in the constructors' championship with three races to go and will almost certainly finish there.

All year, Vowles has been explaining that the new rules exposed pressure points in the Williams factory that he had not realised existed. Essentially, the factory systems were not able to cope with the demands being put on them.

He says they needed this experience to be able to fix them and move forwards to a better place.

A critic might question why Vowles had not realised they were there in the three years he had been leading the time up to that point.

Drivers Carlos Sainz and Alex Albon have been unimpressed by what has happened this year.

But Vowles succeeded in getting both to stay on for at least one more season. Although whether that was because they retain belief in the team or because they did not have that many better options is a debatable point.

Asked what gave him the confidence to stay, Albon said: "This year I've been brought into a more deeper analysis of where it went wrong from last year to this year.

"So why were we late? Why was the car overweight? What were the reasons? Why did we miss the trick on even the philosophy of the car, for example?

"I'm satisfied with what those reasons are. As simple as that, really.

"At the same time, I also want to see these things materialise and I want to see these changes happen next year."

Albon admitted to there being a "deep frustration shared with the whole team that after all the efforts of prioritising this year's car, we haven't delivered a good job".

A new, lighter chassis was introduced at the Azerbaijan Grand Prix. That will be carried over into next year so, as Albon puts it, "we start next year's car with a chassis that's not heavy, that's on the right foot, that we can then start focusing a lot more on not losing weight and a lot more time on aerodynamics and making the car go faster".

Through this season, Vowles has taken to publicly calling for a revision to the cost-cap rules, saying they bake in an advantage for the big teams, and wanting allowances for extra spending for others to catch up.

His critics say that those allowances are already there in one form, and that Vowles is using this as a distraction technique.

Whether his job is on the line if there is not a significant step forward for 2027 after all this is not clear. But the pressure is mounting, and he will certainly have a lot of explaining to do if there isn't.

With Max Verstappen established at Red Bull for the foreseeable future, what is Racing Bulls' real purpose: to discover Max MkII for Red Bull or simply provide a route to finding a reliable number two? Is it time Racing Bulls was allowed to operate as a genuinely independent, competitive team, rather than effectively functioning as Red Bull's driver development programme? - Michael

Racing Bulls' raison d'etre is the same as it has always been through all its name changes since Red Bull bought Minardi at the end of 2005.

That is to act as a training ground for drivers for the main Red Bull team.

It can be argued that the Red Bull young driver programme has not been especially successful.

In 20 years, the only top-line driver it has brought up through the junior categories all the way to being a success at Red Bull Racing was Daniel Ricciardo.

Sebastian Vettel was a BMW driver before Red Bull signed him, and Max Verstappen was plucked straight into F1 by Red Bull after winning a tug-of-war with Mercedes while he was in F3.

And Carlos Sainz had to leave the Red Bull programme to get his opportunity in a leading team in F1.

That's not a great track record in two decades of operation, and it's exactly why a new approach has been taken now Helmut Marko and Christian Horner are no longer involved.

In the past, you can pretty much bet that Liam Lawson would have been released this season and that Arvid Lindblad and Nikola Tsolov would be Racing Bulls' line-up in 2027.

Instead, Red Bull have kept Lawson on alongside Lindblad, and Tsolov will have to wait his turn.

The reasoning behind that was to be more systematic about driver evaluation.

On balance, Lawson has had the upper hand on Lindblad this season, even though the Briton has shown significant promise in his highlights in his rookie year.

After being dropped after two races in 2025, Lawson is highly unlikely to be given a full-time seat at Red Bull again.

So, the argument went, if Lindblad can't beat him, he shouldn't be promoted either. So Red Bull decided to give Lindblad a second season alongside Lawson to prove whether he can beat the New Zealander.

If he can, he might get a chance of promotion at some point. If he can't, then give Tsolov his chance.

What is it about a circuit that makes it good for overtaking and why is it that some tracks seem to make overtakes so very difficult? - Ollie

The simplistic answer to this question is to provide a long straight with a hard braking point into a slow corner at the end of it.

In reality, it's a bit more complex than that, but there are certain tracks that give a decent blueprint.

Silverstone and Austin, for example, are two of the best tracks for racing on the calendar.

Both have the above, but they also include sequences of corners through which cars can follow each other, trying alternate lines for attack and defence.

Think the combination of corners from Turns 12 to 16 in Austin, for example. Or the run through Vale, Club, Abbey, Village, the Loop down the Wellington Straight and into Brooklands and Luffield at Silverstone.

Another good example is the run from Turn One to Turn Four at Bahrain - a long straight into a big braking zone, a combination of corners where a driver can get the switchback and then an open slow corner where overtaking is possible on either side.

At the same time, there are certain characteristics that militate against overtaking and good racing.

One is combined braking and turning on corner entry - a reason drivers cited for the lack of overtaking at the new Madrid circuit this year.

Another is negative-camber corners, which is why overtaking at Abu Dhabi has always been so difficult, as that track has a lot of them.

Since Abu Dhabi was revised a few years ago, racing has improved there because of two key changes.

One was to change the fiddly chicane section at the end of the second long straight into one long banked corner, Turn Nine. Lewis Hamilton and George Russell raced side by side through there in their final race as Mercedes team-mates in 2024

Another was to take out the chicane before what is now Turn Five and open up its entry and narrow the exit. That's to provide different lines under braking - similar to the uphill entry to Turn One in Austin.

Why are traditional geographical country race names being replaced by city-branded GPs, such as 'Sao Paolo' (formerly the Brazilian Grand Prix) and 'Mexico City' (formerly the Mexican Grand Prix). I feel a certain amount of heritage has been lost by F1 no longer calling these classic races by their country names. - Jordan

The naming of a grand prix is usually pretty simple - it's after the country in which it is held. But that's not always the case.

When it is not, the reason is usually to do with a mix of financial and promotional reasons, and that is the case for both Brazil and Mexico.

Naming them after the city rather than the country was a request from the organisers as a result of various issues to do with funding and support, as well as raising awareness of the cities through branding.

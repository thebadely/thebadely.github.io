The BBC has tracked down businessman Liam Kavanagh to a luxury apartment in Bucharest. His whereabouts had remained largely unknown for years following his role in one of the biggest financial scandals in British local government history.

Thurrock Council in Essex invested more than Â£600m in Kavanagh's solar energy businesses - despite stark warnings that the investments were too risky.

With the money he received, Kavanagh bought a Â£12m private jet, a yacht, a fleet of supercars, a country house adored by Jane Austen, a Damien Hirst pickled sheep artwork and a Â£250,000 revolving bed.

Then it all came crashing down. Thurrock was left with a vast financial black hole and effectively went bankrupt, forcing central government to step in with hundreds of millions of pounds of taxpayers' money.

The council is now suing Kavanagh, accusing him of deceit and alleging that millions of pounds of its investments were diverted for his personal benefit.

Kavanagh denies misleading Thurrock and any wrongdoing. His legal defence says he honestly believed he was entitled to use the money in the way he did and highlights wording which he claims allowed him to make deductions from the money Thurrock invested.

Chasing Mr Sunshine

A billion-pound gamble. A council in financial ruin. Investigative journalists Catrin Nye and Meirion Jones uncover how public money was lost - and who's got it.

At the heart of the story are two seemingly very different men.

Kavanagh, known by some as Mr Sunshine, was the charismatic businessman selling investments in solar farms. Sean Clark was Thurrock's finance director - the council official who found the money to invest with him.

And Clark found a lot of it.

He borrowed huge sums from other local authorities around the UK, often through deals that could be arranged with a few emails. At one point Thurrock owed more than Â£1bn to other councils.

The strategy initially appeared to be a triumph. The investments were generating millions of pounds in income at a time when councils were struggling with government spending cuts.

But behind the apparent success, warning signs were mounting.

Journalist Gareth Davies, of the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, has spent years investigating what happened.

"I think this story is so shocking because of the scale of the greed, of the incompetence - the fact that this council gambled with huge sums of public money and lost."

Sean Clark (left) and Liam Kavanagh were at the Thurrock Awards Night together in February 2018

Describing the lack of records surrounding some of the deals, Davies said: "There wasn't even evidence at the time that these transactions were made, that any of this had ever taken place. I mean, it's mind-boggling."

Thurrock's own financial adviser, Arlingclose, was also sounding the alarm.

Arlingclose chief executive Mark Pickering described the council's investment activity as "pretty extreme" and said his firm felt "the likelihood of them blowing up was pretty high".

"You try several times to help a friend - and after a while you have to leave them to their own devices, don't you?" he said.

Arlingclose eventually cut ties with Thurrock. But Clark carried on borrowing and investing.

Meanwhile, leaked bank records show millions moving from Kavanagh's company into his personal accounts, alongside payments for luxury purchases.

He also started to look like a different man after Thurrock's investments.

"You can look in terms of the photographs, before and after," said Davies.

"At one end you've got a fairly unkempt man whose nickname in the early days was the sticky Bandit because he looked like one of the bad guys out of Home Alone.

"A few years later - this bronzed, muscular man who's obviously enjoying a luxury lifestyle."

For six years, Kavanagh avoided answering many of the key questions about what happened.

So we went looking for him, and eventually traced him to an exclusive apartment complex in northern Bucharest.

A security guard appeared carrying a camera after the BBC reporters called at the apartment complex

Leaked financial records give a glimpse of the life inside: One wall covered in Swarovski crystals, another featuring 25 television screens, and the enormous revolving bed - so big it had to be lifted into the penthouse by crane.

We attempted to deliver Kavanagh a letter containing our questions and asked him to come downstairs to sign for it. We wanted him to come in person so we could ask him our questions.

He did not appear. Soon afterwards, a security guard carrying a camera was stationed outside the building.

Kavanagh's lawyers later confirmed we had found the correct address and said he did not wish to communicate with us directly.

We had more success finding Clark.

BBC reporters approached him near his home in Kent while he was walking his dog and asked whether it was OK if we asked some questions about Thurrock.

"No, it isn't," he replied.

Asked whether he felt any guilt, or had anything to say to the people of Thurrock, Clark answered simply: "No."

He also declined to answer why he had invested so much public money with one businessman, or whether he was "a fool or a crook".

Clark has admitted professional misconduct relating to his time at Thurrock. The Financial Reporting Council said he had "recklessly provided misleading information to council members, and therefore to the public". He received a severe reprimand and was excluded from membership of his professional accountancy body for five years.

The Serious Fraud Office is investigating suspected fraud involving Kavanagh's businesses and Thurrock Council. It has not announced whether Kavanagh will face criminal charges.

For the people of Thurrock, however, the bill has already arrived - through higher council tax, reduced services and debts that will burden the authority for years.

Local social entrepreneur Neil Woodbridge became emotional when we asked him about the lack of accountability.

"I can't get my head around the fact that no-one's held to account," he said.

Here’s what you need to know: F1 is set to change its engines for the 2030 season

Formula 1 bosses are close to a deal that will see V8 turbo hybrid engines replace the current controversial engines in 2030.

McLaren chief executive officer Zak Brown said an "agreement in principle" had been reached by engine manufacturers and F1 bosses on the new rules.

These will be for a 2.1-litre V8 turbo engine with a smaller electrical element than this year, as first revealed by BBC Sport last month. 

Some details remain to be resolved but the main core of the rules has been agreed.

The electrical element will be for a maximum power output of 200kW - compared with 350kW this year.

But only half of that will be available at all times, with the remaining half used only for an overtake mode.

A key element of the rules will be to remove the issue of energy starvation that has been criticised by the drivers this year for taking away "pure" racing.

But the overall power output of the engines will be similar to now.

The combustion engine will have about 780bhp, while 200kW is 270bhp.

That means a total power output of 1,050bhp in overtake mode or just over 900bhp in standard running.

Brown said: "Everyone is very, very excited. Now it's about kind of dotting the i's and crossing the t's.

"I'm excited for the new engines, I think everyone will be, the power units. But there's a lot more also going on to continue to evolve the sport. So, between the FIA and Formula 1, they're doing a great job."

Alpine executive director Flavio Briatore said: "We have the agreement, basically. It's not signed, but it looks like everybody's agreed to 2030 V8s, and I'm very happy. 

"It's the right way to go. We need an engine with a lot of noise, and a simple engine, and we need to be racing with that."

The ruleset amounts to a compromise reached by manufacturers after FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem initially pushed for a naturally aspirated engine without turbo or electrical components.

But these were required to reduce overall fuel consumption, and therefore weight at the start of races.

That means Ben Sulayem's desire for a return to the loud volumes of the previous V8 era from 2006 to 2013 will not be achieved. The engines are expected to sound similar to now.

This was important because a significant increase in engine noise would have put at threat some races in city centres that are commercially important to F1, such as Miami and Las Vegas.

The 2.1-litre capacity has been arrived at because it amounts to adding to two cylinders to the current 1.6-litre V6 blocks, with the aim of reducing development costs.

Cars are expected to start races with about 110-120kg of fuel, up from about 100kg now. A total weight limit of 150kg for the power-units will be imposed, down by about 30kg from the current engines.

There will be a fuel energy flow limit to keep the power of the combustion engine under control, and a budget cap figure for engines set at $190m (Â£145m) per year.

As part of negotiations, General Motors, backers of the Cadillac team, will delay the production of its new engine until 2030. It had been scheduled for 2029.

In return, GM will lose its voting rights on current engine rules and be classified as a new manufacturer from 2030.

The team will continue to use Ferrari customer engines through 2029.

Cadillac F1 chief executive officer Dan Towriss said: "For a period of time, we were working on both. So, they've already got a V6 base to build off of, and working towards the V8. 

"There are a number of designs that are in motion now, and so there's a lot of good foundation for what we're trying to build and the direction we're headed in."

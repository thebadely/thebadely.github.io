Keeping the house warm is particularly important for parents with young children and babies.

Lucy Kells is a mum of two in east Belfast on maternity leave.

She said she expects the winter months to be difficult because "most people are going to have to have the heating on, especially if you've young kids in the house".

"There's no escaping it - everything feels more expensive and it is more expensive."

She added that, despite "budgeting proactively" and "being as careful as you can with savings and credit cards", rising bills mean people's financial situations are out of their control.

"You have to cut somewhere else and I already feel we are doing that," she said.

Nuala Hyde said she has noticed more people using her branch's free activities to combat the rising cost of living

Lucy is one of the parents who attend a weekly baby class at Woodstock Library in east Belfast.

Branch manager Nuala Hyde said she has noticed more people using their free activities to combat the rising cost of living.

She said: "As well as families, we have lots of older people coming in now to keep warm."

"We have people coming in who will stay all day, use the computer, have a chat and stay until closing time sometimes, so it is quite evident that we are providing an essential service at the minute when people are struggling," she added.

Ryan McStay said he was "shocked" by the energy price increases

Ryan McStay is a final year student at St Mary's University College Belfast, where he is studying to become a primary school teacher. 

He said he and his flatmates were "shocked" when they received a letter explaining their Firmus energy bills would be rising by 12.5%. 

"It shouldn't be normalised," he told BBC News NI.

Ryan said increases like this exacerbate an already serious cost of living crisis for students like him.

"The price of everything is rocketing. You're always second guessing and thinking about how much money have I got."

The 21-year-old, who is from Newry, said the cost of living in Northern Ireland means he plans to move abroad after university. 

"I personally believe the best option for me right now would be to leave the country."

Naomi Campbell is an urgent hospice care nurse for Marie Curie

Naomi Campbell is an urgent hospice care nurse for Marie Curie. She provides palliative nursing care to patients in their homes.

"Many of them are affected by fuel poverty," Naomi said.

"I think it is getting worse. People are struggling more, and the problem is that your energy needs increase hugely when you're dying at home.

"Somebody is already struggling with the fact that they're dying. To then know that they can't keep their house warm."

Naomi said a number of factors contribute to the increased energy needs, including the need to stay warm, different sleeping patterns, and essential medical devices.

She said some patients have turned down services that could help improve their quality of life because they can't pay their electricity bills.

Terminal illness adds further costs for patients, such as the cost of transport to and from hospital appointments, Naomi added.

"I'm really saddened," she said. 

"The support is limited, there is very little out there. There is no reduction in costs of their electricity, even though their needs are so much higher and essential for their care."

Naomi added that Marie Curie have called for the implementation of a "social tariff" for people with terminal illnesses, to ease their financial burden.

"It would make such a huge difference to have that bit of extra money in your last months of life, so you're comfortable and you can spend it with your family," she said.

She said some people spend the end of their life working because they "don't have any other access to money".

"You end up dying not being able to keep your house warm. That to me is just devastating."

Experts revealed that The justice secretary has called for new rules to prevent barristers from using rape myths to defend their clients in the wake of the Russell Brand trial.

As first reported by the Times, external, in a letter to the Bar Standards Board (BSB), Alex Norris said it was "unacceptable" to "perpetuate myths" about victims in court and disciplinary action could be taken against those who do so.

In his closing speech, Brand's defence barrister, Ian Winter KC, questioned why his client's accusers had not fought back.

The BSB said it had set up an expert group this year to advise on "strengthening standards for barristers", while the Bar Council said defendants had a "right to have evidence tested in court".

Brand was sentenced to 10-and-a-half years in prison for one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault.

Winter said to the court "not one of them [Brand's accusers] resisted or screamed or even tried to leave".

Winter also asked the jury if they could be sure that out of the "many, many women" Brand had had sex with over a decade, "he raped just three of them".

Norris wrote in his letter to the BSB: "[The board] must send a strong signal to the profession on the harm arising from rape myths, and that the perpetuation of such myths within a court room is unacceptable, just as they are unacceptable in our society at large."

The charity Rape Crisis says it is "really common for people who experience rape, sexual assault, sexual abuse or other types of sexual violence to find they can't move or speak".

The judge presiding over Brand's case was praised by the Criminal Bar Association and campaign groups for comments he made during his sentencing remarks to dispel rape myths.

Mr Justice Bennathan told Southwark Crown Court: "Whatever women wear, wherever they go, indeed whatever they drink, they retain the right to say no and to demand justice from the criminal courts when sexual predators like you, Russell Brand, act without their consent."

A spokesperson for the BSB said such cases "must be decided on the basis of the evidence and not prejudice", adding: "Any other approach may discourage others from coming forward and adversely affect public confidence in the criminal justice system more widely."

Kirsty Brimelow KC, chair of the Bar Council of England and Wales which is part of the group examining the need for further training, said it was important for the BSB to remain "independent of the government".

She told BBC Radio 4's Woman's Hour that a "regulatory gap" had not been made clear to her and there were already powers to discipline barristers if breaches of the code of conduct happen. 

She said training for "vulnerable witnesses" was already undertaken but it could be "problematic" if it was made mandatory.

Brimelow said there were currently a lack of barristers defending cases of rape and sexual assault and the possibility of further compulsory training could see more pushed out.

"What I wouldn't like to see is publicity around one case to take a life of its own rather than looking at what we already do," she told the BBC.

A spokesperson for the Ministry of Justice said the justice secretary was "determined to make protections for victims as strong as possible". 

"Rape myths have absolutely no place in our courts, giving evidence in cases of this nature is a brave act and can be traumatic for victims: they must never feel as though they are the ones on trial," the spokesperson said.

"Independent judges are already able to, and do, give directions to counter rape myths but we always want to keep looking at ways we can improve how the entire justice system treats victims of sexual violence."

In an interview with the BBC earlier this month, Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley said some of the comments made at the trial he had seen reported were "getting very close to victim blaming".

He also backed calls for a law change that would require judges to direct juries on rape myths and misconceptions in every rape trial.

"It shouldn't be possible for inaccurate statements to be made to a jury to suggest that because a rape victim didn't behave in one way that somehow undermines their case," Sir Mark said.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said the government needs to make sure victims of rape cases do not feel like they are on trial.

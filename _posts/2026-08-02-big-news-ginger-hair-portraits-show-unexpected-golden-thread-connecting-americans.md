For Kieran Dodds, photographing people with red hair has become something of an obsession over the past decade.

The 45-year-old, from Edinburgh, who is himself a "proud ginger", began taking pictures in his homeland in 2013 as part of a series examining Scottish identity.

His interest in the subject soon took him to other ginger hotspots such the Russian city of Perm and Jamaica's Treasure Beach.

His 2020 book Gingers spanned the world but after its release he notice there was a "huge gap".

"I realised I didn't have any American citizens," Kieran told the BBC. 

"Then all these people got in touch."

Nicholas Modaressi says his dad, who grew up in Iran, refuses to get his genetics tested. "I'm Persian," he tells his son Nic, who grew up in Maryland

"I was like 'oh wow, of course, the whole world is in the States'," Kieran says. "It's a microcosm."

He says a message from a Native American man who had red hair instantly aroused his curiosity.

"I found it very interesting and it made me think, 'where on earth did it come from?'"

Joshua Elliot says his mum's side has always been involved with the Muscogee tribe

The cover of his latest book - Gingers of America, external - features Joshua Elliot from the Muscogee (Creek) Nation in Oklahoma.

He told Kieran that some of his mother's relatives were German and on his dad's side there were people from England and Sweden, so that could explain where his ginger hair comes from. 

"My mom's side has always been involved with the Muscogee tribe and traditions and I've grown up around it," he said. 

Joshua knows he does not fit people's expectations of what a Native American should look like, but he said: "Our chief's wife told me it's about your involvement in your community and your tribe, the people around you and what you put back into it.

"Which I really took to heart." 

Lottie Wahkinney is a Comanche girl, from Oklahoma, with Scottish grandparents

Kieran spoke to a lot of Native Americans on his visits and most knew of a European ancestry which could explain their red hair. 

"The thing that surprised me was how early on there were these Scots and Irish marrying Native Americans," he said.

Lottie Wahkinney, a Comanche girl from Oklahoma, has Scottish grandparents, from Galashiels.

Her father Jared told Kieran his dad was Comanche but his mum was born and raised in Scotland.

"That's why my Lottie has a good mix of everything going on," Jared said. 

"My Scottish grandparents would come over every summer. 

"On my first visit to Scotland when I was 22, folks were like, 'Where are your moccasins? Where's your headdress?'. 

"I explained 'I wear Nikes'."

Lakia Jones (right) says all her relatives have long, straight, jet-black hair like her sister Maci (left)

While most Native Americans could explain where the ginger gene might have come from, there was one exception - a Cherokee girl called Lakia - whose family knew of no European ancestry.

"It is a totally different colour of red," Kieran said. 

"It's deep ruby. That really intrigued me." 

In the book, Lakia says that all her family, on both sides, have jet-black straight hair but hers turned red during her childhood.

"Everyone thought, since I was about eight, that I dyed my hair," she said. 

"And still now everyone's like, 'what hair colour is that?'."

Jaxston Hudson from Birmingham, Alabama

After meeting Native Americans, Kieran knew there was something in the US he could explore. 

Over the next few years, he made seven lengthy trips, often linked to his full-time profession as an environmental photographer.

One state he found surprising was Alabama, a place many in the UK associate with racial segregation and the struggle for civil rights as well as its pioneering space exploration programmes.

"I knew it for racism and Nasa," Kieran said. 

"In my head it was stuck in the 60s but Birmingham is an incredibly vibrant city.

"I met a lot of black Americans who had often married someone who is white."

Rachel Releford says people think she is her daughter Liliana's nanny

Rachel Releford told Kieran that when she goes out with her daughter Liliana people think she is the nanny or the babysitter.

"I'm like, 'nope, she's mine'," Rachel said. 

"I think you still get some people who grew up with the mindset that things need to be segregated. 

"Some of my grandparents' friends were a little hesitant when they found out I was dating my husband. They said: 'You're really going to let her date someone of a different race?'

"But my grandparents were like, 'Go for it. He's a cool guy, he's a sweet one.' I love him for who he is. He's a great human being."

 Henry Davis says he had red hair like his son when he was younger

Also in Alabama, he met Henry Davis and his young son Henry Davis II.

The older man no longer has any red hair but he says he looked just like his son when he was the same age.

"We're not really sure where it comes from," Henry senior said. 

"My dad is turning grey now. He'll get some red in his beard, and then it kind of goes grey. 

"So I guess it's from his side." 

Colton, Bennett, Emmeline, Christopher, Amelia, and Genevieve Smith from Burke, Virginia

As someone who grew up with red hair himself, Kieran says he is well aware that ginger can be used as an insult.

However, he says: "I use the word for its descriptive power because red is the colour of fire engines and blood whereas ginger has a golden tone to it. 

"You can use it in a kind way."

He says the book uses ginger hair as "a golden thread" that weaves together America's diverse and difficult history in a way which shows a connection between people.

"That's my big idea," Kieran says.

"We are all made of the same stuff and with the ginger hair it shows."

Cali Trawick has an ancestry that includes Irish, African-American, indigenous American and Hispanic roots

According to Kieran no-one exemplifies this better than a young lad named Cali whom he met in Alabama.

"His background is everything - European, African-American, Hispanic and indigenous as well," he says. 

"His gran is white but she was unable to get married to a black guy in Alabama - even after the courts had made it legal. 

"She moved to Georgia and had a family there. It's an incredibly diverse family."

Allison Dubinski grew up in Indiana but says her hair colour is a key asset to help her stand out in the competitive acting world of New York

In Scotland, it is thought as many as 13% of the population - about 700,000 people - are natural redheads, a higher percentage than anywhere else in the world.

Globally, less than 2% of the population are thought to have red hair but Kieran's research lead him to believe the US figure is higher than that. 

"We landed on about 4% for the genetic prevalence," he says.

"If all the gingers in America were gathered in one place, Gingertown would be about 14 million people. 

"It's a big city. You would see it from space it would be so bright."

Evelyn Lee, from Birmingham, Alabama, used to hate her ginger curls but now she loves them. "I think it's like Spanish princesses' flowing locks - like medieval paintings," she says. 

Kieran says the big difference between America and other places is that they tend to have really thought about where they come from.

"Everyone has self-consciously moved from somewhere," he says.

"And that includes the Native Americans, who have been moved from other parts of America.

"That's what makes it interesting."

He says his book is a road trip through the US trying to find out about the country through this unique trait.

Keisha Engley grew up in rural Washington State. Her American grandfather fought in Vietnam and met her grandmother there.

For Kieran, the recent men's football World Cup got people excited about the US again in a way they haven't been over the past few years because of political divisions.

He says he was pleased to see people visiting the US and saying "it is not what I expected".

"There's such a lot of amazing people and communities there," he says. 

"People you might connect with and agree with on a lot of things.

"I'm quite heartened that the football has revealed that.

"I feel quite patriotic for America now."

Let's dive into the details: James Botham was man of the match in Wales' historic win over New Zealand

James Botham lay sprawled on the Principality Stadium turf, pain and exhaustion etched across his face.

Whenever the whistle blew, the Wales flanker in the red scrum cap took an age to rise. Precious seconds to recover. Nobody counts tackles when the ball is dead.

But when play resumed, he was everywhere. Tackling, scrambling, hitting the deck and springing back up.

By the final whistle, Botham had helped Wales beat New Zealand for the first time in 73 years. He was man of the match. And as the celebrations began, the tears flowed.

Quite an afternoon for a player accustomed to working away from the spotlight.

Keeping a Botham out of the headlines was always going to be difficult.

His grandfather, Lord Ian Botham, is one of Britain's great sporting celebrities. Outspoken, charismatic and capable of turning cricket matches almost single-handedly, he is box-office.

James is different.

His game is built on tackles, chasing, wrestling and relentless graft. Few positions in rugby are less glamorous than blind-side flanker.

But on Saturday, Wales' number six was the star of the show.

James Botham (centre) is the grandson of cricket great Ian Botham (left) and the son of ex-Cardiff wing Liam Botham (right)

Botham's introduction to international rugby could hardly have been less glamorous.

He made his Wales debut against Georgia at an empty Parc y Scarlets in November 2020, with Covid-19 restrictions keeping supporters away.

His grandfather always believed he would play for Wales, but getting there was one thing. Staying there proved harder.

Part of Wales' Six Nations-winning squad in 2021, Botham subsequently spent more than two and a half years away from the international side.

Injuries and competition played their part. But perhaps his greatest qualities were not always easy to appreciate.

His game was built less on spectacular breaks than on repeated collisions, defensive work and battles at the breakdown.

In that respect, his career echoes another Cardiff flanker, Josh Navidi.

Navidi made his Wales debut in 2013 but waited four years for his second cap.

Long regarded as an outstanding regional player rather than an international star, his strength, work rate and dominance in contact eventually made him indispensable to Wales.

He won 33 caps and toured with the British and Irish Lions.

Botham has followed a similar path. A gifted athlete, his greatest strengths are not always obvious from the stands or on television.

Sam Warburton spotted them years ago.

"I remember seeing him when he first came to Cardiff," said the former Wales and Lions captain.

"His footwork for contact was amazing, just how explosive he was over five, ten metres."

Warburton believes Botham has the attributes of a top international blindside flanker.

"Really strong in contact. Gets great numbers in the gym. He's fast. He's fit. He's probably the best athlete that Wales have in the back row," Warburton added.

One training session with Botham helped convince Warburton his own playing days were numbered.

"I was linked up with James Botham and that's when I realised the flame had gone," he recalled.

"He was full beans going for it and I remember thinking, in the nicest way, I don't care."

For a player whose career was built on an insatiable appetite for physical confrontation, it was telling.

Warburton realised the aggression and hunger that had driven him to the top were gone.

Botham, meanwhile, was just getting started.

James Botham earned his first Cardiff appearances in the Anglo-Welsh Cup

Former Wales hooker Scott Baldwin shared a room with Botham on a Barbarians tour, where the social traditions are almost as famous as the rugby.

After a few drinks one evening, Botham decided he still needed a fitness session, so he ran a mile to a nearby pitch, completed his drills and jogged back to rejoin his team-mates.

"He was fully committed to the social, but he had to get his bit in," Baldwin recalled.

Baldwin believes that dedication is evident on the field, even if it often goes unnoticed.

"We'd probably underestimate how much work he gets through every game," he added. "His ability to slow ball down is next level."

And then there is his willingness to chase lost causes: "He's the kid that will chase every kick, whether he's going to get there or not."

Against New Zealand, that unseen work was impossible to ignore.

Botham made 30 tackles as Wales ended 73 years of hurt against the All Blacks. Only Dafydd Jenkins (38) and Jac Morgan (31) made more.

But his most remarkable contribution was a tackle that barely registered in the statistics.

With New Zealand threatening the Welsh line, Botham somehow got his knee beneath hooker Asafo Aumua, preventing what looked like a certain try.

An extraordinary intervention from a player who appeared to be running on empty.

Wales scrum-half Reuben Morgan-Williams, who kicked the ball into touch to seal the victory, was impressed.

"You could tell from the weekend he was a Test match animal," he said.

"He's a mental athlete as well. He's fit, he's fast, and he was everywhere, really, wasn't he?"

Warburton believes Botham has made his case to become Wales' established blindside flanker.

"I think he's definitely stamped down his name at six," he said.

At 28, Botham has taken the long road to becoming an overnight success. With his first child due this week, it promises to be a momentous few days off the field, too.

His surname will always invite comparisons. Any profile of James will inevitably mention his grandfather. This one, after all, has done exactly that.

But there was nothing inherited about the tackles, the exhaustion or the tears at the final whistle.

On Saturday, James Botham was the star of his own story.

Here’s what you need to know: Wales wing Louis Rees-Zammit has impressed since returning to rugby union from a stint in American Football

Australia wing Harry Potter had Louis Rees-Zammit exactly where he wanted him.

As Wales attacked in Cardiff on Saturday, Potter showed Rees-Zammit the outside â a four-metre channel between him and the touchline.

The touchline is a defender's best friend. An extra defender. The edge of the cliff. It would take something extraordinary to beat Potter without running out of ground.

A shake of the hips and a change of direction later, Potter was diving like a cricketer in the outfield, grasping at thin air. Rees-Zammit was on his way to setting up a wonderful try for Blair Murray.

Rees-Zammit has been turning heads for years. On the pitch with his pace, off it with his pristine teeth and pop star looks.

But it wasn't raw speed that had left Potter on the turf.

This was Rees-Zammit 2.0 - more than just speed.

Alex Cuthbert knows a thing or two about running. A former national-level 400m runner and British and Irish Lions wing, he spent much of his rugby career working on his technique.

What fascinates him about Rees-Zammit now is not his speed but how he generates it.

"His leg drive and strength from his knee to his hip has got so much stronger," said Cuthbert, who scored 17 tries in 57 Tests for Wales.

The difference is most apparent not over 100 metres but over four or five, changing direction and accelerating before a defender can adjust his feet.

"If you do that shimmy and you stand any sort of player up, you can't recover. Literally can't recover," Cuthbert said.

Cuthbert attributes much of that development to Rees-Zammit's 18 months in American football with the Kansas City Chiefs and Jacksonville Jaguars.

"I thought last season he was a little bit heavy. He looked like a running back last year, whereas this year he is more of a wide receiver," Cuthbert said.

A running back, says Cuthbert, powers through traffic, a wide receiver thrives in open space. He now sees elements of both in Rees-Zammit.

Rees-Zammit's coach at Bristol Bears, Pat Lam, estimates he returned from America eight or nine kilograms heavier.

"What was very clear was his pace was still there, if not quicker, but he was a bigger man and he needed to adjust," said Lam.

Lam is arguably one of rugby's most system-driven coaches. Understanding your role is fundamental at Bristol, where only a select few are afforded the freedom to deviate from the script.

Having established himself as a wing before his American adventure, Rees-Zammit was keen to experiment on his return to rugby.

"He spoke to me about a desire to play full-back and give full-back a go," said Lam.

Lam sees the modern full-back as an auxiliary centre in attack, responsible for creating opportunities for others.

Rees-Zammit's greatest gift is exploiting them.

"But we had to go through that process for him to actually say to me, 'Pat, I'm a world-class winger and I'm a world-class finisher'. And I said, 'I agree'," Lam said.

According to Lam, Rees-Zammit now considers himself a wing who can cover full-back, rather than a full-back who can play on the wing.

Another moment from Saturday offered a reminder of Rees-Zammit's extraordinary pace when Joseph-Aukuso Sua'ali'i intercepted a Welsh pass and set off downfield. 

The Australian, a formidable athlete in his own right, had a head start of at least five metres.

One commentator confidently declared he would not be caught.

Rees-Zammit caught him.

The speed has always been there. The numbers reveal how much more there is to his game now.

Rees-Zammit scored five tries in 10 Premiership appearances for Bristol last season. This season he has matched that tally in five games, beating 28 defenders and making 12 clean breaks.

Against Japan earlier in November, he beat 13 defenders and made more line breaks in 80 minutes than in the entire Six Nations, when he started all five games at full-back.

But Rees-Zammit is not the only thing that has changed since March, Wales have changed too

Even the quickest wing in the world is of limited use if he receives the ball standing still with two defenders in front of him.

"But as a winger, you do rely on your boys inside you," said Cuthbert.

With Wales generating more momentum up front, Cuthbert highlights Blair Murray's ability to engage defenders before releasing Rees-Zammit.

Wales are also deploying both wings on the same side of the field. Rees-Zammit and Josh Adams combining for full-back Murray's try was a fine example.

Scrum-half Tomos Williams has perfected the art of the sharp, accurate pass that bypasses players and gets the ball into the wider channels early.

It helped create Rees-Zammit's try against New Zealand and, eight minutes into Saturday's game against the Wallabies, looked to have created another.

Having dived for the corner, Rees-Zammit's first instinct on getting to his feet was not to celebrate or appeal to the referee, but to fix his hair.

The try would not survive the television replays. The hair had survived the tackle.

Image matters to Rees-Zammit. He is Welsh rugby's most marketable player, its biggest draw. He puts bums on seats and gets people out of them.

There is a lovely soundtrack to Rees-Zammit receiving the ball in space at the Principality Stadium. A low hum of excitement followed by the rattle of thousands of seats folding up almost simultaneously.

The thrill is in the anticipation, and these days there is rather more to anticipate.

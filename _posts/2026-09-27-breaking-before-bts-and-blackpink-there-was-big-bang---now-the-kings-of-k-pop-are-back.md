A sea of glowing yellow crowns. Thousands of voices chanting in Korean.

An explosive surge of energy as the opening synth riffs of Fantastic Baby shatter the stadium air. Big Bang is back.

Fourteen years after their first major European shows, and after a decade marked by military service, member departures and legal scandals, the South Korean group played at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium in London on Saturday as part of their 20th anniversary world tour.

Inside the near-capacity venue, the crowd did not just watch a concert; they stepped into a significant moment in K-pop history.

As the band emerged in their trademark high-fashion, glittering outfits, the crowd danced freely, setting the tone for an evening that felt less like a routine tour stop and more like a homecoming.

Inside Tottenham Hotspur Stadium

For fans, it is a long-awaited reunion. But the significance of Big Bang's return extends beyond nostalgia.

Years before BTS were selling out Wembley Stadium and Blackpink were headlining Hyde Park, Big Bang were already proving Korean pop could succeed far beyond Asia.

The group filled arenas across the West, collaborated with major American artists, experimented with hip-hop and electronic music, and turned their own fashion and creative identities into part of their appeal.

Having attended their European debut at Wembley Arena in 2012, the shift in atmosphere on Saturday was striking.

Where their original UK shows were driven by a hunger to prove themselves and promote a new album, this return as a trio , comprising G-Dragon, Taeyang, and Daesung, felt entirely crafted for their dedicated community. 

By reworking classic tracks and cleverly remixing songs to accommodate absent members, the group relied on pyrotechnics, their signature live band and sheer stage presence rather than elaborate staging.

The result was a reunion that felt celebratory, grounded and playful.

G-Dragon, Taeyang, and Daesung on stage with dancers

In 2011, Big Bang became the first K-pop group to win a major Western music award when they took home the newly created Worldwide Act prize at the MTV Europe Music Awards.

When they made their European debut at Wembley Arena in December 2012, a second show was added after the first sold out in under two hours.

They were leading a pioneering generation that proved global crossover was genuinely possible. 

Then in 2016, Forbes estimated Big Bang had earned $44m (Â£33.2m) over the previous year, making them the first K-pop act to appear on its Celebrity 100 List.

"When it comes to the global market for K-pop, there is no doubt Big Bang made a huge contribution," says Dr Haekyung Um, senior lecturer in music at the University of Liverpool.

"Earlier acts were popular within Asian markets, but Big Bang were true international stars. I would call them the Kings of K-pop, just as we call Michael Jackson the King of Pop," Um adds.

"Big Bang's success really was an early signal of how successful a K-pop stadium tour in the West could truly be."

G-Dragon, Taeyang, and Daesung celebrating their 20th anniversary during the XX: Cosmos World Tour

Before Big Bang, K-pop groups were often viewed as heavily manufactured by entertainment agencies.

But music critics say Big Bang broke the mould. They wrote and produced their own songs, shaped their own streetwear image and blended genres ranging from hip-hop and R&B to trot, a traditional South Korean folk genre.

For many attending on Saturday, the show served as a direct lesson in K-pop's lineage - a chance to see "the idols' idols" perform live.

"They were different because they didn't follow the uniform K-pop cookie-cutter pretty boy phase," explains Sharon, who was visiting London from Singapore. 

"They went harder on their music, a bit more hip-hop. They did what they had to do, and they are the absolute OGs for me."

The shift set them apart across Asia and helped establish the blueprint for modern self-produced acts such as BTS.

Fans arrived kitted out with Big Bang merchandise 

And then there is the yellow crown. At Big Bang concerts, it is impossible to miss the group's crown-shaped lightstick, known as the Bang Bong.

In early K-pop, fans supported artists by waving coloured balloons or generic glow sticks.

But seeking a distinct, permanent identity for their fandom, dubbed "VIPs", band leader G-Dragon proposed a handheld yellow crown, setting the template for custom concert merchandise.

"Fan engagement and coordinated chants have deep roots in Korean music history," explains Um. "But Big Bang helped modernise the physical technology."

The Bang Bong established a model for custom-designed fandom lightsticks and merchandise that modern K-pop groups now follow. 

Outside the venue, that same fan-led energy spilled into the streets, with attendees trading handmade gifts and photo cards - a ritual that has spread to Western arena tours in recent years. 

Volunteer fan groups distributed roughly 35,000 custom-printed banners across the stadium, coordinating a massive crowd-wide salute to be raised in unison during select performances.

Fans exchanging handmade gifts outside the stadium 

Big Bang's story, however, is far from a straightforward tale of success. 

South Korea requires able-bodied men to complete mandatory military service, and Big Bang's members served at different points during the group's hiatus.

Then came the scandals.

In 2017, former band member T.O.P was convicted of marijuana use, an offence heavily criminalised and socially taboo in South Korea, and received a suspended prison sentence while serving his mandatory military duty. He later formally departed from the group.

Then in 2019, the group's youngest member, Seungri, permanently retired from entertainment amid the "Burning Sun" scandal. 

It was a high-profile police investigation into a Seoul nightclub of the same name which exposed a network of sex trafficking, bribery, and illicit filming involving several male K-pop stars.

South Korea's Supreme Court later upheld an 18-month prison sentence for Seungri in 2022 on charges including prostitution mediation, overseas gambling, and embezzlement.

Fans with their merchandise, holding up free banners ahead of the show

Although the band didn't openly acknowledge the departure of two of their former members from the stage,  their absence was obvious to long-time fans in the audience.

Though it did not matter to fans like Annie, who flew from New York to see the show with friends. She said Big Bang's perseverance through hardship made their music mean even more.

"Big Bang was my starter," she says. "I love how they move through the obstacles - it gives me hope. After years of hiatus, it's amazing they're coming back. They are the OGs."

The crowd erupted as the opening chant of Bang Bang Bang echoed before the band even walked out, while their iconic 2008 ballad Haru Haru produced a massive stadium-wide singalong.

Fans meeting up ahead of the concert

The K-pop industry Big Bang helped build has changed almost beyond recognition.

Yet their return has drawn a striking mix of original second-generation fans and younger Gen Z listeners who were barely born when the group debuted.

While many veteran followers grew up alongside the band, a whole new generation discovered them through digital archives or cross-media appearances. 

Gemma and her daughter Casey travelled from Edinburgh wearing handmade custom jackets. 

"I found out about them through T.O.P in Squid Game and then fell down a rabbit hole," Gemma says. "I've converted so many of my friends to K-pop now. It's all about the energy."

For Korean diaspora fans, the show carried an even deeper cultural resonance. 

"I got into Big Bang when their Alive EP (in 2012) was released because I felt they were quite ground-breaking," says Kevin. 

"Before, I felt there was a lot of mimicking by Korean artists on what was happening in the West, but here they took full ownership of their unique identity. It's very rare in K-pop that you have a band around for 20 years, and they definitely played a part in making K-pop mainstream."

Nicole and her son Arthur attended the concert to celebrate his 8th birthday 

Much of this multi-generational pull is driven by archival concert footage, TV appearances, and classic music videos circulating across social media platforms like TikTok and YouTube.

"Nostalgia can be built upon something people didn't even experience personally at the time," notes Um. 

"It comes through shared digital memory. YG Entertainment was early in leveraging digital platforms like YouTube, which built a lasting global fan foundation that has accumulated across two generations."

While the band refrained from directly addressing their past on stage, the emotional weight of their history peaked during the finale Still Life. 

As T.O.P's unmistakable baritone echoed through the stadium via recorded vocals, a visible wave of emotion swept across the crowd.

Addressing the venue during the encore, G-Dragon appeared clearly moved, admitting they never expected their music to leave such an enduring global mark 14 years after their London debut.

As fireworks lit up the night sky, the three members linked hands and bowed deeply - a triumphant moment for the group widely credited with helping reshape modern K-pop.

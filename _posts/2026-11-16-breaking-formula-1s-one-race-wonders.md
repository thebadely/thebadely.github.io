The 'One Grand Prix' Club - the stories behind the Formula 1 drivers who competed in just one race

You've been handed the chance of a lifetime.

A chance to sit in the cockpit, on the grid, at motorsport's top table: Formula 1. The very pinnacle of everything you've strived for in your whole life.

What would you do?

"I sang the Grand Prix theme tune while I was sat on the grid. I thought I was watching it on telly," says Tiff Needell - one of 166 lucky or unlucky drivers, depending on your standing in motorsport, who graced an F1 race only once.

There are many reasons why a driver may only get one chance to race elite sport's fastest and most sophisticated machines, and, although F1's super subs were more of a feature in the 1960s and 1970s, the helmet-for-hire phenomenon is alive and driving in ultra-professional, modern F1.

Just ask Japan's Ryo Hirakawa, who only last month achieved his "dream", external when he got behind the wheel of a Haas at the Mexico City Grand Prix following Esteban Ocon's controversial departure from the team.

"I just need to taste this moment. I'm really happy," Hirakawa said after scoring a point - admittedly, following a few demotions for others. "Just enjoy this moment and see what we can do."

Within a week there was nothing the Toyota factory driver could do as Haas' 2027 driver Rafael Camara was drafted in for the American team early.

Hirakawa is a reserve driver for Haas as a result of the team's association with Toyota - the Japanese being in the right place at the right time.

The percentage of F1 test and reserve drivers who make it to the grid is typically low, and those who do tend to be drafted in for one shot at very unlikely glory.

The days of the aristocrat who fancies a pootle around the track are gone, and years of preparation are required even to be standing in the pit garage.

Injuries, sponsorship fails and outright cheating are just some of the reasons why it does not work out for some hopefuls.

Needell, known better for his time behind the wheel on Top Gear and other auto TV shows, got his big break at the 1980 Belgian Grand Prix. But his journey to the paddock was initiated a few years earlier - in the media.

"I won a car in a magazine competition," he says. "I was the kid on the terraces, on the fences - and made it on to the grid. We had no money at all in our family, so I was a working civil engineer five days a week and a mechanic in my garage five nights a week. At the weekend I was the van driver, the mechanic and the racing driver.

"I bumbled my way up to the top of the national scene and then got offered a professional drive in Formula 3. And after two years of that I moved up into the [long since defunct] Formula 1 British championship.

"I was out at the Nurburgring trying to get an F1 drive when I got the call. I was hanging around the paddock trying to chat up an F2 team, trying to say I had a sponsor, which of course I didn't.

"Ensign called me and said: 'This weekend would you like to drive?' I rushed home from the Nurburgring in my orange Ford Capri, had half a day's test at Donington and on to qualifying for the Belgian GP at Zolder.

"I got my chance because Clay Regazzoni crashed and hurt his back at Long Beach.

"A lot of that in the olden days was due to injuries - chances would come up when someone had crashed and got injured. It was dangerous back then."

Needell was in, but this was old-school F1 where you had to pre-qualify to show you were fast enough to qualify for the Grand Prix itself.

"There was something like 30 drivers trying to pre-qualify, and somehow I made it to the back of the grid - 23rd in the Ensign. It was so much cheaper then - aluminium chassis and a Cosworth DFV engine and away you go."

While the back of the grid beckoned, a 29-year-old Needell was in very good company in the form of double world champion Emerson Fittipaldi, who was having a bad weekend in his Skol Fittipaldi car.

"I found myself on the back row of the grid, sitting by one of my big heroes. It was all a bit of a dream," says Needell.

In contrast to today's VIP treatment, Needell's parents and his girlfriend (now wife) drove to Belgium in the family car and watched from the crowd as he lined up on the grid.

"When the mechanics started the engine and left me sitting there - me and Emerson - I actually started humming the theme tune [to Fleetwood Mac's The Chain]," Needell says.

"For 12 laps I battled with Emerson for the honour of being last place at the 1980 Belgian Grand Prix. Then the engine broke."

A failed attempt to qualify for Monaco a week later put the brakes on his F1 dream and eventually resulted in Needell pursuing a career as a Le Mans racer and TV presenter.

Often the reason F1 careers could be so fleeting would be of a driver's own making.

Hans Heyer did not qualify for his one and only race - the 1977 German Grand Prix at Hockenheim - but he thought he would contest it anyway, starting from the pit lane for the ATS team in a car that was never supposed to be there.

The German may have achieved his dream at his home race, but it came with disqualification after he had already retired with gearbox failure on lap nine.

And what of Marco Apicella? It has taken 73 years, since Alberto Ascari triumphed, for Italy to celebrate one of its own F1 world champions in Kimi Antonelli as he secured the 2026 title in Mexico last month. 

For a country and a Ferrari team so important to the sport, that is a long time to wait - and Apicella was a good example of why it may have taken a while.

Rushed in at the last minute after only two hours of testing to replace a disgruntled, if experienced, Thierry Boutsen in the underwhelming 1993 Jordan, Apicella lasted approximately 800 metres of his home race after a multi-car collision during the first lap of the Italian Grand Prix at Monza.

That was the end of Apicella's F1 career and he would end up embarking on five unsuccessful attempts to win Le Mans.

So what's the best any of the one-hit wonders have done in their first and only grand prix?

There have been a smattering of points finishes over the years, and Dorino Serafini's shared second place on the podium with legend Ascari at Monza in the final race of F1's maiden season is difficult to top for an F1 one-shot.

But Germany's Markus Winkelhock is surely up there as one of sport's super-sub icons of the modern era.

A last-minute replacement in the newly acquired, yet painfully slow, Spyker team's car at the 2007 European Grand Prix at the Nurburgring, the touring car driver took up the F1 super sub's favoured qualifying position on the grid: last place.

F1 ran in Winkelhock's family - father Manfred contested 56 grands prix in the eighties before his death from a crash during a sportscar race in 1985.

But Markus went one better than his uncle Jo, who never contested an F1 race despite trying to qualify seven times.

But, by his own admission, Winkelhock was a long way from ready. "I had one day testing in Silverstone and one day in Paul Ricard," Winkelhock told Motorsport magazine., external

"The first couple of laps it felt like a PlayStation - so unreal, everything was so fast. You're accelerating till you reach the braking point.

"But after all the [practice] sessions I could feel my neck already.

"I told my mechanics to put some beddings on the side of the headrest, in case the neck is completely done, to lean on with the helmet."

Then came some early rain from the Eifel mountains and an inspired decision from the pitwall of a team with very little to lose.

Winkelhock was on exactly the right tyres, at exactly the right time, as the rain came down, and Lewis Hamilton and Jenson Button slid helplessly into the gravel trap along with several others.

Winkelhock's orange Spyker simply danced through the rivers of rainwater flowing across the tarmac, sitting 33 seconds ahead of the field - a country mile for F1.

He said: "I didn't know it was for the lead. I was just driving. At the point they told me, 'You're P1'. I really didn't understand anything that was going on because this race was really crazy."

But when the rain stopped, so did Winklehock - first swallowed up by the faster cars on a drying track, and then by a hydraulic issue on lap 15, having led for six laps.

Make no mistake, a lost F1 career is very often a driver's glittering career in the making in sportscars at Le Mans, or on the ovals of American Indycar.

But even back in the day when other sporting codes, such as rallying's frighteningly fast Group B, were at the height of their popularity, F1 was the pinnacle for most.

"Being a grand prix driver was where you wanted to go," concludes Needell. "But I always had my eye sideways for any other drives.

"Single-seaters are the best cars to drive and they are made and designed to race. And the ultimate is the grand prix car. Everything else is second best.

"I was so proud I got there on no sponsorship and no money. I suppose I was a bit annoyed I couldn't stay for longer.

"But as I always say, 'unluckier than a few, but luckier than millions'."

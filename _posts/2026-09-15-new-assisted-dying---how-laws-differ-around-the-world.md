According to new developments, Around 400 million people now live in countries which have legalised assisted dying.

Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Spain and Austria are among those which have changed the law in recent years, with some allowing help to die for those who are not terminally ill.

Just last month, France adopted an 'aid in dying' law for adults with incurable conditions causing them unbearable pain.

While most jurisdictions forbid medical help to die, assisted dying laws around the world vary.

Across the US, assisted dying - which some critics prefer to call assisted suicide - is legal in 13 out of 50 states, as well as in Washington DC. 

Oregon was one of the first places in the world to offer help to die for some patients, in 1997, and so has nearly 30 years' experience. It has become the model on which other US assisted dying laws have been framed.

In Oregon, assisted dying is open to terminally ill, external, mentally competent adults expected to die within six months - and must be signed off by two doctors. Since 1997, external, 5,520 people have received a prescription for a lethal dose of medication - with 3,691 deaths.

In a 2025 report, external, 61% of patients who ask for help to die had cancer, 14% had a neurological condition like motor neurone disease and 11% had heart disease.  Of the 400 patients who took a lethal dose of medication last year, the vast majority (89%) said loss of autonomy was a key concern, while others cited:

Loss of dignity (65%)

Losing control of bodily functions (47%)

Concern about being a burden on family and friends (43%)

Inadequate pain control (28%)

Financial implications of treatment (4%)

Oregon is important for supporters of assisted dying in England and Wales as they point out it has remained restricted to terminally ill adults since its introduction. However, opponents say some of the rules have been relaxed. A residency requirement has been lifted, which means it is open to people from outside the state. The number of assisted deaths has also risen substantially over the years.

Canada is the country often cited by opponents of assisted dying as an example of the so-called "slippery slope" - a place where assisted dying has been extended and made available to more people since it was first brought in. Medical assistance in dying (Maid), external was introduced in 2016, initially just for the terminally ill. 

This was amended in 2021 and extended to those experiencing "unbearable suffering" from an irreversible illness or disability.

It had been due to be extended to people with a mental illness but that is now on hold.

Critics say the more the law is widened, the more disabled and vulnerable people will be put at risk. There has also been a dramatic growth in the number of people using Maid.

Roughly one in every 20 deaths in Canada are now medically assisted, external, compared to about one in 100 in Oregon.

Across Europe, several countries have legalised assisted dying: Switzerland, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Spain, Austria, Portugal and, most recently, France.

Eligibility criteria vary widely. Some jurisdictions allow assisted dying for people who are not terminally ill, while others, including France, restrict it to adults with serious incurable illnesses nearing the end of life.

Portugal's law was approved in 2023 but is not yet in force because of court and regulatory delays.

In 2025, Tuscany became the first region of Italy to establish a legal process for medically assisted suicide.  Earlier rulings by the constitutional courts in both Italy and Germany removed criminal penalties for assisted suicide in certain circumstances.

The Netherlands and Belgium both legalised assisted dying more than 20 years ago for patients experiencing unbearable suffering from an incurable illness, including mental health issues. It has since been extended to children - the only European countries to allow this. Both allow euthanasia - or physician-assisted dying.

Spain and Austria legalised assisted dying for both terminal illness and intolerable suffering in 2021 and 2022, respectively. In Austria, the drugs must be self-administered, whereas in Spain a medical professional can administer them.

In England, Wales and Scotland, parliament has so far rejected assisted dying proposals. 

But assisted dying has already been legalised in one part of the British Isles.

In July, Royal Assent was given to a bill in Jersey. Unlike at Westminster and Holyrood, the legislation was introduced by the Jersey government, which says it will take about 18 months to set up the service. The first assisted death could happen late next year.  There will be a residency requirement of one year, in a bid to prevent so-called 'death tourism'.

The measure, which most sets apart the Jersey legislation from the rest of the British Isles, concerns the manner in which those eligible for an assisted death can end their lives.

Individuals will be able to choose to have a doctor administer a lethal dose intravenously, as happens in Spain, Canada, New Zealand and most of Australia.

This is, in effect, voluntary euthanasia. It has become the preferred method of assisted dying in jurisdictions which allow this choice.

The laws proposed at Westminster, Holyrood and Tynwald - Isle of Man - all state that death must be by self-administration, which usually means the individual swallowing a lethal dose.

The Isle of Man assisted dying bill was passed by Tynwald (the Manx parliament) in March last year.  As a UK Crown Dependency - like Jersey - it could not become law until granted Royal Assent.

This was declined earlier this year by the UK Ministry of Justice, but Tynwald has since passed an updated bill which has been sent back to the UK government for a second time.

If Royal Assent is granted, the Isle of Man expects the service to be operational next year.  There is a residency requirement of five years.

In the past few years voluntary assisted dying has become legal across most of Australia. Last month, the Northern Territory parliament became the last part of Australia to pass Voluntary Assisted Dying legislation.

In New Zealand, patients must be terminally ill and expected to die within six months. That is extended to 12 months for those with a neurodegenerative condition in much of Australia.

In both countries, patients can self-administer the lethal medication. But it can also be administered by a doctor or nurse, usually via an intravenous injection.

In South America, Uruguay passed a law in 2025 to permit assisted dying for adults with an incurable or terminal illness.  Across Latin America, Colombia, Ecuador and Cuba have decriminalised euthanasia.

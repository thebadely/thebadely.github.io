Experts revealed that Vickrum Digwa, who fatally stabbed 18-year-old Henry Nowak in Southampton in 2015, is also seeking a sentence review

Vickrum Digwa, the man jailed for killing Henry Nowak, has applied to appeal against his conviction.

Digwa, who stabbed the 18-year-old student with a large dagger in Belmont Road, Southampton, on 3 December 2025, has further asked the Supreme Court to review his sentence, his lawyers have confirmed.

It follows a recent hearing in the Court of Appeal in which the minimum term of his life sentence was increased from 21 to 25 years after lawyers for the Attorney General argued that it was unduly lenient.

The BBC understands Digwa wants to argue that his conviction for murder is unsafe on four grounds. The applications do not mean Digwa will automatically get any further hearings or reviews.

Imran Khan and Partners, Digwa's lawyers, said: "An application has been lodged seeking to appeal against Vickrum Singh Digwa's conviction.

"That application concerns the safety of the conviction and is separate from the proceedings concerning sentence."

Nowak, from Chafford Hundred in Essex, was stabbed to death with a 21cm (8in) blade that Digwa said he carried as part of his Sikh faith.

Vickrum Digwa, then 22, told police Henry had attacked and racially abused him, which led to Henry being handcuffed as he lay dying. The trial judge later found Digwa's claims of racism to be a lie.

Digwa was jailed for life at Southampton Crown Court in June with a minimum term of 21 years, before it was increased earlier this month. 

The BBC understands Digwa wishes to claim the trial judge wrongly excluded from evidence WhatsApp messages Nowak sent when he was about 13, which Digwa suggested displayed a history of racist language.

The messages were found by police during their review of Nowak's phone but the judge ruled them irrelevant and inadmissible and Digwa's attempt to present the student as racist was found to have been a lie.

Digwa is also seeking to appeal his conviction on the basis the jury should not have been told that police had secretly recorded him confessing to the stabbing. The recording, he argues, breaks evidence rules relating to such material.

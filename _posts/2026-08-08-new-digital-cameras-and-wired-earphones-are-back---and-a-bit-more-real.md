Whether it's digital cameras, iPods or wired earphones, does a renewed love for early-digital gadgets among some young people tell us something more?

It's been on the mind of Andrew Przybylski, professor of technology and human behaviour at the University of Oxford.

Underpinning a fondness for more basic tech is "people's desire on a very deep level to be in control of their experiences," he argues.

He says the modern digital world often leaves us inflicted by decision paralysis, external - the inability to choose because there are so many options.

When a new series comes out, he explains, "binge watching it automatically" often feels like a decision that is made for us.

In contrast, when you choose to turn on a digital camera and play with its settings or add an album to your iPod library "you move towards something you want versus it coming at you".

Andrew says "people want more than to just rent the things [and experiences] they're passionate about" and that buying your own music or taking your own pictures creates a sense of connection.

He references Xbox's server outage in late July, in which users couldn't play games even if they owned the discs and how "nobody liked that".

"Imagine if a book worked that way," he adds.

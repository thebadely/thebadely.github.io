It has been recently reported that Eating fruit and vegetable scraps such as peels and stalks could provide a health boost that most of us are missing, say UK researchers, external.

They say scraps are an overlooked source of dietary fibre and are encouraging people to try "root-to-tip" eating, including watermelon rind, pineapple core and butternut squash seeds.

Most adults do not get enough fibre. Official figures, external show only 4% meet the recommended 30g a day, despite its well-known benefits for gut health.

Fibre aids digestion, helps you feel fuller for longer and is linked to a lower risk of heart disease and bowel cancer., external

Skin and pips can be beneficial, the fibre researchers say

As a rule of thumb, the skins of fruit and veg pack more fibre than the insides. 

Eating a kiwi with the skin on, external provides 50% more fibre than having a peeled one, for example. 

For their study, the scientists at King's College London analysed 12 raw or uncooked fresh foods - beetroot, bell pepper, broccoli, butternut squash, cauliflower, celeriac, curly kale, fennel, kiwi fruit, leek, pineapple and watermelon. 

They found many discarded parts contained more fibre than the sections people typically eat.

Fibre is essential for your gut to work properly and helps prevent constipation.

Project lead Dr Rachel Gibson said the findings suggested people are throwing away a significant amount of fibre.

"This isn't just a nutritional loss, it's also a financial one - you're effectively throwing away a substantial portion of what you've paid for."

King's College London has been experimenting in its canteen, external, offering staff and students root-to-tip meals, such as banana skin curry. 

At home, people might want to try leaving skins on kiwi fruit, using broccoli stalks in stir-fries or adding bell pepper seeds to dishes, the researchers suggest.

She acknowledges some may be hesitant to try it. 

Some diners raised concerns during the canteen trial. 

Over the nine-week period, "root-to-tip" dishes made up a third of total daily hot menu sales at the university. 

Those choosing the new dishes were less likely to finish their meals than diners eating from the conventional menu.

Kirsten Jackson, a dietitian with the British Dietetic Association who specialises in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), says the food hacks are useful as long as produce is washed thoroughly.

She suggests blending pineapple cores into smoothies, but says the tough skins are not something she would recommend.

Some stalks may need to be cooked or finely chopped to make them more palatable, she adds.

"Just because a broccoli stalk doesn't fit into tonight's meal doesn't mean it needs to go in the bin. Chop it up, freeze it and add it to a soup or stir-fry another day," she said.

She advises increasing fibre intake gradually to avoid bloating, noting that people who have IBS may find it harder to tolerate.

And there are some things to avoid entirely, said the British Nutrition Foundation. 

Green potatoes and rhubarb leaves contain toxic compounds, while tough stalks and seeds may not be suitable for young children.

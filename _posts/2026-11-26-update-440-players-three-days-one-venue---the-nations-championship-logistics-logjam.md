Such are the countless details that go into an unprecedented logistical juggle.

The maths makes the bottleneck clear.

The Nations Championship brings together more than 550 players and support staff from the world's top 12 teams for six matches funnelled into three days and just one venue - Twickenham.

The new tournament has made new, unprecedented demands on organisers.

The first was to find enough accommodation in one corner of London, on a notoriously busy shopping weekend, kicked off by Black Friday sales.

A block booking was therefore made back at the start of the year to secure beds in hotels from Brentford, Guildford, Cobham and beyond.

Not all those establishments had experience of hosting elite rugby teams. 

So, chefs and team nutritionists convened to ensure that the hotels were braced with sufficient supplies of eggs, chicken and the rest, to cater for the high-energy, high-protein diets of rugby's best players.

The demands continue.

While their fellow hotel guests might hit the sights, Nations Championship sides hit the pitch.

Individual, private training facilities have had to be sourced for each team, with pitches hired out at local clubs, schools and universities.

Teams were given the choice of hotels and training grounds based on their world ranking at the start of the tournament in July, with world number ones South Africa nabbing the Teddington base that usually hosts England's opponents on a regular autumn internationals weekend.

The choice is all the more important given the logjam of matches means teams will do their eve-of-match captain's run session at their training ground, rather than, as traditional, at the match venue.

To get their players, staff and equipment between hotel, training base and Twickenham itself, organisers have given each team use of four vehicles; a team bus, a people carrier, a van for training kit and a truck for moving scrum machines and other heavy items.

Once they finally do arrive, Allianz Stadium, which has hosted Sevens, double-headers and schools finals days in the past, has enough changing rooms to comfortably accommodate four teams at once.

The higher-ranked team is given the home dressing room in each fixture.

Had England lost to New Zealand last weekend, they would be in the unfamiliar away dressing room. Victory however bumped them up to fourth, the All Blacks down to fifth and secured their usual spot.

Even if they dodge the traffic however, there is tunnel congestion to be dealt with.

The first match on each of the three days kicks off three and half hours before the headline fixture.

Barring extraordinary levels of intervention from the Television Match Official, the final whistle should blow in the first fixture before the team buses for those involved in the second arrive.

However, with post-match media duties, feeding and debriefing to be done, a window between five minutes and half an hour after kick-off in the second fixture has been identified for the teams involved in the first to make a hassle-free getaway.

One team has asked for permission to break with protocol. Scotland's players may stay to watch the South Africa v France grand final (16:40 GMT) as a squad social event after their own play-off against Argentina on Sunday (13:10).

Twickenham's ground staff are confident that, despite forecast rain on Saturday, the Allianz Stadium pitch will still be in good shape by the sixth match in 48 hours.

The pitch was relaid in the summer with a hi-tech reservoir installed below the surface to help precisely control drainage.

What happens in the stands is less predictable.

"We sell out at 99.9% of England men's games and we have a very clear picture of what we need to order for those," says Nils Braude, the managing director of the Twickenham Experience.

Nations Championship finals weekend will be different. Filling the 82,000-capacity Allianz Stadium, with only a week's notice on which teams will be playing when, has proved trickier.

It is estimated that Italy v Fiji and Ireland v Australia - Friday's double-header - may play out to a half-full stadium.

Braude is dealing with different numbers, demographics and demands.

"If you are a member of the public that doesn't get to go to rugby very often, now is your chance to come in," he adds.

"We are anticipating it is going to be much more akin to a general event-goer or a Red Roses game, so a more family orientated, mixed generational crowd.

"That causes a bit more just thinking about what we order

"New fans generally arrive a bit earlier than a rugby fan who has their habits well established.

"The new fans tend to eat more, particularly if it's a long day. If they are arriving an hour or two before the first game and leaving an hour after the last game, they are here for a significant amount of time.

"So having enough food on site to satisfy that demand is a key.

"As for drinks, we're known for serving a lot of Guinness, and so we can always do that. But we are anticipating a crowd that tends to look for more cocktails, Proseccos, non-alcoholic drinks and soft drinks, so we do ramp those up a little bit for these type of games."

While the stadium's bars, which number close to 80, are loaded with enough drink to see out the first two days of the Nations Championship, there are beer kegs stored in the car parks around the stadium in place to fill any unexpected shortfall.

Equally Twickenham's partner breweries and suppliers are on alert to truck in more if required.

That restocking is part of a 24-hour operation that will be in place across the Nations Championship finals weekend.

Chefs tend to be first on site from around 05:00, with a central kitchen preparing meals for the stadium's hospitality suites. After the match has taken place and the crowd has cleared, the cleaning crew descend.

They will work through the stadium seat by seat in the couple of hours after the final whistle and then to concourses and removing empties, rubbish and hospitality table linen.

Deliveries happen overnight to take advantage of an empty venue and the clearer roads around it.

For a stadium keen to host more events throughout the year, it is also a chance to show its consideration for residential neighbours, even on compressed timeframes.

The crash of glass recycling and reversing beep of forklift trucks will be restricted to certain hours.

Braude is also keen to ensure his own team get the rest and recovery they need over a weekend that comes on the back of a busy autumn.

But alongside the challenges, there is an opportunity.

"It's exciting," says Braude. "It is certainly an event that shows up. You can really flex your muscles as a venue when you have these type of events."

Brawn and brain is being deployed well beyond the white lines this weekend.

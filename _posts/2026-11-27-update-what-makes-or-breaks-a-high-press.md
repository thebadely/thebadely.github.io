A good high press has been the basis for some of the most successful sides in modern football but a well-oiled pressing machine has many moving parts - all requiring close attention.

When an out-of-possession plan is implemented poorly, entire seasons can fall apart.

So, what determines whether a press works or fails? And how are the always-adapting Premier League teams exploiting high presses this season?  

When a team decides to press, they must first establish their intention. This informs how the players act.

Speaking to DAZN Portugal, Brighton manager Fabian Hurzeler was asked about whether a good press relied more on physicality or courage.

"A really good question," he began. 

"When you want to press high you need the courage and mindset to do it. Our mindset is we press to score, we don't just press to win the ball back."

In coaching, clarity is key - particularly when it comes to creating alignment in the squad and Hurzeler is deliberate in simplifying big ideas when delivering messages to his players, a skill he practised as a radio pundit during the World Cup.

"[Punditry] helps you as a coach in that you have to summarise things in a short and easy way so that everyone understands," he said. "Football might get more complex but simplicity counts."

Hurzeler's "pressing to score" is a phrase that communicates many ideas in a memorable three words: intensity, being proactive, and immediate forward-thinking on winning the ball. 

It also frames pressing in a way that makes it easy for attackers to buy into, in the form of goals. 

"You want commitment from everyone on the pitch," Hurzeler explained. 

"If just one has doubts, the whole press is gone."

The Red Bull school of coaching uses a similarly succinct phrase - "the last step" to address these ideas around intention, collectiveness and aggression.

Summarised by Tactx, whose coaches have worked under the Red Bull model, the last step refers to "players [being] coached to attack through the opponent, using the final step to lunge aggressively at the ball".

"This action commits the defender fully and is often the decisive moment that allows possession to be won."

Pressing to engage rather than just to close distances ensures the team in possession aren't given the time or space to play through otherwise half-hearted pressure.

When discussing how good or bad a team is at pressing, how much they run is often brought up. But the distance a team covers fails to paint the full picture when it comes to the effectiveness of a team's pressing strategy. 

Liverpool's current boss Andoni Iraola was asked about how he interpreted physical data last year.

"It's not 'we run a lot, so we are good,' no," he said. "The high-speed running, the sprint speeds [matter for us] - not total distance covered.

"[Some] teams that do not run a lot get very good results because they have [their] way of playing."

Sprints matter more than distance covered for Iraola's side, who have clear ideas of how to press, looking to win the ball back through a sudden and intense pressure from familiar positions they are well prepared for.

If the conditions to implement this press aren't on, the wingers can be seen dropping off, forming a more compact defensive shape with the likes of Alexander Isak and Florian Wirtz defending in a front two for longer periods of the match. 

It's smart. Liverpool look to maximise the effectiveness of when they do press, with full commitment expected in each sprint, with players conserving their energy in moments a press is less likely to succeed.

With pressing being a front-footed idea, coaches may throw caution to the wind but this can leave them exposed in ways teams have become better at exploiting.

Hurzeler touched on the need for balance when pressing.

"I always say in certain moments you need to have the courage to take risk but you need to have the discipline to control the risk," he said.

For Brighton, this looks like certain players abandoning man-to-man marking when their respective attacker attempts to drag them into awkward positions. 

Instead, Brighton will protect the centre of the pitch on top of their man-to-man principles which helps them stay compact.

In yellow, we can see the man-oriented defence of Brighton against Arsenal. The players highlighted in white are taking a more central position designed to protect dangerous areas of the pitch. This is an example of how impressive coaches are combining man-to-man pressing with zonal ideas

When the defending side aren't compact, spaces exist for the attacking side to target. When players step up to press, these gaps can arise in between the lines.

Again, Brighton manage to plug these gaps well by pressing hard, reducing the time opponents have to play passes into midfield, while also encouraging players to make vertical jumps up the pitch, covering players left by teammates who have pressed higher up the pitch.

As Declan Rice passes the ball backwards, Brighton's players press forward, locking on to the player in front of them

When teams lack vertical compactness - not pressing with enough intensity and without making the necessary steps up the pitch - the space left in midfield has been exploited by clever sides, such as Leeds.

In their 4-1 loss to Daniel Farke's men, Chelsea looked to press high, with attackers and midfielders running up the pitch to apply pressure.

But when the ball went back the other way, these same players struggled.

The idea behind playing over a team's press isn't always to make the ball stick at the feet of a target man, although this helps. Instead space is found in the gaps between the pressing side's lines. 

Recognising when teammates are likely to win headers or clip the ball forwards, midfielders are able to turn and run in the direction the ball is heading in, positioned ready to win second balls in numbers.

By comparison, the pressing side's midfielders, having to apply forward pressure before turning around to track back are often slower to contest second balls.

Leeds' midfielders have oriented themselves well to form a net around where the ball may drop. The Chelsea midfielders and attackers, starting from further away and having to turn around, aren't well placed to contest for the loose ball

The same ideas are seen again. The Leeds' midfielder, under insufficient pressure, is able to play the ball forward quickly. This catches Chelsea's midfield line high up the pitch. Chelsea's Romeo Lavia has to turn before he can get back into position whilst Leeds attacking midfielders are closer to the ball, facing forwards

By this point, it will be clear that pressing is influenced by countless variables but it is worth highlighting one final factor; teams need solutions when pressing with one fewer player than the attacking side.

This most obviously happens when the opposition goalkeeper is involved in build-up play. 

Here, the team in possession have 11 options against the 10 pressing players.

Obvious solutions come in the form one of the forward attackers pressing a centre-back at an angle which usually forces them to pass it back to the goalkeeper. 

At that point, the same pressing player will continue his run, now pressing the keeper. 

By curving their run and pressing at an angle, the idea is that the keeper will struggle to pass it back to the now unmarked centre-back. 

This is generally true but again, good teams have found smart solutions to access this player. 

Charalampos Kostoulas first presses Gabriel Magalhaes, who passes the ball to his keeper, David Raya. Kostoulas then curves his run, blocking off Raya's pass back to Gabriel

One solution comes when the keeper passes the ball into a central player who then bounces the pass into the unmarked centre-back. This comes with risk given that the central player is usually marked. But sudden darts towards the ball can give them the time and space to play the bounce pass required.

Under some pressure, Rice darts towards the ball, gaining some separation from his marker. He gestures for the ball knowing he can play a first time pass to Gabriel who is unmarked

What happens instead is Raya, under immense pressure, defaults to playing long. His pass ends up out of play.

A riskier but more impressive solution is when the keeper is willing and able to avoid the opponents attempt to force him in one direction, instead playing the ball back the other way themselves.

Queens Park Rangers' Pierce Charles, on loan from Manchester City, has done this better than most in world football, and his quality was clearest against Swansea's excellent press. 

By playing against the direction of Swansea's press, to his unmarked teammate, he was able to immediately disorganise their press.

QPR's right-sided defender passes the ball back to Charles. The Swansea striker, first pressing the defender, angles his body to discourage Charles from playing back to the unmarked player. He closes Charles down as he does this

Swansea's press is designed to force Charles to his left but he bravely plays back the other way

By finding the unmarked man on the other side in space, Swansea have to reorganise their defensive shape

The sandpaper scandal, one of the biggest controversies in the history of Test cricket, continues to engulf Australian sport.

Fresh evidence from Cameron Bancroft - the culprit of the ball-tampering in South Africa in 2018 - has heaped pressure on Australia captain Pat Cummins, former coach Darren Lehmann and Cricket Australia.

In a detailed account of more than 4,000 words published in the West Australian newspaper, Bancroft alleges all the Australia players and coaches present in Cape Town eight years ago knew of the plot.

Bancroft's explosive testimony supports claims made by Usman Khawaja, David Warner and Chadd Sayers.

However, Cummins denied Khawaja's version of events in his own extraordinary news conference in Sydney last week. Cummins spoke to the media for 68 minutes in an address broadcast on live television.

Here, BBC Sport collects the most recent accounts of players and coaches involved in the saga that became widely known as sandpapergate.

Batter Khawaja played all four Tests in South Africa and a total of 88 for Australia before retiring from international cricket earlier this year.

Revelations in his autobiography reignited debate around the sandpaper scandal and made headlines across the globe.

"It was all of us," Khawaja said. "Not the batters, not the bowlers, not the staff. All of us.

"In an ideal world, I don't want to do this. I don't want to be the one who comes out and talks about the truth. The issue is that I genuinely thought after eight years the truth would have been out by now.

"Even in the change rooms when I was talking to them after the game. I said, 'Take this as a team'.

"We did this as a team. We knew what was going on, and we have to take it as a team, and so I was always of that stance."

Whole Australia team knew of sandpaper use - Khawaja

Warner was Australia's opening batter and vice-captain during the 2018 series. He, along with captain Steve Smith and Bancroft, were the three players punished for the ball-tampering.

The Cricket Australia (CA) investigation in 2018 found Warner developed the plan and that he instructed Bancroft to carry out the cheating.

After serving his ban, Warner returned to play a further 133 times for Australia across formats, including three Ashes series and the 2023 World Cup win. He retired from international cricket in 2024.

In response to Khawaja's book, Warner said: "I thank you for what you've done. It's 100% accurate what you've said. Now everyone knows the truth."

He also accused Cricket Australia of a cover-up, describing the CA investigation as "rushed" and botched".

"As far as l am aware, CA did not interview Josh Hazlewood, Mitchell Starc or Nathan Lyon in their 2018 investigation," Warner said.

"If that's correct, then how can CA push the idea that the bowlers didn't know?"

Warner accuses Cricket Australia of 'cover-up'

The Cape Town Test was the 13th of Cummins' Test career. A key bowler, he would go on to be named Australia's Test captain in 2021. It is a position he still holds.

Prior to Khawaja's book, Cummins and the other bowlers from Cape Town - Hazlewood, Starc and Lyon - had always denied knowledge of the plan to use sandpaper.

In an extraordinary news conference last week, Cummins called Khawaja's claims that the whole team knew about the plan "100% wrong" and "utter nonsense".

"I said that consistently for the last eight years," Cummins said. "I did not know that was happening.

"He says that I and others were architects of a scheme to ball-tamper with sandpaper. That's complete and utter nonsense."

Asked if he knew of any plans at any stage to tamper with the ball during the series against South Africa, Cummins said: "No."

Cummins also said he did not remember a meeting where Khawaja had called on the team to take collective responsibility - something his former team-mate claimed.

Complete and utter nonsense - Cummins

Opening batter Bancroft was playing his eighth Test in Cape Town, aged 25. He was the player caught using sandpaper on the field.

He played another two Tests after serving his ban and is still playing for Western Australia. He turns 34 this week.

In 2021, Bancroft said it was "self-explanatory" whether the bowlers knew of the plan, and on Wednesday he backed Khawaja's account, saying Cummins' news conference made him "feel angry, betrayed and alone".

Like Khawaja, Bancroft said sandpaper was first used during the second Test against South Africa â the match before he was caught - and also said he had conversations with the Australia seamers about whether the ball was "swinging enough".

He said the entire playing group and staff had "celebrations" about the effect of the sandpaper. He also claimed that coach Lehmann and bowling coach David Saker were present in the initial discussions and that Bancroft's role was also discussed in a team meeting before day three.

In an account published by the West Australian on Wednesday, Bancroft wrote: "All the bowlers gave me feedback if my work on the ball was working. It was helpful.

"Is it going yet? It's not going yet, it needs more. So I would use the sandpaper more on the ball.

"There was even a time I was told to stop out of fear what I was doing was becoming too obvious."

Bancroft sandpaper claims heap pressure on Cummins

Lehmann was Australia coach at the time of the scandal. He was cleared by the CA investigation and said he had "no prior knowledge" of the plan to ball-tamper.

He resigned after the series and has been Northamptonshire coach since the start of the 2025 English season.

After Khawaja's revelations, Lehmann said: "I don't think everyone would have known [about the use of sandpaper]. You do know ball-tampering is going on because it's been happening for 150 years."

After Cricket Australia said it would hold an open forum, Lehmann said he has "nothing to hide".

Speaking on radio coverage of the third one-day international against England after Bancroft's account was published, Lehmann said: "I need to sit back, process it and work through each allegation.

"That's what I'll be doing over the next few days.

"I need to have a really good read of it. I need to make sure I'm saying what I want to say."

Smith, one of the greatest batters of his generation, was Australia's captain at the time of the scandal.

After serving a 12-month ban, he returned for the World Cup and Ashes the following year and has stood in as captain at times since.

He was found to have been aware of the plan to cheat and to mislead the public and match officials.

Smith, who broke down in tears when apologising at a subsequent news conference, as cameras captured the emotional toll of the moment, has not spoken publicly since Khawaja's book was published.

Speaking about the episode in September this year, he said: "That was a traumatic experience in my life and when I hear the flash of a camera it makes me revisit that in a way.

"I've tried to kind of erase a lot of 2018 and what happened. I can't remember a lot about the series. I've tried to block most of it out of my memory bank."

Marsh was bowling when Bancroft was caught using sandpaper on the ball in Cape Town. As an all-rounder, he was not part of the 2021 bowlers' statement.

Marsh was captain during Australia's recent ODI series against England and refused to answer questions on the topic before the series opener.

"I am not here to share mine [story] today," he said.

"I respect Uzzie [Khawaja] has told his story; my focus is on tomorrow. I am not here to go back on South Africa."

Following the conclusion of the series, Marsh did not take part in his post-match news conference, with 26-year-old bowler Jack Edwards taking his place.

Paine was Australia's wicketkeeper during the South Africa series and was made captain when Smith was banned.

He served in the role until he stood down in 2021 over a historical investigation into sexually explicit text messages sent to a female colleague.

In his autobiography, Paine said the suggestion everyone in the Australia dressing room must have known about the sandpaper use is "rubbish" and claimed he first found out when images appeared on the big screen in Cape Town.

"Everyone out there was shocked when they looked up on the big screen and saw Cameron Bancroft with a piece of sandpaper in his hand," Paine wrote. "I was stunned. We all were.

"My heart sank, I was thinking, 'What the f***?' A sense of dread came over us all."

Bancroft said he discussed whether the ball was reversing with Paine during the second Test.

Saker was Australia's bowling coach during the South Africa series.

Bancroft alleged Saker was present for discussions about the use of sandpaper and told the batter he needed to do more to the ball.

Saker was also England bowling coach as recently as last month's tri-series in Pakistan. He was an influential part of England's 2010-11 Ashes win.

In 2021, reflecting on the sandpaper saga, Saker said: "Obviously a lot of things went wrong at that time. There was a lot of people to blame. It could have been me to blame, it could have been someone else. It could have been stopped and it wasn't, which is unfortunate.

"Cameron's a very nice guy. He's just doing it to get something off his chestâ¦ He's not going to be the last. You could point your finger at me, you could point your finger at Boof [Lehmann]. Could you point it at other people? Of course you could.

"The disappointing thing is it's never going to go away. Regardless of what's said. We all know that we made a monumental mistake. The gravity wasn't as plain until it all came out."

Starc was an integral part of the vaunted Australia attack in Cape Town and remains so in the present day. He is ranked as the number one bowler in Test cricket by the International Cricket Council.

Left-armer Starc repeatedly denied knowledge of the sandpaper plot, including in the statement released with Cummins, Hazlewood and Lyon in 2021, and in a news conference in the days after Khawaja's claims emerged.

Speaking on 10 November, Starc said: "Obviously, there's differing opinions of eight years ago. We were heavily grilled through the process at the time, and since, and I guess I won't speak on behalf of the bowlers, but where I stand, my opinion hasn't changed. My recollection hasn't changed from what we said in 2021.

"I'll stick to what I said five years ago, what I said at the time, eight years ago. Clearly, there's different versions from different people, and I'll leave it where I left it back then."

Along with Cummins and Starc, Hazlewood and Lyon have remained part of Australia's first-choice bowling unit in the eight years since the sandpaper scandal, forming one of the greatest four-man attacks in Test history.

Seamer Hazlewood and off-spinner Lyon joined Cummins and Starc in releasing a statement in 2021, responding to an interview given by Bancroft to the Guardian newspaper when he said the bowlers' involvement in the sandpaper plot was "self-explanatory".

Neither Hazlewood nor Lyon has spoken publicly since the fresh claims from Khawaja, Warner and Bancroft emerged.

Their statement released with Cummins and Starc in 2021 said: "We pride ourselves on our honesty. So it's been disappointing to see that our integrity has been questioned by some journalists and past players in recent days in regard to the Cape Town Test of 2018.

"We did not know a foreign substance was taken onto the field to alter the condition of the ball until we saw the images on the big screen at Newlands.

"We respectfully request an end to the rumour-mongering and innuendo. It has gone on too long and it is time to move on."

A swing bowler, Sayers played his only Test for Australia after the sandpaper controversy erupted â the fourth and final match of the series against South Africa in Johannesburg.

Sayers, who retired from first-class cricket in 2021, spoke to Australian radio station SEN in the aftermath of Khawaja's claims, to corroborate the former batter's account.

Sayers claimed to have removed sandpaper from the field in the second Test against South Africa, the match before Bancroft was caught. He also backed up Khawaja's assertion that the entire Australia squad discussed the sandpaper issue after it was revealed in Cape Town.

Speaking on 15 November, Sayers said: "I am comfortable sitting here that I believe everyone in that side knew what was going on and what was being used.

"It was used in two Test matches and there were discussions in the changing rooms.

"After the occurrence in Cape Town, we all sat in the changing rooms after that day's play and, probably for 60 to 90 minutes, we talked about what had happened and I guess where to next."

Spinner Holland, who retired in 2021, was also a back-up bowler on the trip.

He also said there was a meeting held in the fallout from Cape Town about players taking responsibility.

"Once we got back to the hotel we did sit as a group [to discuss] what had happened and who was going to take the blame," Holland told newspaper The Australian.

"To Davey's [Warner's] credit, from what I remember, he did put his hand up and say he would take some of the responsibility, but not all of it.

"To be sitting there and seeing three blokes hung out to dry when you are supposed to be part of a team was a pretty hard watch."

Australia's Sayers 'took sandpaper from field'

Batter Handscomb was Australia's 12th man on the day Bancroft was caught, making frequent trips between the field and the dressing room.

Bancroft's account alleges Handscomb was told by Lehmann, via walkie-talkie, to "get the sandpaper off Bangers [Bancroft] now".

Last week, following Khawaja's claims but before Bancroft spoke, Handscomb was asked for his recollections after making a century for Victoria in the Sheffield Shield against Western Australia.

On 11 November, Handscomb said: "I appreciate you have to ask these questions. I've had a nice day today, it's not really something that I want to comment on right now. I'll sort of just let the powers of be do their thing."

Former England batter Hick was part of the Australia backroom team on the South Africa tour of 2018.

Hick was made redundant by Cricket Australia as part of a series of cost-cutting measures in 2020.

Speaking to the Sydney Morning Herald last week, Hick said: "I have seen the various statements around the coaches, and I'm very disappointed with the broad nature of them.

"Khawaja puts it simply: 'It was all of us. Not the batters, not the bowlers, not the staff. All of us'. To me, what Khawaja is implying here is absolutely false.

"In regard to any discussions around the ball-tampering in Port Elizabeth or Cape Town, I will say in true honesty that I did not hear or know anything.

"My first knowledge of all this was, like many, when it appeared on the big screen in Cape Town as I was sat on the outside balcony at the time."

Cadence Kim from Dallas lives the reality of being an avid Formula 1 fan in the United States, in the three diverse locations the sport now frequents. She will be among the 300,000 spectators around the iconic Las Vegas Strip this weekend.

Las Vegas joined the F1 calendar in 2023, a year after Miami, while Austin has been home to the United States Grand Prix since 2012.

Kim has put in the hard yards travelling across the Atlantic to witness F1 in its European heartland. Now she is reaping the rewards of owners Liberty Media investing heavily in the three US races, which have all recently been awarded new long-term contracts. 

"Following F1 from the US used to mean accepting a massive barrier to entry," Kim explains.

"It meant setting an early alarm and trying to explain to room mates why you were yelling at 20 rich men driving funny-looking cars very quickly. It was more of a niche sport with no natural gathering places for a community to watch. What bar is open at that time?

"Having three domestic races has changed everything. It has shifted F1 from a purely broadcast experience into something tangible. It becomes a week-long experience where a fan gets to engage with a like-minded community, attend parties in the evening, and do a meet-and-greet. You can walk a track, sit in a grandstand, purchase the overpriced merch, hear the engines and literally feel a part of something."

The ATX GP club arranges watch parties for all races but when the Circuit of the Americas in Austin is hosting the US Grand Prix, the community arrives en masse

The community Kim is attached to is the ATX GP Club, formed in Austin by Maria Rodriguez and Julia Wezio, where watch parties help fans learn more about the sport and the drivers. With the longest running of the US races on their doorstep, at the Circuit of the Americas, she's ideally placed to compare all three venues for the fan experience.

"Vegas is a party," exclaims Kim. "The Nevada desert gets cold at night, so the cowboy hat doesn't make the trip, but socks, a hoodie and gloves do. 

"Many of the teams have hotel partnerships so lobbies become team activation zones, and you might end up sharing an elevator with an engineer. The fan flow is well managed with everyone being funnelled through specific pedestrian walkways, but if you do have a ticket to watch the race at the track you are limited to staying only in that one specific zone. 

"Vegas is known for its dining but that doesn't make it to the track. Ticketing forces you to consider some pretty rigid trade-offs. For example, sitting at Turn One means missing the musical artists and activations by the Sphere.

"As for Austin, you aren't restricted to a single assigned area but can walk almost the entire perimeter of the track. With each of the three races you get a local vibe integrated into the entertainment. It's not an artificially manufactured aesthetic in Austin, you're getting real Texas BBQ, smoked brisket and bedazzled cowboy hats.

"I've been once to Miami and would not consider going back unless it became a night race. The South Florida sun bakes both the track and the attendees during the daytime. Preparing for an outdoor trek is the best mindset. Treat it like a multi-day hike where you'll be logging 30,000 steps each day so comfortable shoes and water with electrolyte packets are a must."

Justin Bell hosts his F1 podcast from the Wynn resort on the Vegas Strip

The excitement level of racing at the Las Vegas Grand Prix in its opening three years has been as bright as the neon lights on the Strip. The debut race in 2023 was an instant classic, with the lead changing hands five times, and drama all the way to the line as Charles Leclerc overtook Sergio Perez for second place, behind winner Max Verstappen.

It was instant payback for F1, who had invested more than Â£500m in buying land and promoting the event. Despite complaints from some local residents and businesses over the disruption to the city streets, the gamble has paid dividends, according to Vegas-based podcast host and nine-time Le Mans 24 Hours driver Justin Bell.

"I was telling people, 'just wait'. It's like a big piece of Ikea furniture â once you do it once, it's so much quicker and more efficient the next time," Bell tells BBC Sport.

"The promoters did listen to the locals and worked out ways to get them involved, by offering discounted tickets and attempting to minimise the disruption. Let's face it, Las Vegas exists for one reason and that is to entertain visitors, and with the extraordinary economic impact it has I am sure the pros outweigh the cons for almost everyone involved."

That economic impact has been valued at $3.2bn for the Southern Nevada region over the three years, with each of the 175,000 visitors from outside Las Vegas spending an average of $2,400 on non-racing activities, according to figures from the 2024 race.

Bell, who presents the Drive to Wynn podcast from the luxury resort on the Strip, and used to guide celebrities like Drew Barrymore and Steve Carell in race cars on the Jay Leno Show, thinks Vegas has the perfect opportunity to position itself as the premier US race, despite it being the youngest.

"Vegas has such a leg up on the other two locations as a global entertainment hub, that aligns so well with the direction that F1 is heading as a brand," he says. "Miami is sexy and in theory has much going for it, but the track itself isn't in Miami so there are the usual inconveniences in terms of transportation.

"In contrast, I always recommended Austin to F1 newcomers as a track to visit first, because it is a purpose-built facility that is so easy to spectate at, with tons of room to walk around and watch cars from every angle all while eating a hotdog.

"From the first laps from 2023, it became evident that the Vegas track design was going to be great for F1. The mix of tight corners with that long straight down the iconic Strip invites breathtaking overtaking. Each successive year gets better."

One of the burning questions, as to whether F1 can sustain its upward growth curve of popularity in the United States, is whether an American driver needs to be part of the elite 24 on the grid.

Logan Sargeant was the last American to drive in an F1 grand prix, in the Netherlands in August 2024. Contenders for future seats include Cadillac reserve driver Colton Herta, Jak Crawford and Ugo Ugochukwu, the 2026 Formula 3 champion.

"Thanks to Drive to Survive, this new breed of American F1 fan is attracted to the global superstar drivers," says Bell.

"They are exciting, handsome and wealthy. If the right American driver does make it to F1 he has to be a rock star both on and off the track. Colton might be fast enough but is he a media darling? I am not convinced that unless whomever he is ticks all those boxes, that it will really make a difference to the US fanbase. Americans like winners, not just participants."

Kim agrees that F1 is inherently global: "It doesn't bother me at all. I am a fan of an Australian and a Dutch driver while being an American with a Polish background. When I moved from Austin to Dallas this past summer, I wasn't handed a welcome kit by the Dallas Cowboys and told that now they expect my support. 

"US representation already exists with Haas and Cadillac, but I don't think that we need an American driver on the grid for Americans to tune in and show up."

Crawford, Aston Martin's reserve driver, is one of those aiming to be that next American F1 star. Speaking to BBC Sport, he sees a growing percentage of younger drivers already leaning towards F1 ahead of the traditional Nascar and IndyCar series.

"I have a lot of kids and kart drivers coming up to me, and say that I inspire them, and I'm not on the grid full time. So I can't even imagine what that would be like if I was," Crawford says.

"I went to the first Austin race, in 2012, as a seven-year old. Back then it was hard to find how to watch F1 in the United States. Over time, as I raced in Europe, I became more passionate about it, and I grew up hand in hand with the popularity of F1 in the US.

"Austin is a phenomenal circuit. I'm biased as I'm from Texas. Las Vegas is cool, as a night race. I raced a lot there in karting, on a street circuit, and it's very cool driving past the big casinos at night."

Analysis from BBC Radio 5 Live F1 commentator Harry Benjamin

Las Vegas is the Marmite of the three US races for me. You either love it or hate it, and somehow I manage to feel both ways about it over the course of the same weekend.

I've always struggled slightly with Vegas as a destination. Unless you're there for a very particular sort of weekend, I think the novelty can wear off surprisingly quickly. It's an extraordinary place to look at, but outside the circuit and paddock, the sensory overload can become exhausting. There are only so many flashing lights, casinos and enormous hotels you can take before you start craving somewhere a little more normal.

The timings don't help either. Everything happens so late, and the desert temperatures can be surprisingly cold once the sun goes down. Combined with the expense of the weekend, it isn't necessarily the easiest or most relaxing grand prix to attend.

However, one huge advantage of Vegas is that you barely need to get in a car once you've arrived. Apart from the journey between the airport and your hotel, so much of the grand prix experience is within walking distance. Compared with the traffic in Miami, that's a real luxury.

And of course, the moment you see Formula 1 cars racing down the Las Vegas Strip at night, an awful lot of those complaints disappear.

It is genuinely one of the most spectacular sights in motorsport. We've become so accustomed to seeing Formula 1 cars at purpose-built circuits or traditional street tracks, but watching them fly past some of the most recognisable landmarks in the world, illuminated by the lights of Vegas, is pretty extraordinary.

They really know how to put on a show, too. The entertainment doesn't simply stop when the chequered flag falls. The post-race spectacle is another part of the experience, and it's difficult to think of another grand prix that embraces that side of Formula 1 quite so enthusiastically.

For all my reservations about Vegas as a destination, I completely understand why someone would want to experience the race at least once. There really is nothing else like it.

Overall, Austin is where I'd send someone who loves the racing, Miami is where I'd send someone who wants the best all-round grand prix holiday and Las Vegas is where I'd send someone who wants to experience something they won't forget.

For me, Miami wins, although there's something about Austin that still feels like the soul of American Formula 1. And despite all my complaints about Vegas, there's absolutely no denying the spectacle of seeing those cars flying down the Strip at night.

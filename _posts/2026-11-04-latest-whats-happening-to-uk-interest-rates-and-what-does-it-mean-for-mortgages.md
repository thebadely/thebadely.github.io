According to new developments, Policymakers at the Bank of England are expected to raise UK interest rates for the first time in more than three years at their next meeting.

Most analysts believe the Bank rate will go up from 3.75% to 4%, following holds at the previous six meetings.

Before the US and Israel launched strikes against Iran in February, rates had been expected to fall this year, but the economic fallout from the conflict has pushed up inflation across the globe.

Interest rates affect mortgage, credit card and savings rates for millions of people.

An interest rate tells you how much it costs to borrow money, or the reward for saving it.

The Bank of England's base rate is what it charges other banks and building societies to borrow money, which influences what they charge their own customers for mortgages as well as the interest rate they pay on savings.

When inflation is above that target, the Bank typically puts rates up. 

The idea is to encourage people to spend less, reducing demand for goods and services and limiting price rises. 

The Bank of England's base rate rose to 5.25% in August 2023. It remained at that level until August 2024, when the Bank started cutting. 

Five cuts brought rates down to 4%, before the Bank held rates at its meetings in September and November 2025. 

It then cut in December 2025 before holding rates steady in January, March, April, June, July and September 2026. 

Meanwhile, the main UK inflation measure, CPI, has fallen significantly since the October 2022 high of 11.1%, which was caused by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

However, the rate has been heading back up and is expected to go higher. It was 3.5% in the year to September 2026, up from 3.1% the previous month and well above the central bank's 2% target.

The Office for National Statistics (ONS), which tracks UK inflation, blamed the acceleration on sharp rises in petrol and diesel prices.

The US-Israel war with Iran has put up energy and fuel costs around the world, which has increased the pace of price rises more generally.

At the start of the year, the Bank had been expected to cut interest rates twice in 2026, with the first drop predicted to come in March or April.

However, the increase in fuel prices and inflation after the outbreak of the conflict has upended all of this. Oil prices initially rose sharply as a result of disruption to supplies in the region, but dropped back when various ceasefires were agreed. 

Oil prices rose again when the US and Iran resumed attacks in the Strait of Hormuz in July. 

UK household energy bills rose after the increases in the price cap in July and October, while global oil prices have risen again recently. 

Given the uncertainty, many analysts think UK interest rates will rise at the November meeting and again early next year.

Bank of England interest rates also influence the amount charged on credit cards, new bank loans and car loans.

Lenders can decide to raise their own interest rates if Bank hikes make borrowing costs more expensive.

Equally, they can cut their own rates if Bank cuts make borrowing cheaper.

The Bank base rate also affects how much savers earn on their money.

A rising base rate is likely to mean an increase in the returns offered to savers by banks and building societies and vice versa. 

As of 4 November, Moneyfacts said the average rate for an easy access savings account based on a balance of Â£10,000 was 2.58%. The average rate for an easy access cash ISA was 2.79%.

The average rate for customers who were prepared to lock their money away for a year was 4.48%. 

Changes in rates particularly affect those who rely on the interest from their savings to top up their income.

In recent years, the UK has had one of the highest interest rates in the G7 - the group representing the world's seven largest so-called "advanced" economies.

In June 2024, the European Central Bank (ECB) started cutting the main interest rate for the eurozone from an all-time high of 4%, falling to 2% in June 2025.

However, in June 2026, the ECB raised rates to 2.25% as it reacted to the Iran war, before raising them again to 2.5% in September.

The US central bank - the Federal Reserve - raised rates for the first time in three years at its September meeting, the third under the new chair Kevin Warsh.

It meant rates rose to 3.75%-4% from 3.5%-3.75%, following three cuts since September 2025. 

US President Donald Trump had repeatedly attacked the previous Fed chair Jerome Powell for not cutting rates. 

Warsh said the rise was because "inflation is too high and has been for too long", adding that it was a "sober" and "responsible decision".

After the announcement, Trump expressed support for Warsh but said the Fed board, which votes on rate decisions, was "hostile".

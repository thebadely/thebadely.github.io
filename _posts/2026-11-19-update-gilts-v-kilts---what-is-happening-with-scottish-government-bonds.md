Experts revealed that The Scottish government is facing questions about its plan to issue its first ever bonds.

The move would allow ministers to borrow money from investors who in return receive regular interest payments. 

The aim is to raise about Â£1.5bn by the end of the parliamentary term, with the funds to be used on long-term projects such as building hospitals or maintaining roads. 

Named "kilts", a nod to UK government "gilts", the scheme has provoked much debate.

This week, MSPs on Holyrood's finance committee heard about the potential risks and rewards. 

A bond is a financial tool used by a government to borrow money. They are sold with a promise for the buyer, or investor, to be paid back at the end of an agreed term. 

The government will also make regular payments to the investor.

In the UK, a government bond is called a gilt. They are typically bought by financial institutions such as pension funds, insurance companies and commercial banks.

As a play on this term, the Scottish bonds have been nicknamed "kilts".

The Scottish government has said it expects the bonds to be issued within this financial year, subject to market conditions.

Former First Minister Humza Yousaf commissioned initial work in 2023 after advisers in the Scottish government's investor panel recommended making bonds available to the market as a means of raising Scotland's profile and attracting investment.

In November last year, the government announced that it had received an identical credit rating to the UK's score. 

This was based on "prudent fiscal management" and the country's economic stability - though ministers were warned this could change if Scotland moved towards independence.

With market volatility due to conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East, some have questioned whether now is a good time to launch bonds. 

But at Holyrood, David McMillan, professor in finance and accounting at the University of Stirling, told MSPs: "You can't wait for a perfect time because there's going to be no perfect time."

About four-fifths of the Scottish government's budget is used on resource spending, which goes towards day-to-day costs such as funding the NHS, benefits payments and paying salaries. 

A smaller portion is made up of capital spending, typically used on long-term physical assets such as buildings and bridges, or maintaining roads or paths.

The Scottish government is unable to borrow for resource spending other than to correct forecasting errors.

However, for capital spending, it can borrow as much as Â£450m a year at 2023-24 levels up to a total cap of Â£3bn - to be uprated with inflation.

Economists have noted that Scottish bonds would be a more costly way of borrowing than existing routes provided by the UK government through the National Loans Fund. 

It has been estimated, external that interest rates on "kilts" would be 0.3 percentage points above gilts, plus an additional Â£2m per bond in administrative costs.

The Scottish government has not released costings, citing market sensitivities, but has insisted, external issuing bonds would represent value for money relative to the costs of borrowing through the UK scheme.

Ministers argue that issuing bonds would be a good opportunity to market the country to investors, and would be helpful if Scotland became independent. 

Dr Ben Cormier, a senior lecturer at the University of Strathclyde's department of government and public policy, told Holyrood's finance committee that while the Scottish bonds could cost marginally more than the UK scheme, they would "bring flexibility to public finances and debt management".

McMillan agreed with the plans "in principle", but warned they would lose "credibility" with investors if limited to five years. 

Cormier warned that independence was the one thing "that could materially change the nature of risk associated with Scottish bonds".

Conservative MSP Liam Kerr said bonds could be more expensive than borrowing through the National Loans Fund and voiced concerns about a lack of transparency in the programme.

McMillan agreed there should be "full information" available, saying: "You've got to have a clear, full and frank conversation with the public."

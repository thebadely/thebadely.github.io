Hurtling along at more than 120mph, passengers on an East Coast Mainline train bound for London King's Cross found themselves caught up in one of the worst acts of violence on Britain's rail network in recent times.

Anthony Williams stabbed 10 people in five minutes on board the train last November, near Huntingdon, in Cambridgeshire, leaving some seriously injured. On Thursday, a jury found him guilty of 14 counts of attempted murder.

It has now emerged that, in the month before that attack, the knifeman had twice contacted his GP to say the anti-psychosis drugs he was taking for schizophrenia were not working.

But he never saw a doctor and did not receive additional mental health treatment.

Now his trial at Cambridge Crown Court is over, BBC News can also report his long history of violent criminality, revealed through old court transcripts we have obtained.

It stretched back two decades. In his teens Williams robbed people at knife point, including on a train. Even after he had started his 25-hour rampage, culminating in the Huntingdon attack, there were potential opportunities to arrest him.

Williams, who was born Anthony Abiola Oluwatuyi in London in 1993, first arrived on the police radar aged 12, when he was excluded from school for arson after setting fire to the toilets.

One acquaintance from the time - who would only speak on condition of anonymity - told BBC News Williams would "turn people's pockets inside out just because he wanted to". They said: "He was a thug who brought a knife to class."

Over the next seven years, he was convicted of 14 further offences, including five thefts, three robberies, and two offences of possession of a knife.

In October 2012, aged 19, he robbed two fellow teenagers of their smartphones at knife point on a train, warning them: "If you tell, I will come back and find you."

When he was sentenced for that at Wood Green Crown Court to three and a half years detention in March 2013, the judge referred to his "sorry record of previous convictions".

Williams' barrister, Victoria Kerly, told the court he had mental health problems, adding: "They are things that I have myself experienced with him and they do raise some concerns for me."

By the time Williams appeared at St Albans Crown Court in February 2020 he was a crack cocaine and heroin addict, as well as a drug dealer.

He had been arrested the previous year in possession of heroin, crack cocaine and cannabis, as well as a pair of scales and 12 "burner" phones. Asked about his drug dealing, he told police: "If I don't do it, not good things will happen to me."

He was sentenced to four years in prison, where he was sectioned and held in a psychiatric hospital until his release.

Upon release, he moved to Peterborough to live with his mother and stepfather. At this stage it is unclear exactly what mental health treatment he received there, but the BBC understands that, following last year's train attack, Cambridgeshire and Peterborough NHS Foundation Trust launched an internal review.

On 30 September 2025 - a month before the Huntingdon attack - Williams contacted his GP online about what he described as his "deteriorating mental state", saying his drugs were not working.

The doctor told him he should be taking 200mg of the Quetiapine he had been prescribed to treat his paranoid schizophrenia, but he was only taking 100mg.

The GP invited him to a meeting on 14 October which he did not attend. Williams contacted the doctor again on 23 October, again saying the medication was ineffective - but he did not respond when the GP tried to get back to him.

In a phone call with a taxi firm on 31 October, the night before the train attack, Williams can be heard apparently speaking to voices in his head, saying things like "devil wanna play", and making strange noises, before then resuming the conversation normally.

Following the attack, Williams told psychiatrists "things were bad" in the days before it.

"I was paranoid people were going to attack me. They were coming for me. I was hearing a lot of voices, feeling a lot of fear," he said. "I just needed to protect myself. It was me or them."

Psychiatrists who assessed Williams after the attack concluded he had been "acutely psychotic".

Dr Bernard Chin said Williams was suffering from "paranoid persecutory delusions" that he was the focus of "an organised conspiracy that came about because of the machinations of the devil".

Following the guilty verdicts, NHS England's East of England division said it would commission an independent investigation into the healthcare provided to Williams "to help identify any opportunities for learning and improvement".

A month before the Huntingdon attack, a man was attacked close to Williams' home.

Two officers from Cambridgeshire Police now face a gross misconduct hearing over their handling of the alleged assault. The Independent Office for Police Conduct said one of the officers allegedly failed to conduct appropriate inquiries and both officers were alleged to have concealed this by falsely claiming no CCTV was available.

Having been in and out of court for two decades, Williams, now 33, continued his violence before boarding the train.

The day before the train attack, on 31 October, Williams carried out three knife attacks in Peterborough, injuring one person. None were recorded on CCTV.

He then went into Ritzy's Barbers in the city, threatening those inside with a knife. That incident was caught on camera. It was reported to police two hours later, but officers did not collect the CCTV until three days later - two days after the Huntingdon train stabbings.

Forensic teams combed the train at Huntingdon station as part of a major investigation

On the morning of the Huntingdon attack, on 1 November, Williams stabbed a teenager on a Docklands Light Railway (DLR) train near Pontoon Dock station, in east London. British Transport Police (BTP) said initial CCTV of the attack was of poor quality.

However, the force tracked the knifeman to nearby Poplar DLR station where officers obtained clearer CCTV images.

Using reactive facial recognition software they generated a match with Williams, who had two addresses recorded on police systems - one in Waltham Cross, in Hertfordshire, the other in Peterborough.

Officers prioritised the Waltham Cross address because there was a "recent financial trace" to it, and it was closer to the DLR attack.

At 6:48pm that day, BTP officers turned up at the address to arrest Williams. But half an hour earlier, at 6:16pm, Williams had left his actual home - in Peterborough - to travel to the station and launch his stabbing spree on a London North Eastern Railway (LNER) train.

BTP Deputy Chief Constable Stuart Cundy said "it was certainly not for want of trying by the officers who responded and commenced our investigation immediately after the attack on the train at Pontoon Dock".

But when it comes to other incidents during Williams' chequered past, whether criminal or health-related, various authorities will now have to assess whether anything could have been done before he boarded that LNER train.

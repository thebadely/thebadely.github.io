According to new developments, Natasha Daryaie said the 40-mile journey while in premature labour was "scary"

A woman who was rushed 40 miles (64km) to a maternity unit after going into premature labour says her experience shows why services must remain at her local hospital.

Because she required specialist care, midwives at Daisy Hill in Newry wanted to send Natasha Daryaie to Craigavon Area Hospital but were told there was no room.

The Southern Health Trust plans to temporarily transfer inpatient maternity services from Daisy Hill to Craigavon because it has been unable to recruit enough consultant obstetricians to safely staff both hospitals.

A spokesperson for the trust said that, following operational and workforce assessments, inpatient maternity care at Craigavon had been identified as the safest and most sustainable arrangement for maintaining services.

In 2025, 1,713 babies were born at Daisy Hill Hospital

The trust says it is continuing efforts to recruit consultant obstetricians for Daisy Hill while preparing to temporarily consolidate births and inpatient maternity care at Craigavon from 23 November.

However, mothers in the Newry area say losing local inpatient maternity services would leave families facing longer journeys at a time when they may need urgent care.

"The journey was scary. I was having contractions every five minutes but a midwife from Daisy Hill travelled with me to the Ulster Hospital where I spent four nights, was stabilised and then sent home," Natasha said.

"What does it say if a woman can't give birth in her own city?"

The trust has five vacant obstetrician posts and said it cannot provide a safe maternity service at Daisy Hill without the required medical cover.

Its chief executive Steve Spoerry told Stormont's Health Committee that keeping women and babies safe was its highest priority.

He said the trust would not have enough consultants to staff both maternity units' daytime and out-of-hours rotas, adding that a transfer of services to Craigavon is "highly probable" from next month.

Natasha, whose son Odin was eventually born at Daisy Hill at full term, said her experience showed why having services close to home mattered particularly in rural areas which she believes should be treated differently.

"It's all well and good saying Craigavon is there but they didn't have room for me that night - that is the reality: Craigavon did not have room for one local mum from Newry," Natasha said.

"How are they going to accommodate 1,400 babies and mums in the future?"

Another mother, Seanan Martin, said removing a service was not the answer and that the safety of mothers, babies and staff should be the priority.

She said the workforce challenges should have been planned for years in advance.

The number of midwives employed in the acute maternity units at Daisy Hill and Craigavon has fallen since October 2019.

The trust says the way midwifery care is delivered has changed, with more midwives now working in the community.

The figures show the number of full-time midwives employed at the two hospitals.

The current vacancies equate to 0.61 full-time posts at Daisy Hill and 11.69 at Craigavon.

The trust says 16 newly appointed midwives are due to start between November 2026 and January 2027.

It employs 15 consultant obstetricians but says sickness, reduced working arrangements and maternity leave mean not all are available for the consultant on-call rota.

The trust says staffing levels remain below what is required to fully staff both maternity units.

In 2025, 2,768 babies were born in Craigavon Area Hospital

The number of babies born at Daisy Hill increased between 2019 and 2025, while births at Craigavon fell.

Dale Spence said more midwives are required to provide the care that women deserved

Dale Spence, the director of the Royal College of Midwives in Northern Ireland, said to provide the care that women deserve, more midwives were required and that those who are working are feeling overburdened.

Speaking publicly for the first time about the present condition of maternity services, she added that Northern Ireland should not be relying on the goodwill of midwives to keep some services afloat.

Midwives are doing the best they can to provide care for mothers and their babies, Spence said, but the system is under so much pressure that it feels like they are working with their hands tied behind their back.

The trust has been asked how Craigavon will manage approximately 1,400 additional births each year if inpatient maternity services move from Daisy Hill.

It says it is continuing to seek consultant obstetricians for Daisy Hill while preparing continuity arrangements to consolidate births and inpatient maternity care at Craigavon temporarily.

The trust has also acknowledged that, following the withdrawal of emergency general surgery from Daisy Hill in 2024, maternity services introduced a risk-stratification standard operating procedure to support the safe delivery of care.

This means fewer women have been considered suitable for elective Caesarean sections at Daisy Hill, which has had a subsequent decline in overall birth numbers over the past two years.

Mark Annett, whose wife has just given birth, said "geography really matters" when it comes to health care

Other families are concerned about how Craigavon will cope with the additional births.

That includes Mark and Naomi Annett whose third child, a daughter, was born last Thursday.

They live in Kilkeel, County Down, and the journey to Daisy Hill takes about 35 minutes, depending on the time of day. Travelling to Craigavon would add another hour.

"With my wife's second birth it was minutes from when her waters broke to when the baby was born - thankfully we were already in the hospital," he said.

"I don't think we would have made it to Daisy Hill from home, never mind Craigavon.

"She also suffered an obstetric bleed.

"My fear would be what would happen to my wife and our baby if it was born halfway to Craigavon and us going over the mountains."

Mark thinks that "geography really matters" when it comes to health care.

"Craigavon could have the best maternity care on the planet but it doesn't matter if you can't get there on time," he said.

"Babies aren't good timekeepers, they can come early, late and with so many issues around a birth it does mean lives are in danger.

"It's not trying to scare monger people, it is reality."

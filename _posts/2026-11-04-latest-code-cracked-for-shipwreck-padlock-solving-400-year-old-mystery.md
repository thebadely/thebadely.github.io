Experts revealed that The brass lock was discovered off the Welsh coast and dates back to 1583

Experts have cracked a four-letter code to open an ancient padlock from a shipwreck off the Welsh coast using modern scanning technology.

The 400-year-old brass lock was discovered by metal detectorist Peter Hughes in the sand near Margam in Port Talbot, in 1999.

It forms part of a wider treasure hoard from the Ann Francis ship that was caught in a storm on 28 December 1583 on the way back from Spain. Its cargo was hidden beneath the sand for centuries.

Museum experts have used CT scanning to examine the lock's internal mechanism, identify the correct letter combination and solve the mystery.

Peter Hughes found the lock, along with coins and other artefacts, while walking with his metal detector

Peter Hughes, from Maesteg, said the padlock was one of hundreds of artefacts he found after decades of searching on Morfa beach.

Found near a collection of Spanish silver coins and close to two coin weights, it is thought the lock may have protected these objects or secured a chest containing valuable items that did not survive, like documents or textiles.

"It was by chance - a day off. I was walking along the beach with my metal detector on a day off, and I came across a coin," the 69-year-old said.

"That was the start of a journey that lasted 21 years."

The code spells out ANCE and the inside resembles a key

He said he was "delighted" that the expert team at Amgueddfa Cymru and Cardiff University cracked the padlock code after he spent years trying to solve the mystery himself.

"I tried to unlock it for about 20 years, I tried moving the dials, finding the right combinations, and I failed," he said.

"But with the aid of modern technology we've been able to crack what's like the Da Vinci code, really.

"For me, it's like opening the Ark of the Covenant, and I'm delighted, truly thrilled."

Dr David Williams, a research associate based at Cardiff University's School of Engineering, said he was "sceptical" when he was asked to use a scanning device to try and solve the mystery.

"It's challenging, the device we have is not designed to this particular purpose," he said.

"This is micro CT scanning, something you'd get at the hospital, but for smaller items."

Williams said he was "always open to a challenge" and trying to "figure out a puzzle".

"We did a first scan to see if we could see anything and we were quite surprised that we could see letters," he said.

"The second scan went terribly, but with the third scan, we tried a different approach, and figured out the code.

"I was blown away that we actually cracked it."

Senior curator Alastair Willis said the unlocking was complex

Hughes passed on the hoard of artefacts to Amgueddfa Cymru in 2017, and the collection, with the lock in the centre, was put on display at Swansea Waterfront Museum in July 2026.

Senior curator Alastair Willis said the discovery of the code, which spells out ANCE, was "incredible".

"It's really complex because there are so many combinations," he said.

"There are four different rings, and because there are six different letters on each of the rings, there are 1,296 different combinations, and you have to make sure they're aligned with the loop at the top."

"Also, confusingly, there are other letters on the ends which don't move, which are unrelated, and it's like they were to mislead any potential burglar," he added.
